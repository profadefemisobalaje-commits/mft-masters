# MFT 718 — Intimacy and Relationship Dynamics

> **MSc Marriage and Family Therapy** · Semester Three · 1.71 Units · 15 teaching weeks · Prerequisite: MFT 710

**Course leader's summary.** Intimacy is not a single thing, and sex is not the whole of it. This course
integrates attachment, desire, communication, culture and the body — and gives you language for the
conversations most couples cannot start.

---

## How to use this book

The material is intimate by design. You will be asked to examine your own beliefs about sex, bodies, gender and
obligation, because those beliefs will shape what you notice and what you avoid. Supervision is essential for
this course; several exercises should be processed there.

---

## 1. Course description

The course covers the psychology and physiology of intimacy and desire, the major sexual concerns presenting in
couple therapy, the relational dynamics of pursuit and distance, betrayal and repair, the effects of culture
and religion, and the practical skills of talking about sex.

It is explicitly **not** a sex therapy certification. It is the level of competence a family therapist needs to
assess sexual concerns confidently, intervene relationally, and refer appropriately.

## Week 1 — What intimacy is

![Figure 1.1 — Eight domains of couple intimacy](../assets/figures/fig36-intimacy-domains.svg)

Couples rarely complain about "intimacy". They complain about sex, or loneliness, or a partner who is always on
the phone. Your task is to locate the complaint in the wider system of intimacy domains: emotional, physical,
sexual, intellectual, spiritual, social, recreational, financial.

**The common pattern.** A couple presents with a sexual problem, and assessment reveals that the emotional
domain has been starved for years. Treating the sexual symptom alone usually fails; rebuilding the other
domains usually helps the sexual one.

**Assessment question.** "On a scale of 0 to 10, how satisfied are you with each of these eight areas?" Plot
both partners' answers. The gap between them, and the gap between the couple's *stated* problem and their
lowest domain, is the clinical map.

## Week 2 — Attachment and intimacy

**Secure attachment** allows both closeness and separateness: the person can ask for comfort and can tolerate
a partner's independence.

**Anxious attachment** produces hypervigilance: sex may be used to secure reassurance, and any distance feels
like rejection.

**Avoidant attachment** produces discomfort with dependency: sex may be easier than emotional closeness, and
post-coital distance is common.

**Disorganised attachment** produces approach–avoidance: desire and fear in the same moment.

**Clinical application.** "What does sex mean to each of you?" often reveals the attachment function: proof of
love, proof of adequacy, a way to avoid conversation, a way to end a conflict, a rare moment of safety.

---

## Week 3 — Desire

**Two models.**

- **Spontaneous desire**: desire appears before arousal, seemingly unbidden. More commonly reported by men in
  many samples, and by people in new relationships.
- **Responsive desire**: desire emerges *after* arousal, in response to context, closeness, relaxation or
  stimulation. More commonly reported by women, by parents of young children, and by people under stress.

**The clinical error.** Treating responsive desire as "low desire" or "dysfunction". If a person's desire is
responsive, the intervention is context — time, privacy, relaxation, non-sexual affection — not pressure to
"want it more".

**The dual control model** (Bancroft & Janssen): sexual response depends on both excitation and inhibition.
Stress, shame, performance anxiety, religious guilt and fear of pregnancy all raise inhibition. Lowering
inhibition is often more effective than increasing stimulation.

---

## Week 4 — The sexual response

**The classic model** (Masters & Johnson; Kaplan): desire → excitement → plateau → orgasm → resolution.
**The circular model** (Basson) is more accurate for many people, especially women: intimacy, closeness,
context and stimulation interact repeatedly, without a fixed sequence.

**What clinicians need to know.**

- Arousal is not desire, and lubrication is not consent or interest.
- Arousal non-concordance is normal: the body can show arousal without desire, and desire without arousal.
- Orgasm is not the goal; pleasure and connection are.
- Pain is always a medical question first (vaginismus, vulvodynia, endometriosis, infection, scarring,
  menopause).
- Medication (especially SSRIs) commonly affects desire, arousal and orgasm.
- Ageing changes speed and reliability, not the capacity for pleasure.

## Week 5 — Taking a sexual history

**Principles.** Ask permission; explain why; use the person's language; be matter-of-fact; do not react with
surprise, approval or disapproval; and take a history from each partner separately at least once.

**A workable sequence.**

1. Permission: "I ask everyone about this because it is often part of what brings couples here. Is that all
   right?"
2. Current: "How is intimacy between you at the moment? How often, and how satisfying, from 0 to 10?"
3. History: "Was there a time it was good? What was different then?"
4. Meaning: "What does sex mean to you — closeness, relief, duty, play, reassurance?"
5. Body: "Any pain, any difficulty with arousal or orgasm, any medical condition or medication?"
6. History of harm: "Has anyone ever pressured or forced you sexually?" (Ask individually.)
7. Culture and faith: "What were you taught about sex growing up? What do you believe is allowed?"
8. Expectations: "What would you like to be different — and what would your partner say you want?"

**Language.** Avoid euphemism and avoid clinical jargon. Use the words the couple uses, and offer a plain
alternative if they have none.

> **ASSESSED** — A 30-minute sexual history with a role-played client. Assessed on permission, pacing, breadth,
> non-judgemental tone, and the identification of medical or safety issues requiring referral.

## Week 6 — Talking about sex

**Why couples cannot.** Shame, fear of hurting, fear of being judged, previous rejection, cultural prohibition,
and the belief that "if you loved me you would know".

**The therapist's job** is to make the conversation normal, structured and safe. Techniques:

- **The third object.** Talk about "the sex thing" as an object between you, rather than about the people.
- **Scaling.** "How satisfied are you, 0–10?" Numbers are less exposing than adjectives.
- **Written disclosure.** Each writes three sentences about what they want more of; you read them aloud.
- **The permission sentence.** "I am going to ask something direct. You can say pass."

**Scripts to give couples.**

> "I want to talk about us, and I am nervous. Can we take twenty minutes on Saturday?"
> "I miss being close to you. I am not asking for sex tonight; I am asking whether we can talk about it this week."
> "When you turn away, I tell myself you do not want me. Is that what you mean?"

## Week 7 — Desire discrepancy

**The most common sexual complaint in couple therapy.** It is a normal difference, not a disorder, until it
produces distress.

**The pattern.** The higher-desire partner pursues; the lower-desire partner avoids; the pursuit increases
pressure; the avoidance increases; both feel rejected, and the couple stops touching altogether.

**Intervention.**

1. **Normalise.** "Desire differences are the norm, not the exception. The problem is not the difference; it is
   the cycle around it."
2. **De-pressurise touch.** Reintroduce non-sexual affection with an explicit ban on sex for a period.
3. **Negotiate.** What is acceptable to both? Frequency is a poor target; quality, initiation and affection are
   better ones.
4. **Address the context.** Sleep, children, work, resentment, body image, medication.
5. **Reject the scoreboard.** Sex is not a debt; "I did it last time" is a symptom of the problem, not a
   solution.

---

## Week 8 — Erectile and arousal difficulties

**Always rule out medical causes:** vascular disease, diabetes, hypertension, medication, hormonal factors,
alcohol and smoking, neurological conditions. Refer to a physician; do not treat as purely psychological.

**The psychological pattern.** Performance anxiety → monitoring → arousal drops → confirmation of the fear →
increased anxiety. The partner's disappointment (real or imagined) intensifies it.

**Relational intervention.** Reduce performance focus; reintroduce sensate focus; involve the partner in
non-demanding intimacy; address the couple's broader pattern; and refer for medical assessment and, where
appropriate, medication.

**Arousal difficulties in women** follow a similar logic, with a stronger contribution from context, pain,
relationship quality and hormonal change.

---

## Week 9 — Pain, orgasm and low desire

| Presentation | First question | Relational work |
|---|---|---|
| Painful intercourse | Has a medical cause been excluded? | Pace, consent, fear, communication |
| Difficulty reaching orgasm | Is this new? Is it situational? | Reduce goal focus; explore what works alone |
| Absent or low desire | Is it global or partner-specific? | Context, resentment, responsive desire model |
| Aversion | Is there a history of coercion or trauma? | Safety, trauma-informed pacing, referral |

**Referral is not failure.** Persistent pain, unexplained physical symptoms, severe trauma responses and
psychiatric complexity belong with specialists. The relational work continues alongside.

## Week 10 — Betrayal and repair

**Betrayal trauma** (developed in MFT 710). The betrayed partner's symptoms mimic PTSD: intrusion,
hypervigilance, intrusive images, and a shattered assumption that the partner is safe.

**Repair requires four things, in order.**

1. **Safety** — the behaviour has stopped; boundaries are in place; no violence.
2. **Disclosure** — enough truth to end the lying, not so much detail that it becomes a weapon. Agree what
   will be asked and answered.
3. **Remorse that is not self-pity** — "I did this, it was my choice, and I see what it cost you."
4. **Consistency over time** — transparency, reliability, and patience with the betrayed partner's cycling.

**The cycling is normal.** Recovery is not linear: the betrayed partner may be calm for two weeks and then
devastated by a song. Warn both partners, so the cycle does not become a second betrayal.

**Sex after betrayal.** Often the couple either avoids sex entirely or uses it to short-circuit the
conversation. Both are understandable and neither is repair. Rebuild slowly, with explicit consent and no
expectation.

## Week 11 — Culture, religion and sexual scripts

**Sexual scripts** are culturally learned instructions about who should want what, when, and with whom.

**Questions for the couple (each individually first).**

- What were you taught about sex before marriage? By whom?
- What did you learn about your body?
- What is permitted, and what is forbidden? What happens if you break the rule?
- Is sex a duty, a gift, a right, a sin, a reward?
- Who initiates? Who is allowed to refuse? What happens when someone refuses?

**Clinical themes in Nigerian and similar contexts.**

- Pre-marital abstinence teaching that produces guilt after marriage.
- The expectation that sex is a marital obligation, which can silence refusal.
- Female sexual pleasure as unspoken or stigmatised.
- Bride price and the sense of "ownership" it can create.
- Family planning, fertility pressure, and the stigma of childlessness.
- Religious counselling that frames desire as sin.
- Polygyny, and the sexual dynamics between co-wives.

**The clinical stance.** You are not there to adjudicate the script; you are there to make it speakable, and to
ensure that consent is real for both people.

> **CASE PLAN (40%)** — A couple with a desire discrepancy and a history of religious guilt. Includes sexual
> history, relational formulation, medical rule-outs, intervention plan, and referral criteria.

## Week 12 — Consent, coercion and obligation

**Consent is ongoing, specific and freely given.** It can be withdrawn at any point, including during sex, and
including in marriage.

**Coercion includes** pressure, sulking, threats, guilt, economic withholding, and the use of religion. It does
not have to be physical to be harmful.

**Assess individually:** "Can you say no to your partner without fear of what will happen? What does happen
when you say no?"

**When there is coercion,** the work is with the person experiencing it first: safety, validation, and
options. Couple work is contraindicated until safety is established.

## Week 13 — Lifespan and special populations

| Group | Considerations |
|---|---|
| Parents of young children | Exhaustion, touch saturation, interrupted privacy; plan rather than wait for spontaneity |
| Midlife | Hormonal change, body image, teenagers in the house, career strain |
| Later life | Assumption of asexuality by families and clinicians; health, medication, bereavement, new partners |
| Chronic illness and disability | Access, pain, fatigue, body image, and the partner's carer role |
| LGBTQ+ couples | Minority stress, disclosure, family rejection, and the absence of affirming services |
| Asexual people | Absence of sexual attraction is not pathology; negotiate intimacy on other terms |
| Post-cancer | Body change, fertility, fear, and the couple's avoidance |

**The clinician's assumption to avoid:** that everyone wants sex, that everyone should, and that a relationship
without sex is deficient. Ask what *this* couple wants.

## Week 14 — Sensate focus

**Purpose.** To reduce performance anxiety and rebuild non-demanding physical intimacy.

**Progression.**

1. **Non-genital touch, no sex.** Twenty minutes, one partner touches, the other receives; then swap. Feedback
   only on what felt pleasant, not on what was missing.
2. **Genital touch, still no intercourse.** Continue the same structure.
3. **Intercourse permitted but not required.** Often the couple is advised to continue the exercises first.
4. **Generalisation.** Gradually return to the couple's own sexual repertoire.

**Rules.** No goal, no intercourse during the first stages, no negative feedback, and a clear opt-out at any
time. Couples who "fail" at sensate focus are usually still performing.

## Glossary

**Arousal non-concordance** — mismatch between physiological arousal and subjective desire.
**Desire discrepancy** — a difference between partners in the frequency or intensity of sexual desire.
**Dual control model** — sexual response as the balance of excitation and inhibition.
**Responsive desire** — desire that emerges in response to arousal or context.
**Sensate focus** — graded, non-demanding touch exercises.
**Sexual script** — culturally learned expectations about sexual behaviour and meaning.
**Spontaneous desire** — desire experienced before arousal, without an obvious trigger.
