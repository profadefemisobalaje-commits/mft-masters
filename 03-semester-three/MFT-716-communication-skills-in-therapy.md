# MFT 716 — Communication Skills in Therapy

> **MSc Marriage and Family Therapy** · Semester Three · 1.71 Units · 15 teaching weeks · Prerequisite: MFT 706

**Course leader's summary.** Every therapeutic effect passes through communication. This course refines the
therapist's own instrument — listening, responding, questioning, silence, feedback, and the management of
difficult conversations — and teaches the protocols you will hand to families.

---

## How to use this book

Skills are built by repetition under observation. Each week has a **micro-skill** to practise, a **protocol**
to teach to families, and a **transcript** to analyse. Record yourself weekly; the gap between what you think
you said and what you said is the curriculum.

---

## 1. Course description

The course covers therapeutic communication at three levels: the **micro** (attending, reflecting,
summarising, questioning), the **meso** (structuring difficult conversations, giving feedback, managing
conflict), and the **macro** (written communication, interprofessional communication, and communication
across language and culture).

It also teaches the **structured communication protocols** used with couples and families — speaker–listener,
the Imago dialogue, negotiation formats, family meetings — and requires you to deliver them with fidelity.

## Week 1 — Communication as the medium

**Three levels of every message.**

1. **Content** — what is said.
2. **Relationship** — what it implies about the relationship ("you are the kind of person who…").
3. **Process** — what is happening between the people while it is said.

![Figure 1.1 — Five axioms of communication](../assets/figures/fig11-communication-axioms.svg)

**The therapist's communication is an intervention.** Every reflection selects something from the client's
account and returns it amplified. There is no neutral way to summarise.

**Baseline recording.** Record ten minutes of your own session (consented) or a role-play. Count: questions,
reflections, advice statements, interruptions, and seconds of silence you initiated. Most beginners are
surprised by the advice count.

---

## Week 2 — Attending and non-verbal communication

**The SOLER posture** (Egan): **S**it squarely, **O**pen posture, **L**ean slightly forward, **E**ye contact
(culturally calibrated), **R**elax.

**What families read.** Pace, volume, facial expression, seating, who you look at, how you respond to
distress, and whether you check your phone. A therapist who says "take your time" while glancing at the clock
has said something else.

**Cultural calibration.** Eye contact signals respect in some cultures and challenge in others; physical
distance varies; silence may indicate deference rather than resistance. Ask, or observe what the family does
with each other.

**Working with more than two people.** Your attention is a resource that structures the family. Distribute it
deliberately; narrate your choices ("I am going to come back to you, I have not forgotten"). Do not let the
most articulate person become the narrator of the family.

> **DRILL** — Triads. The therapist must convey three different messages using only non-verbal behaviour
> (welcome, "I need to interrupt", "take your time"). The client guesses each.

## Week 3 — Listening

**Paraphrase** (content), **reflect** (feeling), **summarise** (pattern across people and time).

**Reflection levels.**

| Level | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | "You are angry about the money." | Shows attention; may stall |
| Complex | "You are angry about the money, and underneath that, frightened that you are the only one thinking about the future." | Adds meaning; deepens |
| Double-sided | "You want him to be more involved, and you also do not trust him to do it right." | Holds ambivalence |

**The 2:1 rule.** Two reflections for every question, until it becomes automatic.

**What blocks listening.** Formulating your next question; evaluating; wanting to reassure; your own emotional
reaction; diagnostic thinking that closes the account prematurely.

## Week 4 — Empathy

**Three components.**

1. **Affective empathy** — feeling something in response.
2. **Cognitive empathy** — understanding the other's perspective.
3. **Empathic communication** — conveying that understanding so the other feels understood.

**The therapist needs all three, and needs to keep the first from driving.** Affective empathy without
boundaries produces over-involvement; cognitive empathy without warmth produces technique; communication
without either produces scripts.

**Empathy is not agreement.** "I understand why you would want to leave" is empathy. "You are right to want
to leave" is agreement. The first opens exploration; the second closes it and creates a coalition.

**Empathic accuracy is testable.** Watch a recorded session and pause before each therapist response. Write
what you think the client felt. Then check with the client (or with the recording's subsequent cues).

> **DRILL** — In pairs, one person describes a minor grievance. The listener must respond with an empathic
> statement that contains no advice, no question and no story of their own. Three rounds each.

## Week 5 — Questioning and prompting

Six families of question (from MFT 706), refined for communication work:

- **Open** — "What was that like?"
- **Circular** — "Who noticed first?"
- **Clarifying** — "When you say 'disrespectful', what did he actually do?"
- **Reflexive** — "If your daughter were listening, what would she say you both do?"
- **Scaling** — "How close are you, 0–10?"
- **Confronting (gently)** — "You say it does not matter, and you have mentioned it four times."

**The prompt instead of the question.** "Tell me more about that" often produces more than any question. Use
prompts when the client is in flow; questions when you need to change direction.

---

## Week 6 — Content, relationship, process

**Transcript analysis exercise.** Take fifty lines of a session transcript and mark:

- every **content** statement (topic, fact)
- every **relationship-level** message ("you always…", "you are the kind of…")
- every **process** moment (something audible happening between speakers: interruption, softening, joke,
  silence)

Then ask: what did the therapist respond to? Beginners respond to content and miss process. Advanced
practitioners respond to process and use content as evidence.

## Week 7 — Feedback

**Feedback to clients** should be behavioural, specific, timely and owned: "I noticed your voice dropped when
you said that" (not "you are passive-aggressive").

**Feedback between family members** is often the missing skill. Teach the four-part structure:

1. "When you ___" (behaviour, no adjectives)
2. "I feel ___" (feeling)
3. "Because ___" (meaning)
4. "What I would like is ___" (specific request)

**Feedback to colleagues and supervisees**: use the same discipline. "What I saw / what it did to me / what to
keep / what to try" — never the sandwich, which trains people to hear praise as a warning.

---

## Week 8 — Observed micro-skills session

Thirty minutes. Assessed on: attending; reflection ratio; complex reflections; question variety; one
deliberate silence; one summary; and a clean close.

---

## Week 9 — Conflict communication and repair

**The escalation sequence:** complaint → criticism → contempt → defensiveness → stonewalling.

![Figure 9.1 — The four horsemen and their antidotes](../assets/figures/fig14-four-horsemen.svg)

**Repair is the skill that matters.** Teach couples to use a repair phrase: "Can we start again?", "I am
flooded, give me twenty minutes", "That came out wrong."

**The therapist's repair.** You will make mistakes: mis-summarise, miss a disclosure, take a side. Repair in
the moment: "I got that wrong. Let me try again." Modelling repair is more powerful than describing it.

---

## Week 10 — Protocols

**Speaker–listener.** A floor card, a timer, paraphrase before responding, then swap. Teach it, rehearse it
in session, and set it as homework with one agreed topic.

**Imago dialogue.** Three steps: mirror ("so what I heard you say is…"), validate ("that makes sense
because…"), empathise ("I imagine you feel…"). More demanding than speaker–listener, and more powerful with
practised couples.

**Negotiation format.** Define one issue; each states a position; generate three options; choose one to trial
for two weeks; review.

**Fidelity matters.** A protocol delivered loosely is a protocol that fails, and the couple will conclude that
"communication exercises do not work". Practise until you can teach it in ten minutes without notes.

> **ASSESSED** — Teach speaker–listener to a role-played couple in twelve minutes, then coach them through one
> round. Assessed on clarity, demonstration, coaching, and management of a partner who will not follow the
> rules.

## Week 12 — Difficult conversations

**Four types and their structures.**

1. **Bad news** (a diagnosis, a death, a safeguarding outcome). Structure: warning shot ("I have some
   difficult news") → information in small chunks → check understanding → silence → emotional response →
   plan → follow-up. Do not fill the silence after the news.
2. **Disclosure** (a secret that must be shared, an affair, a risk). Structure: prepare the person → agree the
   content → be present → manage the response → plan.
3. **Confrontation** (a boundary violation, a safety concern, a colleague's conduct). Structure: describe the
   behaviour → its impact → the expectation → the consequence → the support available.
4. **Ending therapy when it is not working** (referral, rupture, incompetence). Structure: name the
   difficulty → own your part → offer a route → document.

**The common failure** is rushing to reassurance. "At least…" and "it will be fine" terminate the conversation
the client needs to have.

---

## Week 13 — Adaptation

**Children.** Short sentences, concrete language, check understanding by asking them to explain it back, and
use a medium.

**Interpreters.** Speak to the family, first-person interpretation, brief and debrief (see MFT 708).

**Low literacy.** Avoid written-only instructions; use drawings, demonstration, and teach-back ("show me how
you would do it").

**Hearing or cognitive impairment.** Reduce background noise, face the person, one idea per sentence, and
confirm comprehension.

**Second-language sessions.** Slow down, avoid idiom, check meaning ("when you say 'respect', what does that
include?").

---

## Week 14 — Difficult conversation assessment

Twenty-minute simulation drawn from a bank (bad news, disclosure, confrontation, ending). Assessed on:
structure, pacing, emotional attunement, clarity of the message, and a documented follow-up.

---

## Week 15 — Written and interprofessional communication

**Clinical notes.** Behavioural, dated, factual, and signed. Avoid adjectives about people; avoid speculation.

**Referral letters.** One page: reason, relevant history, what you have tried, what you are asking for, and
who to contact.

**Reports for courts and schools.** Answer the question asked; state the limits of your expertise; separate
fact from opinion.

**Interprofessional communication.** Use SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation). It is
short, it survives interruption, and it is what other professions are trained to receive.

---

## Glossary

**Complex reflection** — a reflection that adds meaning or feeling beyond the client's words.
**Double-sided reflection** — a reflection that holds both sides of an ambivalence.
**Empathic accuracy** — the degree to which a therapist correctly infers a client's experience.
**Mirroring** — repeating back the other's words as the first step of a structured dialogue.
**Process** — the relational layer of communication, distinct from content.
**Repair** — an attempt to de-escalate and reconnect after a rupture.
**SBAR** — Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation: a handover format.
**SOLER** — a posture mnemonic for attending behaviour.
