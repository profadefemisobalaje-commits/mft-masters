# MFT 704 — Ethical and Legal Issues in Therapy

> **MSc Marriage and Family Therapy** · Semester One · 1.71 Units · 14 teaching weeks · Prerequisite: none

**Course leader's summary.** This course trains judgement, not just compliance. You will learn the codes,
the law, and — more importantly — how to reason when the codes and the law point in different directions,
when the family's culture conflicts with the institution's policy, and when your own values are in the room.

---

## How to use this book

Every week presents a **standard**, a **case**, and a **decision**. Ethics is not memorised; it is
practised. The weekly incidents in this book are drawn from the ordinary texture of clinical life — the
secret, the subpoena, the gift, the neighbour, the unpaid fee — because those are the situations that
actually end careers.

---

## 1. Course description

Family therapy multiplies ethical complexity. In individual work there are two parties (you and the
client). In family work there may be six, with conflicting interests, unequal power, and secrets that
each person believes belongs to them. Add a school, a child protection agency, a court, an insurance
company, and a faith community, and the question "who is my client?" becomes the central professional
question of the course.

The course combines **normative ethics** (codes, duties, principles), **law** (the parts a clinician must
know and the parts a clinician must refer), and **professional formation** (the kind of clinician you are
becoming).

## Week 1 — Moral philosophy for clinicians

You do not need to become a philosopher, but you need to know which argument you are making when you say
"that would be wrong". Most ethical failure in clinical practice is not the product of a bad person; it is
the product of a good person using one tradition of reasoning while believing they are using another, and
therefore being unable to see the objection that the other tradition would have raised.

| Tradition | Central question | Therapy example |
|---|---|---|
| Deontology (duty) | What are my duties regardless of outcome? | Keeping confidentiality even when disclosure would be convenient |
| Consequentialism | What produces the best overall outcome? | Breaking confidentiality to prevent a serious harm |
| Virtue ethics | What would a person of good character do? | Refusing a gift that would create obligation |
| Ethics of care | What does this relationship require of me? | Attending to the family's dependence on you |
| Justice / rights | Are power and resources fairly distributed? | Whose voice is missing from this room? |

**The four principles** (Beauchamp & Childress) remain the most portable framework: **autonomy,
beneficence, non-maleficence, justice**. In family work they collide constantly — respect for the father's
autonomy may conflict with the mother's safety, and a clinician who has not learned to name the collision
will resolve it silently, in favour of whoever is more articulate in the room.

**Why a family therapist needs philosophy more than an individual therapist does.** In individual work the
ethical questions are mostly about the boundary between two people: the client and the clinician. In
family work the questions are about *distribution*: whose interests the clinician is serving, whose secret
they are holding, whose account they believe, and whose voice the institution will hear. Those are
questions of justice and care before they are questions of compliance, and the codes — which are written
mostly for two-party work — do not answer them.

> **CASE** — A father telephones the clinic to ask what his wife said in her individual session. He is
> polite, he is paying the fee, and he is, in his own understanding, being a responsible head of household.
> The clinician has three duties in tension: confidentiality to the wife, honesty to the husband, and the
> welfare of a couple whose therapy depends on both of them returning. Which tradition she reaches for
> decides what she says in the next thirty seconds.

**The teaching decision that makes the week work.** The lecture begins with the telephone call, before any
theory, and every student writes the sentence they would say. The sentences are read aloud and sorted onto
the board by tradition — and the class discovers that most of them are consequentialist ("it will damage the
therapy if I tell him"), a few are deontological ("I cannot discuss her sessions"), and almost none are
about care or justice. The sorting is the lesson: their instincts are already philosophical, and they have
never inspected them.

**The sentence the lecture must land.** "You already have a moral theory. This week is about finding out
which one, and whether you would defend it if a family asked you to."

### 1.2 Five traditions, and what each one cannot see

**1. Deontology: duty regardless of outcome.** The tradition that produced the codes. Its strength is
predictability: a rule that holds when it is inconvenient is a rule that can be relied upon by a family who
has never met you. Its characteristic failure is rigidity — the clinician who keeps a confidence that is
killing someone because the rule says so, and who experiences the resulting harm as bad luck rather than as
a decision. In family work, deontology's specific blindness is to *distribution*: a rule about confidentiality
applied equally to a powerful father and a dependent grandmother produces an unequal result, because they
have unequal capacity to use the information.

**2. Consequentialism: the best overall outcome.** Its strength is realism — it takes harm seriously and it
permits the clinician to weigh a small breach against a large protection. Its characteristic failure is
that nobody can calculate the consequences, and the calculation is usually done by the person who will
benefit from the answer. In family work, its blindness is to the *unrepresented*: the person whose interests
do not appear in the therapist's forecast is the person who gets sacrificed, and that person is usually the
one with least power in the room.

**3. Virtue ethics: what a person of good character would do.** Its strength is formation — it asks what kind
of clinician you are becoming, which is the question that survives when the code is silent. Its
characteristic failure is vagueness and self-congratulation: "I would know" is not a method, and the
practitioner who trusts their own character is the one least likely to notice its defects. In family work,
virtue ethics supplies the questions the codes cannot answer — courage, patience, humility, temperance about
one's own influence — and it needs a discipline to keep it honest, which is supervision.

**4. Ethics of care: what this relationship requires of me.** The tradition that family therapy needs most
and uses least. Its central claims: that moral life is constituted by relationships rather than by isolated
choosers; that responsiveness matters as much as rules; and that dependence is not a defect to be overcome
but a condition of being human, which every client in your room is in. Its strength is that it describes
family work accurately. Its characteristic failure is that it can justify almost anything in the name of the
relationship, and it has no natural defence against a clinician who is more attached to a family than is good
for the family.

**5. Justice and rights: are power and resources fairly distributed?** The tradition that asks who is not in
the room and who is paying for the room. Its strength is that it names the questions the other four avoid:
who has access to this service, whose language is spoken, whose account the institution will believe, and
who carries the cost of the arrangement. Its characteristic failure is abstraction — a clinician can conduct
a fine analysis of structural injustice and still be unjust to the person in front of them.

**The five are not a menu.** They are five different questions, and a competent clinician asks all five about
a hard case and then has to decide, in the open, which one governs. The decision is the practice; the
inspection of the decision is the profession.

### 1.2a The four principles, and how they collide in family work

The four principles are the most portable framework in the field, and the reason is that they are not a
theory: they are four considerations that any defensible decision must have addressed. Their use is not to
produce the answer but to make sure the answer has not ignored something.

**Autonomy.** The client's right to decide what happens to them, grounded in their capacity to do so. In
individual work it is the dominant principle and it is generally straightforward. In family work it is
immediately difficult, for three reasons. The first is *multiplicity*: there are several autonomous people
in the room, and their autonomies conflict — the adolescent's autonomy over her own body against her
parents' legal authority; the wife's autonomy over her own disclosure against the husband's claim to know.
The second is *capacity*: a nine-year-old, an eighty-one-year-old with dementia, and a woman in an abusive
relationship all have autonomy claims that are not identical to an adult's unconstrained choice, and
"respecting autonomy" without distinguishing them is a way of abandoning the vulnerable. The third is
*context*: in many of the households this book is written for, decisions are made by families rather than by
individuals, and a clinician who insists on individual consent is not being respectful; they are being
parochial.

**Beneficence.** The duty to act in the client's interest. It sounds unarguable and it is the principle most
often used to justify paternalism, because "the client's interest" is defined by the clinician. In family
work the principle has a specific structural problem: whose interest? A decision that benefits the marriage
may harm the wife; a decision that protects the child may end the family's engagement.

**Non-maleficence.** First, do no harm. The most useful of the four in practice, because it operates as a
check rather than a goal: before acting, ask what harm this could do, including the harms of the
intervention itself. In ethics, the harms that are most often overlooked are the ones the clinician's own
action causes: the disclosure that ends a therapy, the report that produces a violent reprisal, the note
that is read in court five years later.

**Justice.** Fairness in the distribution of benefits, risks and resources. In family work it appears in
three places that the other three principles do not reach: *access* (who can get to this clinic, at these
hours, at this fee); *voice* (whose account is taken seriously, in a room and in a record); and
*consequence* (who bears the cost of a decision — frequently not the person who made it).

**The collision, worked.** A seventeen-year-old discloses to the therapist that she is sexually active and
does not want her parents told. The four principles give four different answers. *Autonomy*: she is nearly
an adult and her confidence should be kept. *Beneficence*: telling the parents may protect her; not telling
may leave her without support. *Non-maleficence*: telling may produce the beating she fears, which is a harm
the clinician will have caused. *Justice*: her parents have legal responsibility and a legitimate interest;
she has the greater exposure to the consequences. The principles do not resolve it. They ensure the clinician
has seen all four, and then the clinician must decide and be able to say why.

**The order of the principles is not fixed.** Students frequently assume autonomy comes first because the
codes list it first in some jurisdictions. It does not. In a case involving a child's safety, non-maleficence
governs. In a case involving an adult's refusal of treatment, autonomy governs. Deciding which principle
governs *in this case* is the act of judgement the course is teaching, and it cannot be reduced to a rule
without producing the very rigidity deontology is criticised for.

### 1.2b Ethics of care: the tradition family therapy should own

Family therapy is, almost alone among the mental health professions, built on a relational ontology. It is
therefore strange that its ethics are usually imported from a tradition built on isolated choosers. This
section states the alternative, because it is the one that fits the work.

**The relational claim.** The ethics of care, developed by Gilligan, Noddings, Tronto and others, holds that
moral life is constituted by relationships of dependence and response rather than by transactions between
independent agents. Three consequences follow.

**First, responsiveness is a moral skill.** The question "what does this person need from me, here, now?" is
not a softer version of "what is the rule?" It is a different and equally rigorous question, and it requires
attention — the capacity to notice what is actually happening rather than what the category predicts. A
clinician who has not developed attention will apply the right rule to the wrong situation.

**Second, dependence is normal.** Every client in your room is dependent on someone, and most are dependent
on people who are also in the room. The tradition therefore refuses the picture of the autonomous chooser as
the ethical ideal: a grandmother with dementia, a nine-year-old, a woman who cannot leave — none of them is
a defective version of an independent adult. Each has claims that arise from their relationships.

**Third, power is the central ethical fact.** Care is not symmetrical. The person giving care has power, and
the ethics of care requires that the power be used responsively rather than according to the carer's
preferences. This is why the tradition is useful in family work: the therapist's power is enormous and
largely invisible, and a principles-only analysis gives almost no account of it.

**The critique, which the tradition must answer.** Care ethics can justify almost anything in the name of the
relationship. It has been used to keep women in caring roles, to excuse clinicians who will not end a
treatment, and to defend a therapist's attachment to a family that has outgrown the therapy. Noddings's own
answer is that caring must be *received* — the cared-for must experience it as care — which is a testable
condition, and it is the version this course uses. "I did it because I cared" is not an ethical justification
unless the person cared for can say that they were cared for.

**The clinical use, in three questions.** Whose need is being met by this decision — theirs or mine? Who in
this family is dependent on whom, and is the dependence being used or served? And what would the person with
least power in this room say if they were asked, in a language they had, with nobody watching?

### 1.3 The decision model: seven steps

The course uses one model all term, so that the incident analyses and the examination are comparable. It is
deliberately pedestrian. Its value is not that it produces the right answer but that it makes the reasoning
visible, which is what supervision, a court, and a professional body will all ask for.

**Step 1 — State the ethical question, not the practical one.** "What should I do about the husband's
telephone call?" is practical. "Whose confidence am I holding, and to whom do I owe what?" is ethical. Most
hard cases are hard because the ethical question has never been stated.

**Step 2 — Establish the facts.** What is actually known, by whom, and how. This step takes longer than
students expect and prevents most errors: a great many ethical dilemmas dissolve when the facts are
established (the husband already knew; the wife had said she would tell him; the child is not the father's).

**Step 3 — Identify the parties and their interests.** Every person affected, including the absent ones and
the institution. Name the interests, not the positions. Then ask which party has least power to protect
their own interest.

**Step 4 — Apply the frameworks.** All five traditions and all four principles, briefly, and record where
they disagree. The disagreement is the case's real content.

**Step 5 — Consult.** The code, the law, the agency policy, a supervisor, and — where appropriate and
possible — the client. Consultation is not a step for difficult cases; it is a step for any case in which
the clinician notices they have a preference.

**Step 6 — Decide, act, and record the reasoning.** The decision, the reasons, the alternatives rejected and
why. In a record, because the record is the only evidence that a decision was made rather than a drift.

**Step 7 — Review.** What happened; whether the prediction held; what would be done differently. The step
that nobody does, and the only one that produces learning.

### 1.3a Moral distress, moral injury, and the difference that matters

Two terms that are used loosely in the literature and that name different things. A clinician who cannot
tell them apart will misdiagnose their own state, and will look for the wrong remedy.

**Moral distress** is what happens when you know what the right action is and you are prevented from doing
it. Its classic form is institutional: the clinician who knows a child should be referred and cannot get an
appointment; who knows a family needs an interpreter and is not given one; who is required by an agency to
keep a number of sessions that the work does not need. The distress is not confusion about the good. It is
the experience of being unable to enact it.

The remedy for moral distress is structural, not psychological. It consists of changing the condition, or
of naming it collectively, or of leaving. What does not work — and what is usually offered — is resilience
training, which asks the clinician to feel better about an arrangement they are right to object to.

**Moral injury** is different and more serious. It names the lasting harm that follows acting against one's
own moral commitments, or witnessing such an act and being unable to prevent it. The term comes from
military psychiatry and it travels well into clinical settings, because clinicians do commit acts they
believe to be wrong under constraint: a disclosure that produces a reprisal, a report that ends a family's
engagement, a court report written in language the clinician knows to be misleading. The sequelae are not
anxiety or burnout; they are shame, a changed sense of self, and a withdrawal from the aspects of the work
that once mattered.

**The distinction, in one line.** Moral distress is "I could not do what I should". Moral injury is "I did
what I should not, or I stood by while it was done".

**What follows clinically.** For distress: audit the condition, escalate, and use supervision to keep the
judgement intact. For injury: supervision is necessary and insufficient; the remedy involves naming the act,
restoring the moral account (which sometimes means an apology to the family), and in some cases a change of
setting. A trainee who has been required to write a report they believe false needs a supervisor who will
say that it was wrong, not one who explains the agency's constraints.

**The course's position.** A significant proportion of what is called burnout in this profession is moral
distress that has been individualised. The remedy is not more self-care. It is a decision model that makes
the reasoning visible, a supervisor who will disagree, and a professional identity that can survive being in
the minority in a room.

### 1.4 The trolley, in a clinic

The trolley problem is a thought experiment about whether it is permissible to harm one person in order to
save several. It is useful here not because clinicians face trolleys but because the structure of the problem
— *may I redirect a harm toward someone who did not choose it?* — is the structure of half the hard cases in
family work.

**Variant 1 — The disclosure.** A wife tells the therapist, in an individual session, that her husband has
been physically violent twice. She does not want him told. The couple is in therapy together. The therapist
can: say nothing and continue couple work (harm: the wife is unprotected and the therapy is built on a
secret); disclose to the husband (harm: the wife loses her confidentiality and may be at greater risk); or
stop the couple work and see the wife alone (harm: the couple loses the treatment). *Redirection:* every
option pushes harm onto someone who did not choose it. The wife did not choose to lose her confidence; the
husband did not choose to be discussed; the children did not choose any of it.

**Variant 2 — The paternity.** A father asks whether the child is his. The mother knows the answer. The
therapist knows because the mother told her. Nothing the therapist does is neutral: silence is a lie by
omission to the father; disclosure is a breach to the mother; ending the therapy is a harm to the child.

**Variant 3 — The adolescent's risk.** A fifteen-year-old discloses self-harm and asks the therapist not to
tell her parents. Disclosing may end the therapeutic relationship; not disclosing may leave her alone with a
risk. *This is the variant that most resembles the trolley*, because the clinician is choosing which of two
identifiable people will bear the harm.

**Why the thought experiment is worth the time.** Because it trains three things. First, the recognition that
*someone will be harmed in every option*, which dissolves the fantasy of the harm-free decision and with it a
great deal of paralysis. Second, the discipline of naming who bears the harm, which is the justice question.
Third, the habit of asking whether the harm is being *redirected onto someone who did not choose it* — and
if it is, whether that person's consent could be obtained, which is frequently the move that converts an
impossible case into a workable one.

> **PRACTICAL** — Take three ethical dilemmas from your own life, not clinical ones. For each, identify which
> tradition you used at the time, and which you used to justify it afterwards. Then answer one question:
> were they the same? Most students find that the tradition they acted on was care or virtue, and the
> tradition they justified with was consequence. Write down what that gap suggests about how you will
> account for your decisions in a clinical record.

### 1.5 Case: the telephone call from the father

The case from the lecture, worked through the seven steps. This is the model the incident analyses follow.

**The facts.** The Okonkwos — Ada, 34, Chuka, 39, Ebuka, 9 — have been in couple therapy for six weeks.
In her third individual session, Ada disclosed that Chuka's night shifts have resumed and that she is
"managing everything alone", and that she has begun to think about taking the children to her mother's in
Lagos. She has not told Chuka. She asked the therapist not to tell him. On Thursday, Chuka telephones: "I
want to know what she said on Tuesday."

**Step 1 — The ethical question.** Not "what do I say to Chuka?" but: *I am holding a confidence from one
member of a couple I am treating jointly; to whom do I owe what, and how do I honour the couple's treatment
without deceiving either of them?*

**Step 2 — The facts.** What does Chuka actually know? (He knows the sessions exist; he does not know what
was said. He has noticed she is distant.) What has Ada been told about the confidentiality arrangement? (At
the start of therapy the therapist said, in front of both, that individual disclosures would be handled by
agreement — a sentence Ada may or may not have understood as a promise.) Is there a risk to anyone? (No
violence; no risk to the children; a risk of family separation if the decision is taken unilaterally.) What
is the therapist's contractual position? (The clinic's policy: no information from an individual session is
disclosed without the individual's agreement, except in the specified exceptions.)

**Step 3 — The parties and their interests.** Ada: to have a place where she can think without consequences,
and to be supported in a decision she has not made. Chuka: to know what is happening in his marriage, and
not to be humiliated by a therapist who knows more than he does. The children: continuity and the marriage's
stability. The therapist: to keep the treatment viable and to remain honest. The clinic: its policy. *Least
power:* Ada, because she has already been described by her husband to the therapist as "difficult", and
because she is the one whose confidence is at stake.

**Step 4 — The frameworks.** *Deontology:* the confidence holds; the clinic's rule is explicit. *Consequence:*
refusing Chuka may end his engagement with the therapy, and his engagement is what has kept the family in the
room. *Virtue:* what would a clinician of good character do — and would she be able to say it to both of them
in the same room? *Care:* Ada needs a place to think; Chuka needs not to be excluded; the couple needs an
arrangement that holds. *Justice:* the person with least power must not be the one who bears the cost of the
therapist's convenience.

**Step 5 — Consultation.** The clinic's written policy; a supervisor, by telephone the same day; and Ada
herself, before anything is said to Chuka.

**Step 6 — The decision.** The therapist does not disclose content. She says to Chuka: "I cannot tell you what
your wife said, and I want to be straight with you about why: if either of you cannot use an individual
session to think out loud, this therapy will not work. What I can do is ask her whether she is willing to
talk to you about the thing that is on her mind, with me in the room." She then telephones Ada, tells her
exactly what was said and what was not, and asks whether she will raise it in the joint session. Ada says
she will, on one condition: that the therapist opens the session, so that she is not the one who starts it.

**Step 7 — The review.** At the next joint session, Ada raises the question of Lagos. Chuka is angry and
then frightened. The disclosure does not end the therapy; the *manner* of it — preceded by a week in which
Ada had chosen to make it — is what keeps the therapy alive.

**What made the difference.** Two things, and neither is a principle. The first is that the therapist
consulted *before* answering, which is what converted a trap into a process. The second is that she told Ada
exactly what she had said to Chuka, which is the practice — not the rule — that keeps a multiperson therapy
honest. Neither is in any code. Both are what an experienced clinician does.

### 1.5a The four questions that stop a decision going wrong

Before the seven steps, four questions that an experienced clinician asks almost automatically, and that a
trainee can be taught to ask deliberately. They take ninety seconds and they catch the majority of errors.

**Question 1 — Who is not in this conversation?** The absent person is usually the one who will bear the
cost. A decision about a child made without the child; a decision about a marriage made without the husband;
a decision about a placement made without the person who will be moved. Naming the absent party does not
always mean convening them; it means accounting for them in the reasoning.

**Question 2 — Whose interest does my preferred option serve?** Asked honestly, this question is
uncomfortable, which is why it is asked *before* the decision rather than after. The clinician who wants to
disclose a secret is frequently protecting themselves from the discomfort of holding it. The clinician who
wants to keep it is frequently protecting the therapy they have built.

**Question 3 — What would I do if the person with least power were the one I had to explain this to?** The
test is a version of the publicity test: a decision that cannot be explained to the person most affected by
it is a decision that needs re-examining. The explanation does not have to be acceptable to them; it has to
be *possible*.

**Question 4 — What would I write in the record?** If the sentence that would have to go in the file reads
badly, the decision is bad — not because records are read, but because writing forces the reasoning into
plain language, and plain language is unforgiving of self-deception. This is the cheapest ethical instrument
in the book and the one most likely to change a decision in the thirty seconds before it is taken.

### 1.6 Transcript: the call, annotated

Eleven turns, reconstructed from a supervision recording. The therapist is a trainee, four months into
placement. The annotation names the tradition governing each move, which is the discipline the week is
teaching.

> **T1 Chuka:** I want to know what she said on Tuesday. I am her husband. I am paying for this.
> — *Two claims, both true in his frame: a status claim and a contract claim. Neither is answered by
> silence, which he will read as confirmation that something is being hidden.*
>
> **T2 Therapist:** You are her husband and you are paying, and both of those are reasons I should be
> straight with you rather than avoid you. — *The move: concede the true parts before refusing anything.
> Deontological in form, care in tone.*
>
> **T3 Chuka:** Then tell me.
>
> **T4 Therapist:** I am not going to tell you what she said. I want to say why, and I want you to hear that
> it is not because she has said something terrible about you. — *The disclosure of the rule, and the
> pre-emptive denial of the catastrophic reading. Without the second sentence, the first produces exactly
> the fantasy the therapist is trying to prevent.*
>
> **T5 Chuka:** Then she has said something.
>
> **T6 Therapist:** She has said something she is not ready to say to you, and I have said I will not say it
> for her. If I break that, neither of you can use a session to think. You would be sitting in that chair
> wondering what I am writing down. — *The justification is consequentialist, and it is chosen deliberately:
> the deontological version ("it is the rule") is true and it lands as bureaucracy. The consequentialist
> version gives him a reason he can use.*
>
> **T7 Chuka:** *(long pause)* So what do I do?
> — *The question that tells the therapist the refusal has been accepted. Note that it took four turns, not
> one, and that a therapist who stopped talking after T4 would not have got here.*
>
> **T8 Therapist:** Two things. I will ask her whether she is willing to bring it into the room with both of
> you, and I will tell you what she says either way. And in the meantime, I want to know what you have
> noticed at home, because you have noticed something or you would not be telephoning me. — *The offer of a
> next step, and the redirection of his energy from a demand for information to an account of his own
> observation. This is the turn that keeps him in the therapy.*
>
> **T9 Chuka:** She is packing. I saw a bag.
> — *The fact the therapist did not have. The husband is not asking for gossip; he is frightened, and the
> therapist's rule has, until this moment, kept her from knowing why.*
>
> **T10 Therapist:** *(pause)* Thank you. That is important, and I would not have known it if you had not
> telephoned. — *The acknowledgement is accurate, and it is also the repair: his call has produced something
> of value rather than only a refusal.*
>
> **T11 Chuka:** What do I do about the bag?
>
> **T12 Therapist:** Nothing yet, and not nothing forever. Give me until tomorrow to speak to her, and I will
> telephone you. If she will not talk to you by Friday, I will tell you that too, and we will decide then
> what the three of us do. — *A time limit, a commitment, and a fallback. The deontological refusal has been
> turned into a process with dates, which is what makes it survivable for a frightened man.*

**What the transcript shows that the model does not.** The seven-step model produces the decision; the
transcript produces the *sequence*, and the sequence is where the skill lives. Three things in particular.
First, the therapist never says "I understand your concern" — she demonstrates it by conceding the true
parts. Second, she offers a *reason* rather than a *rule*, and the reason is chosen for the listener. Third,
she ends with a date, which converts a refusal into a relationship.

### 1.6a Bad arguments, named

Students lose marks, and clinicians lose cases, by using one of six arguments that sound ethical and are not.
Naming them makes them visible.

**"It is my duty."** Sometimes true. More often a rule is being used to end a conversation the clinician does
not want to have. The test: can you state the duty, its source, and the harm it is preventing?

**"It is for their own good."** The paternalist's sentence. It may be correct; it is never sufficient. The
test: who decided what their good is, and were they asked?

**"Everyone does it."** A statement about a norm, not about an obligation. The test: is the practice defensible
or merely common?

**"It will damage the therapy."** Frequently true and frequently the clinician's own convenience in
therapeutic clothing. The test: whose therapy, and what is the damage measured against?

**"I had no choice."** Almost never accurate. There is usually a choice with a cost, and the honest statement
names the cost rather than denying the choice.

**"I was following the policy."** The defence that fails at every professional tribunal, because the
professional's obligation is to exercise judgement about the policy, not to be its instrument.

**The exercise.** In the laboratory, students are given six anonymised sentences from real disciplinary
findings and asked which of the six arguments each one is. The recognition is fast, and it transfers: once a
clinician can hear the shape of the bad argument, they can hear it in their own mouth.

### 1.7 Laboratory: the ethics incident analysis

**Aim.** To produce a 600-word incident analysis that a supervisor, a professional body, or a court could read
and follow. This is the format of the four assessed pieces of coursework.

**Procedure.** Students bring a real or witnessed incident (anonymised), or use one of the four briefs the
trainer supplies. They write the analysis in the session, in fifty minutes, against the seven steps, and then
exchange with a partner who marks it against the rubric. The last twenty minutes is the plenary, in which two
analyses are read aloud and the class identifies which step each one skipped.

**Marking rubric (20 marks).**

| Criterion | 0–1 | 2–3 | 4–5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The question** | A practical question | Ethical but vague | Ethical, specific, and the practical question is explicitly set aside |
| **The facts** | Assumed | Listed, but no account of what is unknown | Established, with what is unknown named and how it would be established |
| **The parties** | Client and therapist | All present parties | All parties including absent ones and the institution, with the least powerful identified |
| **The frameworks** | One tradition, unnamed | Two traditions, named | Four or five, applied briefly, with the disagreement between them stated |
| **The decision** | A preference | A decision with reasons | A decision, the rejected alternatives, and the review |

**The distinction that carries most of the marks.** The top band requires the student to state *where the
frameworks disagree*. A paper that applies five frameworks and finds they all point the same way has either
misapplied them or chosen a case that is not an ethical dilemma; and a paper that applies one framework and
calls it the answer has written an opinion rather than an analysis.

**The four briefs.**

*Brief A.* A couple's sixteen-year-old daughter asks to see you individually, and says she does not want her
parents to know. The parents are your clients and are paying.

*Brief B.* A colleague tells you, at lunch, that a family you both know is "probably abusive" and that he has
not reported it because "there is no evidence".

*Brief C.* A family offers you a substantial gift at the end of therapy. You know the family cannot easily
afford it.

*Brief D.* You are asked by a court to produce a report on custody, and you have been treating the mother for
a year. The fee is three times your usual rate.

### 1.8 Six errors

**Error 1 — Confusing the rule with the reason.** "Because the code says so" is a true statement and, said
to a frightened husband, it is heard as evasion. The code is the floor of the reasoning, not its content; a
clinician who cannot give a reason a family can understand has not yet understood the rule.

**Error 2 — Applying individual ethics to a multiperson system.** Almost every professional code is written
for two parties. Applied unchanged to a couple or a family, it produces a therapist who is scrupulously
confidential with one member and quietly deceptive to the other, and who believes they have been ethical
because they have followed the rule.

**Error 3 — Calculating consequences in your own favour.** Consequentialist reasoning is the most useful and
the most corruptible. The test is whether the calculation was made before or after the therapist noticed
what they wanted to do.

**Error 4 — Virtue ethics as a substitute for method.** "I would know" is not a decision procedure, and the
clinician who most trusts their own character is least likely to notice its defects. Virtue needs
supervision the way a knife needs a stone.

**Error 5 — Care as an alibi.** "I did it because I cared" has justified more boundary violations than
cynicism ever has. The test is Noddings's: was it *received* as care by the person cared for?

**Error 6 — Treating moral distress as a personal failing.** The clinician who cannot get a child an
appointment, and who concludes that they are not resilient, has misdiagnosed a structural problem as a
character flaw. The remedy is escalation, not self-care.

### 1.9 Culture, faith and the moral framework

**Three moral worlds in one room.** The family's moral framework, the profession's, and the clinician's own
are frequently three different systems, and the clinician's is the one least likely to be named. In the
households this book is written for, the operative framework is often not any of the five Western traditions
but a religious or communal one: duties are owed to a lineage rather than to an individual; the family's
reputation is a moral good in its own right; elder authority is not negotiable by argument; and shame, rather
than guilt, is the mechanism by which wrongdoing is registered.

**What that changes about the four principles.** Autonomy in the individualist sense is not the organising
value in many of these households, and a clinician who treats its absence as a deficit will misread
deference as incapacity and consultation as inefficiency. The workable translation is not to abandon
autonomy but to ask the family's version of the question: *who in this family has the standing to decide,
and does the person most affected have a route to be heard?* That question travels across frameworks, and it
is answerable in any of them.

**Shame, guilt and the ethics of disclosure.** In a guilt-oriented framework, wrongdoing is registered by the
individual's conscience and confession is restorative. In a shame-oriented framework, wrongdoing is
registered by the community's judgement and disclosure may be catastrophic for the whole household. This is
not a reason to keep a secret that must be told; it is a reason to plan *how* it is told, with whom, and in
whose presence, which is a clinical skill and not a concession.

**Faith as a moral authority in the room.** A pastor, an imam, or an elder may hold the authority that the
clinician's code does not. Where that is so, the code is not the only source of moral authority in the case
and pretending otherwise produces a clinician who is right and useless. The moves that work are the ones from
Week 13 of MFT 703: join the framework, add a mechanism, and hold one non-negotiable.

> **DEBATE** — "A therapist should never act against a competent adult client's stated wish, whatever the
> consequences." Argue for: the client's authority over their own life is the foundation of the profession,
> paternalism has a long and cruel history, and a clinician who overrides once will override again. Argue
> against: competence is not a switch, adults are frequently pressed by people with more power than they
> have, and a clinician who watches a foreseeable death without acting has chosen an abstraction over a
> person. Then state your actual rule, and the case in which you would break it — because every clinician
> eventually meets that case, and the ones who have never thought about it are the ones who improvise badly.

### 1.10 Evidence note

**What the research supports.** The four-principles framework is the most widely used analytic structure in
clinical ethics teaching, and studies of ethics education consistently find that structured decision models
improve the *consistency* and *documentation* of ethical reasoning, though not necessarily the quality of the
decisions. The finding that moral distress is prevalent in mental health work, and that it is associated with
intention to leave rather than with clinical incompetence, is well replicated. The association between
reflective supervision and improved ethical reasoning is modest but consistent.

**What is weaker.** Almost everything about *outcomes*. There is no body of evidence showing that clinicians
trained in one tradition make better decisions than clinicians trained in another, and the field's outcome
measures are self-report and vignette-based. The literature on moral injury in mental health professions is
recent and largely qualitative, mostly borrowed from military and nursing research.

**What is not supported.** That codes prevent misconduct — the disciplinary literature consistently finds that
violations are committed by clinicians who know the code. That ethics can be taught as compliance —
knowledge of rules is necessary and has never been sufficient. And that ethical reasoning is a stable trait;
it is a practice, and it degrades under workload and isolation, which is why consultation is a structural
protection rather than a courtesy.

**The matrix cell.** *Development is probabilistic and non-linear; the same input produces different outcomes
depending on timing and context.* A clinician with excellent ethical reasoning at nine in the morning, with
a supervisor available, is a different decision-maker from the same clinician at seven in the evening with a
waiting room full of people. The variables that most reliably predict ethical failure are not moral but
situational: fatigue, isolation, and speed.

### 1.10a What the codes do not cover

A short section with a long consequence. The professional codes are necessary and they are not a complete
account of the moral life of a clinician, and the gap is where most of the difficulty lives.

**The codes address conduct, not character.** They say what may not be done; they say almost nothing about
what kind of clinician to be. The virtues that matter most in this work — patience with a family that
returns for the eleventh time with the same problem, courage in a case conference where the consultant is
wrong, humility in front of a grandmother who has raised six children — are not in any code and are not
enforceable. They are formed by supervision, by example, and by the company a clinician keeps.

**The codes are written for individuals.** Confidentiality, consent and competence are all framed around a
single client and a single clinician. Multiperson work, which is the whole of this course, is covered by
adaptations rather than by the codes' own logic. The clinician who follows the code precisely in a family
case may still be unjust, because the code has no category for the second client.

**The codes are jurisdiction-bound.** Every code is written against a legal background that differs by
country and, in federal systems, by state. Statements about reporting obligations, minors' consent, and
record retention that are correct in one jurisdiction are wrong in another. This book states principles and
flags where the law must be checked locally, which is a permanent obligation rather than a limitation of
this text.

**The codes lag.** Reproductive technology, digital records, online therapy, social media contact with
clients, and cross-border tele-therapy are all areas in which practice has outrun guidance. A clinician
working at the edge is making policy by their conduct, and the obligation that follows is to be able to
defend the reasoning, not to be able to point at a rule that does not exist.

### 1.11 Key terms

**Deontology** — the ethics of duty; the right action follows from a rule or obligation regardless of
consequence.

**Consequentialism** — the ethics of outcome; the right action is the one that produces the best overall
result.

**Virtue ethics** — the ethics of character; the question is what a person of good character would do, and
how such a person is formed.

**Ethics of care** — the ethics of relationship and responsiveness; moral life is constituted by dependence
and response rather than by transactions between independent agents.

**Justice** — fairness in the distribution of benefits, risks and resources; in family work, the question of
whose voice is heard and who bears the cost.

**Autonomy** — the client's right to decide what happens to them, grounded in their capacity to do so.

**Beneficence** — the duty to act in the client's interest.

**Non-maleficence** — the duty not to cause harm, including the harms of one's own intervention.

**Moral distress** — knowing the right action and being prevented from taking it.

**Moral injury** — lasting harm following an act against one's moral commitments, or witnessing one.

**Paternalism** — overriding a person's wishes for their own good.

**Relational ethics** — the position that obligations arise from relationships and their power asymmetries,
not only from rules or outcomes.

### 1.12 Self-test

1. Name the five traditions and state the characteristic failure of each.
2. How do the four principles collide in a case involving a seventeen-year-old's confidentiality, and which
   one governs? Defend your answer.
3. What is the difference between moral distress and moral injury, and why does the remedy differ?
4. In the Okonkwo case, what two practices — neither of them in a code — kept the therapy alive?
5. Why does the lecture insist that "because the code says so" is an inadequate answer to a family?
6. What does the ethics of care add to a principles-based analysis, and what is its characteristic
   corruption?
7. What is the test for whether an act of care was really care?
8. Give the seven steps of the decision model, and say which one students most often skip.

**Answers.** *(1)* Deontology — rigidity; consequentialism — self-serving calculation and blindness to the
unrepresented; virtue — vagueness and self-congratulation; care — it can justify anything in the name of the
relationship; justice — abstraction, analysing structures while being unjust to the person present. *(2)*
Autonomy supports her confidence; beneficence may support disclosure as protection; non-maleficence warns that
disclosure may produce the beating she fears; justice notes that she bears the consequence and her parents
hold legal authority. Which governs depends on the assessed risk: in the absence of danger, autonomy and
non-maleficence together; where there is danger, non-maleficence governs and the disclosure is planned with
her rather than about her. *(3)* Distress is being unable to do what you believe right, and is remedied
structurally; injury is having acted against your commitments, and requires naming, repair and sometimes a
change of setting. *(4)* Consulting before answering, and telling Ada exactly what had been said to Chuka.
*(5)* Because the code is the floor of the reasoning, not its content; a family cannot use a rule they cannot
understand, and a clinician who offers only the rule is heard as evasive and hides something in doing so.
*(6)* It adds attention, dependence and power; its corruption is that it can justify almost anything as
care. *(7)* Noddings's test: the cared-for must be able to say they were cared for. *(8)* State the ethical
question; establish the facts; identify parties and interests; apply the frameworks; consult; decide, act and
record; review — the review is the step most often skipped.

### 1.13 Reflective task

Write down the last time you did something you believed to be wrong because you were required to. Do not
write about the ethics of the requirement; write about what you did, in one paragraph, in plain words. Then
answer three questions: who else knew? what did you tell yourself at the time? and what would have had to be
available for you to have acted differently? Most students find the answer to the third question is
concrete — a supervisor, an hour, a policy, one other person who agreed — which is the argument for
structures rather than for character.

## Week 2 — The codes and informed consent

**What codes do and do not do.** Codes set a floor, not a ceiling. They are written to be enforceable, which
means they are often silent on the hardest questions. They also vary by jurisdiction and by professional
body; know which one governs you.

**The core obligations across the major codes:**

1. Competence and its limits
2. Informed consent (including consent for recording and for supervision)
3. Confidentiality and its limits
4. Multiple relationships
5. Sexual intimacy with clients and former clients (prohibited; time limits vary)
6. Fees and financial arrangements
7. Advertising and representation
8. Research ethics
9. Cultural competence and non-discrimination
10. Reporting obligations and legal compliance

**Informed consent in family therapy is a document and a conversation.** It should cover: who the client is;
who is seen and in what combination; the confidentiality policy including secrets; recording and
supervision; fees and cancellation; the limits of confidentiality (risk, abuse, court order); and the
complaint route. Review it aloud in the first session, in the family's language, and record that you did.

**Why consent is the hinge of the course.** Almost every other week depends on it. Confidentiality is only
meaningful if the parties understood, at the start, who would be told what. A boundary can only be violated
if it was known to exist. A record can only be defended if the client consented to what it contains. And the
defence that succeeds at a professional tribunal is almost never "I did the right thing"; it is "the client
understood, agreed, and I recorded that they did." Consent is the one document in this book that a clinician
will be asked to produce years later, and it is the one most often written badly.

> **CAUTION** — A signed form is not consent. Consent is a process of understanding, and the signature is
> merely evidence that the process happened. A family that signed a document they could not read, in a
> language they do not speak, at the end of a first session in which they were upset, has not consented, and
> a tribunal will say so. The signature protects the clinic; the conversation protects the family; and the
> clinician needs both.

**The teaching decision that makes the week work.** The lecture opens with a real disciplinary finding — a
clinician suspended for something that sounds trivial — and the class predicts which clause was breached.
They are usually wrong, and they are wrong in an instructive direction: they assume the violation was
dramatic (sex, fraud, a breach of confidence) when it was almost always procedural (no documented consent, a
record that did not match the account, a policy never explained). The exercise revalues documentation, which
is the part of ethics that students find least interesting and that ends most careers.

**The sentence the lecture must land.** "You will not be struck off for having the wrong theory. You will be
struck off for not having written down what you agreed."

### 2.2 The ten obligations, and the two that generate most complaints

**1. Competence and its limits.** The obligation to practise within one's training and to refer when a
presentation exceeds it. The obligation is not to know everything; it is to know the boundary and to act on
it. (Week 7.)

**2. Informed consent.** Everything in the rest of this week.

**3. Confidentiality and its limits.** The obligation to protect information and the obligation to disclose
in specified circumstances. The second half is the part families do not hear. (Weeks 3, 4, 5.)

**4. Multiple relationships.** The obligation not to hold a second role that could impair judgement or
exploit the relationship. (Week 6.)

**5. Sexual intimacy with clients and former clients.** Prohibited. Time limits on former clients vary by
code, from two years to never, and the variation tells you something about how seriously the profession
regards the boundary.

**6. Fees and financial arrangements.** The obligation to agree fees in advance, not to exploit financial
dependency, and not to continue treatment for financial reasons when it is no longer indicated. (Week 12.)

**7. Advertising and representation.** The obligation not to overstate competence or outcomes. In an era of
social media, this is the fastest-growing category of complaint, because a clinician's profile is advertising
whether they intended it to be or not.

**8. Research ethics.** Consent, deception, anonymity, and the ethics of publishing case material. (Week 13.)

**9. Cultural competence and non-discrimination.** Now written into most codes, and the clause with the least
agreement about what compliance looks like. Week 13 of MFT 703 is the substantive account; in this course the
obligation is to notice when the clinician's cultural assumptions are producing a decision.

**10. Reporting obligations and legal compliance.** The obligation to know the local law on child protection,
adult protection, and the reporting of serious harm. A clinician who does not know their jurisdiction's
threshold has an obligation they cannot discharge.

**The two that generate most complaints.** The disciplinary literature is remarkably consistent, and the
answer surprises students: not sexual misconduct, which is rarer and more dramatic, but **failure to maintain
adequate records** and **failure to obtain or document informed consent**. The two are connected, because the
record is where consent lives. A clinician with an immaculate record and a mediocre theory is safer, in
professional terms, than a brilliant clinician who cannot show what was agreed.

**Why that is not as cynical as it sounds.** The record is not a bureaucratic artefact. It is the only
evidence that a family was told something, understood it, and agreed; that a risk was assessed; that a
decision was reasoned rather than drifted into. In the absence of that evidence, a tribunal has only the
family's account and the clinician's memory, and it will prefer the family's. Documentation is the
clinician's honesty made checkable.

### 2.2a Where the codes disagree, and what a clinician does then

Students often assume the codes say the same thing in different words. They do not, and the differences are
instructive because they reveal that the profession has not settled the question.

**Issue 1 — Secrets in couple work.** The AAMFT code requires that therapists clarify at the outset which
persons are clients and what relationship each has to the therapist, and that they obtain written consent to
disclose information; it does not prescribe a secrets policy. The APA ethics code addresses multiple
relationships and confidentiality but is written for individual treatment and gives no guidance on
therapeutic secrets in couple work. National codes vary further, and many say nothing at all. The result is
that a couple therapist's confidentiality policy is a *professional judgement* rather than a compliance
requirement, which is why it must be written down and explained.

**Issue 2 — Sexual relationships with former clients.** Most codes prohibit them outright or impose a time
limit that ranges from two years to five years to never. The disagreement is not trivial: it reflects a
genuine dispute about whether the harm arises from the power asymmetry (which does not end when therapy does)
or from the therapeutic relationship (which does). A clinician moving jurisdictions must check, because the
rule that was correct at home may be a violation at the new post.

**Issue 3 — Duty to warn or protect.** In some jurisdictions the clinician has a legal duty to warn an
identifiable third party of a serious threat; in others the duty is to protect, which may be discharged by
other means; in many there is no statutory duty at all and the obligation is ethical only. A clinician who
learns this from the code rather than from the statute will get it wrong.

**Issue 4 — Minors' consent.** The age at which a young person may consent to treatment without a parent
varies from about twelve to eighteen and is frequently different for different treatments. The codes defer
to law, which means the clinician must know the local statute and not the code.

**What a clinician does when the codes disagree.** Four moves, in order. First, establish which code governs
you — by licence, by membership, and by employment contract, which may impose a stricter standard than either.
Second, apply the *stricter* standard, because a tribunal will. Third, where the codes are silent, write your
policy down and follow it consistently, because consistency is the only defence available when there is no
rule. Fourth, ask the professional body in writing where the stakes are high, and keep the reply.

**The professional point.** The existence of disagreement is not a defect of the codes; it is information
about which questions the profession has not resolved. Those are exactly the questions on which a clinician
must think rather than look up, and they are the ones this course is about.

### 2.2b What a professional body actually does when a complaint arrives

A short account, because clinicians fear the process more than they understand it, and fear produces both
over-documentation and concealment.

**The route.** A complaint is made, in writing, usually by the client or a relative. The registrar assesses
whether it falls within the code's scope. If it does, it is put to the clinician for a written response, with
a deadline. Many complaints end there, because the response shows that the matter is a disagreement rather
than a breach. Those that continue go to a panel, which hears both parties and may dismiss, caution, impose
conditions, suspend, or strike off.

**What the panel asks, in practice.** Not "was the outcome bad?" but three questions: what did the clinician
do; what did they record; and what would a reasonable clinician in that position have done? The third question
is answered by expert evidence and by the code, and the first two by the file.

**What helps a clinician, in order of usefulness.** A contemporaneous record of what was agreed. A record of
the reasoning, including options rejected. Evidence of supervision or consultation on the matter. An apology
offered early and without qualification. And a consistent account — the same story in the file, the response,
and the hearing.

**What harms a clinician, in order of seriousness.** Altering or backdating a record, which converts a
complaint into a finding of dishonesty and ends careers that would otherwise have survived. An account that
changes between documents. Defensiveness in the response, which is read as an absence of insight. And
criticising the complainant, which is heard by the panel as a failure to understand the harm.

**The one sentence to remember.** The panel is not asking whether you are a good person; it is asking whether
a reasonable clinician could have done what you did, and whether your file shows that you thought about it.
Almost every answer to that question was decided months earlier, in a note written or not written at the end
of a session.

### 2.3 Informed consent: the eight components

Consent has eight components. A document that omits any of them is incomplete, and the omission is
frequently the one that matters in the case that goes wrong.

**1. Who the client is, and who is not.** In family work this is the first and hardest component. Is the
client the couple, the family, the individual who telephoned, or the child who was brought? The answer
determines who holds the confidentiality and to whom the therapist owes a duty. The consent document should
name the client explicitly, and the answer should be recorded in the family's words: "the client is the
couple, and the children are seen as part of the family's treatment."

**2. Who will be seen, in what combination, and on what terms.** Couple work with occasional individual
sessions is a different arrangement from individual work with occasional joint sessions, and the family must
know which they are getting before they begin. The most common complaint in this area is not that individual
sessions happened, but that nobody said they would.

**3. The confidentiality policy, including the secrets policy.** Stated in plain language, with the
exceptions named rather than implied. (Weeks 3 to 5.)

**4. Recording, observation and supervision.** Families are routinely recorded, observed through a one-way
mirror, or discussed in supervision, and routinely not told. This is one of the clearest and most commonly
breached obligations in training clinics. The disclosure needs three parts: what happens, who sees it, and
how it is stored and destroyed.

**5. Fees, cancellation, insurance and what happens when the money stops.** Including the awkward parts: the
fee for a missed session, the position when a third party pays, and what the clinician will do if a family
falls into arrears. (Week 12.)

**6. The limits of confidentiality.** Risk to self, risk to others, abuse of a child or vulnerable adult,
court orders, and any statutory reporting duty. Named, in examples rather than in abstractions: "if I
believed a child was being hurt, I would have to tell the authorities, and I would tell you first that I was
doing it."

**7. The complaints route.** Who to contact, and what will happen. A family that knows the route is less
likely to need it, and a clinician who discloses it looks less like someone with something to hide.

**8. The right to withdraw.** Including the right to withdraw from recording, from research, and from
treatment, and what happens to the record afterwards. Consent is not a contract of attendance.

**The test of the document.** Hand it to someone who has never been in therapy and ask them what will happen
to their information if they tell the therapist something dangerous. If they cannot answer, the document has
failed, whatever it says.

### 2.3a Consent when the family cannot read the document

Most of the world's families do not read a clinic's consent form fluently, and a substantial minority cannot
read it at all. A clinician who responds by reading the document aloud in English has produced a signature
and not a consent.

**The four adaptations, in order of importance.**

**1. Change the medium before you change the words.** Consent is a conversation and it can be witnessed.
Sit with the family, explain the eight components in their language, in plain speech, and ask each of them to
say back what they understood. Then record, in the file: "consent explained orally in Yoruba to both parents
and to the paternal aunt; each repeated the confidentiality limits and the supervision arrangement in their
own words; all agreed. Written summary provided; both parents marked; consent form signed by the father as
head of household and by the mother." That record is stronger evidence of consent than any signed form.

**2. Use a witness who is not a family member.** A colleague, an interpreter, or a member of the clinic staff
who can attest that the explanation happened. The witness is not a formality: in a dispute two years later,
a witness is a person who can be asked what was said.

**3. Reduce the document, do not translate it.** A translation of a dense document remains dense. The
effective version is one page, in short sentences, with the eight components as headings and the exceptions
in bold. Where literacy is limited, a page of icons or a simple diagram of the four confidentiality limits is
more useful than prose, and is used in several services.

**4. Give them something to take away and to show someone else.** The consent conversation usually needs to
be repeated at home, with a relative who was not present and whose opinion the family trusts. A one-page
summary in the family's language, with the clinic's telephone number, is the only part of the process that
continues after the family leaves, and it is the part that most determines whether they return.

**The error to avoid.** Do not treat limited literacy as limited capacity. A woman who cannot read a form can
understand confidentiality perfectly well, and will hold the clinician to it more precisely than a reader
would. The obligation is to make the understanding possible, not to test the reading.

### 2.3b Consent and the young person

Consent with children and adolescents is not a single question but three: who consents, who assents, and who
is told.

**The legal position, which varies and must be checked.** In most jurisdictions a parent or guardian consents
for a child below a stated age; above that age, and in some jurisdictions for specific treatments such as
contraception or mental health care, the young person may consent themselves. Many jurisdictions recognise a
"mature minor" whose capacity is assessed rather than assumed. Both parents may need to consent where they
share custody, and a clinician who accepts one parent's consent without checking may find the treatment
challenged later.

**Assent, which is not the same as consent.** A child who is old enough to understand what therapy is should
be asked whether they agree to it, in language they have, and their refusal should be taken seriously even
where the law permits the parent to override it. The clinical reason is not legal: a nine-year-old who has
been brought to a therapist without being asked will cooperate in the first session and withdraw by the
third, and the family will describe this as the child's problem.

**What the young person is told, and what is kept from them.** There are three separate audiences in a child
case: the child, the parents, and the institution. The consent conversation must establish, before anything
else, what the child will be told about who sees what. A twelve-year-old is entitled to know whether what
she says will be reported to her mother, and a therapist who does not tell her is not protecting her; she is
teaching her, accurately, that adults cannot be trusted with information.

**The four-part script for a child's consent conversation.**

1. "Your mum and dad have asked me to talk with you. You do not have to talk about anything you do not want
   to. If you want to stop, you can say so and we will stop."
2. "What you say here stays here, unless I am worried that somebody is being hurt — including you. If that
   happens I will tell you before I tell anyone else."
3. "I talk to other people about my work, with their names hidden, so that I can learn to do it better. I
   will not use your name."
4. "Is there anything you want to ask me before we start?"

**The age at which each part lands.** Fifty-five percent of adolescent self-disclosure depends on part 2 being
true and being believed. A therapist who says it and then breaks it without warning has done more damage than
one who never offered it.

### 2.4 The first session: consent in practice

The consent conversation in a first session takes twelve to fifteen minutes and is the best investment in the
hour. The sequence below is used throughout the course.

**Minute 1 — Name the arrangement.** "Before we start, I want to tell you how I work, and then you can decide
whether you want to go on. Nothing you say in the next ten minutes goes into a record unless you decide to
continue."

**Minutes 2 to 5 — Who the client is.** "I am going to be working with you as a couple. That means the
relationship is my client, and I will not take sides with either of you. It also means that if one of you
telephones me, I will tell the other that you did, without telling them what you said."

**Minutes 5 to 9 — The confidentiality limits, with examples.** "What you tell me stays here, with four
exceptions. If I think you are in danger of seriously hurting yourself, I have to act. If I think someone
else is in danger from you, I have to act. If a child is being hurt, the law requires me to report it. And if
a court orders me to produce my records, I have to obey. In the first three, I will tell you what I am doing
before I do it, unless telling you would put someone in danger."

**Minutes 9 to 11 — Recording and supervision.** "I record some sessions, and I take my work to a supervisor
who is also a therapist. Your first names are used; your surname is not. The recording is deleted after six
months. You can refuse the recording and still be treated."

**Minutes 11 to 13 — Fees and practicalities.** Fee, cancellation, who pays, what happens if it is late.

**Minute 13 — The secrets policy.** "There is one more thing, and it is the most important sentence of the
hour. If one of you asks to see me alone, I will say yes. But I will not keep a secret that affects this
relationship, because a therapy built on a secret is a therapy that is not working. If something like that
comes up, I will tell you both that it has come up, and I will help whoever it is to decide how to say it. Is
that acceptable to each of you?"

**Minutes 14 to 15 — The question that tests it.** "Tell me, in your own words, what you have understood
about who I would tell if you told me something dangerous." Then record the answer. The recording of *their
words* is the evidence of consent; the signature is not.

**Why the sequence is fixed.** Because the four limits are the parts families do not hear, and they are
heard only if they are given last, with examples, after the clinician has established that they are not
looking for a reason to report. A therapist who begins with the limits has begun with a threat.

### 2.4a The sentence that has to be said, and the four ways of saying it badly

Of the fifteen minutes of a first session's consent conversation, one sentence does most of the work, and it
is the one trainees most often soften. It is the secrets policy.

**The sentence.** "If one of you sees me alone and tells me something that affects this relationship, I will
not keep it. I will tell you both that something has been said, and I will help whoever it is to decide how to
say it — but I will not carry it."

**The four bad versions, and why each fails.**

**"There are no secrets in this therapy."** False, and heard as a prohibition. Couples test it in the first
month, discover that the therapist does keep ordinary confidences, and conclude that the rule was rhetorical.

**"Anything you tell me individually, I may share."** A licence without limits. The family hears "she will
tell him", which suppresses the individual disclosure entirely, and the therapist loses the material that
would have been most useful.

**"We can discuss it if it comes up."** The most common and the most damaging, because it is a deferral of the
question that must be answered before the question arises. When it arises, the therapist is negotiating under
pressure with a person who has already disclosed.

**"I will use my clinical judgement."** True, and useless to a family, because it tells them the rule is the
therapist's discretion. Every subsequent decision will be experienced as arbitrary.

**Why the good version works.** It is specific about what the therapist will *do* (tell both that something
has been said), it preserves the discloser's control over the content (help them decide how to say it), and it
names the limit (the secret will not be carried). Three commitments, all of which can be kept, which is what
distinguishes a policy from a warning.

**The follow-up that tests whether it landed.** "If your wife told me something on Tuesday that she did not
want you to know, what do you think I would do?" A husband who can answer accurately has consented; one who
cannot has heard the words and not the arrangement. The answer is worth recording, in his words.

### 2.5 Case: the family who signed and did not understand

**The referral.** The Baloguns — Femi, 55, Funke, 49, and Femi's mother, 81, with dementia — were referred
eight months ago by a physician at the private hospital where Mama is seen. The original referral was for
"carer stress". The family attended four sessions and stopped.

**Why the case is in this week.** The family has now returned, and has made a complaint: that the clinic
"told the hospital things about us without asking". The complaint is being handled by the clinic manager, and
the file has been sent to the therapist's supervisor.

**What the file contains.** A two-page consent form, signed by Femi in the first session. No date of
explanation. No record that the limits of confidentiality were discussed. No record that supervision was
mentioned. A note in session three: "spoke to Dr Adeyemi at the hospital re: Mrs Balogun's care — agreed to
share summary."

**What actually happened.** In session three, the therapist telephoned the physician at the hospital to ask
about Mama's medication, because Funke had said she was "sleeping all day". In the course of the call she
described the household: that the daughter-in-law was exhausted, that there was a question about whether
Mama could be left alone, that the family had "not been entirely honest with the physician about how much
help they have". The physician recorded this in Mama's hospital file. A receptionist who knew the family read
it and mentioned it to a relative. The family learned of it from the relative.

**The analysis, through the seven steps.**

**Step 1 — The ethical question.** Not "was the telephone call wrong?" but: *what did this family consent to,
what did the therapist owe the physician and the family respectively, and what does the clinic owe them now
that a harm has occurred?*

**Step 2 — The facts.** The consent form covered confidentiality in general terms. It did not name
third-party consultation. The therapist believed she had "implied consent" because the family had asked her to
liaise with the hospital. What the family had asked for was that she *telephone about medication*; they had
not agreed to a description of the household. The disclosure reached a relative through a third party — the
receptionist — which the therapist could not have foreseen and which she is nonetheless responsible for
having made possible.

**Step 3 — The parties.** Mama: her clinical information was shared with her physician, which is defensible,
and her family's private business was shared, which is not. Funke: the description of her exhaustion, and of
the family's "not being honest", is now in a hospital file. Femi: he signed a form he did not read. The
physician: he received information he did not ask for and recorded it. The relative: now holds information
the family did not choose to give. The clinic: a complaint, and an exposure.

**Step 4 — The frameworks.** *Deontology:* the consent did not cover this disclosure, and the duty was to
obtain it. *Consequence:* the therapist's goal — better medication for Mama — was legitimate and could have
been achieved by asking about medication alone. *Virtue:* would she have made the call if the family were in
the room? *Care:* the family's trust was the treatment's only asset, and it has been spent on a telephone
call. *Justice:* the person most affected, Funke, was the person with least standing, and it is her
description that is now circulating.

**Step 5 — Consultation.** The supervisor, the clinic manager, and — critically — the clinician's own
professional indemnity provider, because a complaint has been made. This is not an admission of fault; it is
the ordinary procedure.

**Step 6 — Decision and action.** Three things, in this order.

*First, the apology and the account.* The therapist meets the family — all three adults, including Mama, who
is told in simple terms — and does four things: states what she did, without euphemism ("I telephoned the
doctor about Mama's medication, and while I was on the telephone I described how things are at home, and I
should not have done that"); states what she should have done (asked their permission first, and described
only the medication); apologises without qualification; and states what she will do now (write to the
physician asking that the household description be removed or annotated as not consented to, and confirm in
writing to the family).

*Second, the record.* An incident note in the file: what happened, what was disclosed, to whom, the harm
identified, the action taken, and the review date. Not a defence; an account.

*Third, the practice change.* The clinic's consent form now includes a named item: "I may need to speak to
other professionals involved in your care. I will ask your permission before doing so, and I will tell you
what I intend to say." The therapist's own practice changes in a way that is recorded in her supervision
notes.

**Step 7 — Review.** At three months: has the letter to the physician been answered; is the annotation in
place; has the family returned; and has the therapist made any further third-party calls without permission?
The answer to the last question is the one that determines whether this was an incident or a pattern.

**What the case teaches that the code does not.** Four things.

1. **"Liaising with the hospital" is not consent to describe a household.** Consent is specific. A family that
   asks you to discuss medication has consented to a discussion of medication, and the scope of consent is
   the scope of the request.
2. **The disclosure you make is the disclosure that circulates.** The therapist made one telephone call to one
   physician. The information reached a receptionist, a relative, and now a complaint. A clinician who thinks
   in terms of "one person" has not understood how information moves in a small community.
3. **The signature is the weakest part of the file.** Had the therapist written four lines in session one —
   what she explained, in whose words it was repeated back, and what the family agreed — the complaint would
   still have been made and would have been answerable.
4. **The apology is a clinical act.** The family returned after it, which they would not have done after a
   letter from a manager.

### 2.6 Transcript: the consent conversation that failed, and the one that worked

Two transcripts. The first is the original first session, reconstructed from the therapist's memory in
supervision. The second is the repeat, eight months later, with the same family.

**The original, in five turns.**

> **T1 Therapist:** Before we start, I have a form for you to sign. It is about confidentiality. — *The
> disclosure of the document rather than of the arrangement. The family now believes the form is the consent.*
>
> **T2 Femi:** *(signs without reading)* Where do I sign?
>
> **T3 Therapist:** There. *(pause)* So, tell me about your mother.
> — *Two minutes and eleven seconds into a therapy that will last four sessions, and the four limits of
> confidentiality have not been mentioned.*
>
> **T4 Funke:** She is not sleeping. She wanders at night. I am the one who —
>
> **T5 Therapist:** *(interrupting)* And does the doctor know about the wandering?
> — *The question that leads, three sessions later, to the telephone call, and that shows the therapist was
> already thinking about the physician on day one. The failure was not a lapse; it was a pattern set in the
> first five minutes.*

**The repeat, eight months later, in nine turns.**

> **T1 Therapist:** Before we talk about anything, I want to tell you how I work, and I want to tell you what
> I got wrong last time. — *The apology first. The family has come back in order to hear it, and everything
> else will be heard through it.*
>
> **T2 Femi:** *(nods)*
>
> **T3 Therapist:** I telephoned your doctor about your mother's medication, which you had asked me to do. I

> **T5 Therapist:** I did not know that. It makes it worse, and it is my responsibility, because I made the

> therapist and onto a stranger.*
>
> **T6 Femi:** So what happens now?
>
> **T7 Therapist:** Three things. I have written to the doctor asking that the part about our home be removed

### 2.6a The record of consent, written out

The single most useful paragraph a clinician will ever write, and the one that is almost never written. It
goes at the end of the first session's note, takes ninety seconds, and looks like this.

> "Consent explained orally, in English and Yoruba, to both parents and to the paternal aunt. Covered: the
> client is the couple, with the children seen within the family's treatment; no secrets policy, stated as a
> promise to tell both if a secret arises and to help the discloser decide how to say it; supervision (first
> names only, clinic supervisor, Dr O.); audio recording of sessions 1–4, to be deleted at six months, family
> may refuse without affecting treatment; fee ₦X per session, 24 hours' notice for cancellation; limits of
> confidentiality — risk to self, risk to others, child protection, court order — given with examples. Mother
> repeated back: 'if I tell you my husband is hitting me you will not tell him without asking me first' —
> confirmed, with the exception that if children are at risk the report is made regardless and she will be
> told. Father repeated back the supervision arrangement. Both agreed. Written summary in English provided."

**Why each element is there.** The language (proves it was accessible), the audience (proves who was
informed), the content (proves what was covered), the teach-back (proves understanding rather than
transmission), the correction in the mother's restatement (proves the clinician did not accept a
misunderstanding), and the written summary (proves something continues after the session).

**What the paragraph is not.** It is not a defence prepared for a tribunal. It is the clinician's own record
of an agreement she intends to keep, and its practical function is that she can reread it in session six, when
she has forgotten what she promised.

**The habit worth forming.** Write it in the session, before the family leaves, and read it back to them:
"this is what I am writing down about what we agreed — is it right?" Two sentences of confirmation, and the
consent is real.

### 2.7 Laboratory: drafting the consent document

**Aim.** To produce a one-page informed-consent document for couple therapy that a family with limited
literacy could actually use, and to defend every sentence in it.

**Procedure.** Students draft the document in forty minutes against the eight components. They then exchange
with a partner who plays a family member reading it for the first time, and who must answer three questions
from the document alone: *who is your client? who would be told if I said I was afraid of my husband? and
what happens if I miss a session?* If the document cannot answer all three, it is rewritten in the session.
The last twenty minutes is a plenary in which two documents are read aloud and the class marks them.

**Marking rubric (20 marks).**

| Criterion | 0–1 | 2–3 | 4–5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The eight components** | Four or fewer present | Six or seven present | All eight, in plain language, with the exceptions given as examples |
| **Who the client is** | Unclear or absent | Named, but the implications are not | Named, with what it means for confidentiality and for the individual sessions |
| **Plain language** | Clinical vocabulary, long sentences | Mostly plain, with lapses | Readable by a twelve-year-old; no sentence over twenty words; the three test questions answerable |
| **The secrets policy** | Absent | Stated as a rule | Stated as a rule *and* as a promise about how it will be applied |
| **The record of consent** | Signature only | A note that consent was explained | A note recording what was explained, what the family said back, and who witnessed it |

**The distinction that carries most of the marks.** The top band requires the document to state the secrets
policy *as a promise about conduct* — "if this happens, I will do this" — rather than as a rule. A rule tells
the family what is forbidden; a promise tells them what the clinician will do. Families comply with promises
and litigate rules.

**The three sentences that fail most often.** "Confidentiality will be maintained in accordance with
professional standards" (means nothing to a family). "Information may be shared with relevant third parties
where clinically indicated" (a licence to do what the Balogun therapist did). And "the therapist reserves the
right to breach confidentiality in exceptional circumstances" (a threat with no content). All three are
rewritten in the laboratory, and the rewrites are the most useful pages the students take away.

### 2.8 Six errors

**Error 1 — Treating the signature as the consent.** It is evidence of a process, not the process. A file
with a signature and no note of what was explained is a file that will not survive a complaint.

**Error 2 — Disclosing the document instead of the arrangement.** Handing over a form and saying "it is about
confidentiality" is not a consent conversation. The family learns what they need by being told, in speech,
with examples, and being asked to say it back.

**Error 3 — Leaving the limits until they are needed.** A family that learns about the reporting duty after a
disclosure has been made to them has been ambushed. The limits are disclosed at the start, and again as the
case requires.

**Error 4 — Not mentioning supervision and recording.** The most commonly breached obligation in training
clinics, and the one most easily fixed. "I take my work to a supervisor" is a sentence, not a form.

**Error 5 — Assuming one parent's consent covers the household.** Custody arrangements, guardianship, and
the standing of a grandmother who is raising a child all affect who can consent. Ask, record the answer, and
where it is unclear, obtain both.

**Error 6 — Writing the consent note from memory.** The note must be written in the session, in the family's
words, and dated. A note reconstructed a week later is worth very little, and a tribunal will notice the
distinction.

### 2.9 Culture, faith and consent

**Consent is not always an individual act.** In many of the households this book is written for, a decision to
enter therapy is made by a family, and the person who signs is the person with standing rather than the person
most affected. A clinician who insists on individual consent from each adult will sometimes be told what they
want to hear and will not have consent in any meaningful sense. The workable procedure is to obtain the
signature from the person with standing *and* to speak directly to each adult, including the grandmother and
the adolescent, asking each whether they agree. The record then says both things.

**The head of household and the wife's separate consent.** A woman may be in treatment because her husband
arranged it, and may not feel able to refuse. This is a consent problem, and its signs are the woman who says
nothing in the first session and looks at her husband before answering. The clinical response is not to
refuse treatment; it is to see her alone once, early, and ask whether she wants to be here. The answer is the
consent.

**Faith, authority and the referral route.** Where a family has come because a pastor or an imam advised it,
the authority in the room is partly elsewhere. The consent conversation should establish who else will be told
about the treatment, because in these households the answer is frequently the religious community, and a
family that assumes the therapist will brief the pastor and discovers otherwise will feel exposed.

**Language and the interpreter.** A consent conversation conducted through an interpreter is valid, provided
the interpreter is briefed (Week 13 of MFT 703) and the family member is not used. A husband interpreting for
his wife in a consent conversation about confidentiality is a contradiction in terms.

> **DEBATE** — "A family that cannot read the consent form cannot give informed consent, and treatment should
> not begin." Argue for: consent requires understanding, a document is the standard evidence of understanding,
> and proceeding without it exposes the family to treatment they did not agree to. Argue against: literacy is
> not capacity, oral consent with a witness is a recognised and lawful route, and refusing treatment on the
> grounds of literacy denies the service to the people who need it most. Then write the clause you would put
> in your own consent policy to cover the oral route, and say what evidence you would keep.

### 2.10 Evidence note

**What the research supports.** The relationship between documentation quality and disciplinary outcomes is
among the most consistent findings in professional regulation: inadequate records appear in a large minority
of substantiated complaints, and in many the record failure is the finding rather than the clinical act.
Studies of informed consent consistently find that recall of consent information is poor — typically less than
half of what was explained is remembered a week later, and much less where the information is complex or the
person is distressed — which supports the use of plain language, written summaries and teach-back methods.
Teach-back, in which the client repeats the explanation in their own words, reliably improves comprehension.

**What is weaker.** The evidence on whether better consent processes improve *clinical* outcomes is thin.
Consent documentation improves defensibility, and it plausibly improves the therapeutic alliance, but the
trials are few and the outcome measures are satisfaction rather than change.

**What is not supported.** That a signature is sufficient. That longer forms produce better consent — the
evidence points the other way, with comprehension falling as length rises. And that the limits of
confidentiality deter families from disclosing — the finding is the reverse, that clear disclosure increases
trust, which is why the sequence in 2.4 puts the limits after the clinician has established the relationship
rather than before.

**The matrix cell.** *Development is probabilistic and non-linear; the same input produces different outcomes
depending on timing and context.* The same consent form, explained to one family in a first session and
posted to another with an appointment letter, produces consent in one case and a complaint in the other.

### 2.11 Key terms

**Code of ethics** — a professional body's enforceable statement of minimum standards.

**Informed consent** — agreement to treatment given by a person who understands what is proposed, its risks,
its alternatives and its limits, and who is free to refuse.

**Assent** — a child's or young person's agreement to treatment, given in language they understand, where the
legal consent rests with a parent or guardian.

**Capacity** — the ability to understand and weigh information relevant to a decision; distinct from age and
from literacy.

**Mature minor** — a young person judged able to consent to treatment without a parent, under some
jurisdictions' law.

**Teach-back** — asking the client to restate the explanation in their own words, as a check on comprehension.

**Therapeutic secret** — information held by the therapist from one member of a couple or family, known to
the other members not to be shared.

**Disclosure** — the giving of information; not the same as consent, which requires understanding and agreement.

**Witness** — a person present at an oral consent conversation who can later attest to what was said.

**Scope of consent** — the limits of what was agreed; consent to discuss medication is not consent to
describe a household.

1. Name the ten core obligations and the two that generate most complaints.

3. Give the eight components of informed consent for couple therapy.
4. What is the difference between consent and assent, and why does the distinction matter clinically?
5. In the Balogun case, what exactly did the family consent to, and what was disclosed beyond it?
6. What are the four limitations of confidentiality, and how should they be framed to a family?
7. What is teach-back, and why does it matter more than the form?
8. What would you write in the record to evidence that consent happened?

**Answers.** *(1)* Competence; informed consent; confidentiality; multiple relationships; sexual intimacy;
fees; advertising; research ethics; cultural competence and non-discrimination; legal compliance — the two that
generate most complaints are inadequate records and failure to document consent. *(2)* Because it evidences a
transaction rather than an understanding; a family can sign a document they cannot read and have consented to
nothing, and a tribunal will say so. *(3)* Who the client is; who is seen and in what combination; the
confidentiality and secrets policy; recording and supervision; fees and cancellation; the limits of
confidentiality; the complaints route; the right to withdraw. *(4)* Consent is the legal authorisation, held by
an adult or a guardian; assent is the young person's agreement, which is not legally required and is
clinically decisive, because a child who did not assent withdraws by the third session. *(5)* Consent was to
discussion of Mama's medication with her physician; the disclosure included the daughter-in-law's exhaustion,
the family's handling of care, and a judgement that they had not been honest — none of which was within the
scope. *(6)* Risk to self, risk to others, abuse of a child or vulnerable adult, and a court order — framed as
promises about what the clinician will do ("I will tell you before I tell anyone"), with examples rather than
abstractions. *(7)* Asking the client to restate the explanation in their own words; it matters because recall
of consent information is poor and because the client's words, recorded, are the evidence of understanding.
*(8)* What was explained; in what language and by whom; what the family said back; who witnessed it; the date;
and what was agreed.

### 2.13 Reflective task

Find a consent form — any clinic's, any profession's — and read it as though you were a frightened parent with
two children and no time. Then answer three questions: what does it tell you that you need to know? what does
it fail to tell you? and what would you have to ask, out loud, to understand what will happen to your
information? Most students find that the document answers the clinic's questions and not the family's, which is
the difference between a release and a consent.

## Week 3 — Confidentiality in the multiperson system

There is no single confidentiality in family work; there is a **policy** you negotiate.

In individual therapy confidentiality is a wall with four doors. In family therapy it is a *room with several
people in it*, each of whom has a different claim on the information, and the therapist's task is not to
defend a wall but to design and run a room. That is a different skill, and the codes — written for the wall —
describe it badly.

**Three workable models.**

| Model | Rule | Advantages | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| No-secrets | Anything told individually may be shared with the couple | Simple, transparent | Individual may withhold important material |
| Full secrecy | Individual disclosures are kept private | Protects individual therapy | Creates a therapeutic secret that distorts the couple work |
| Selective (negotiated) | The therapist and client agree in advance what will be shared | Flexible and realistic | Requires skill and documentation |

Most experienced couple therapists operate a **modified no-secrets policy**: they will not keep a secret that
materially affects the couple's work, and they tell each partner this before the first individual session.
This protects both the relationship and the therapist.

**The distinction that makes the policy workable: privacy is not secrecy.** Privacy is information a person
chooses not to share, and it is legitimate — a client's history of sexual abuse, her feelings about her
mother-in-law, what she earns. Secrecy is information that is being kept *from* someone in a way that
distorts the treatment: an affair in progress, a plan to leave, a diagnosis that the other partner believes
has been excluded. A policy that cannot distinguish the two will be experienced by the family as a demand for
total exposure, and they will keep everything.

**Why the therapist cannot be neutral about a therapeutic secret.** A secret is not a piece of information
stored; it is a *structural feature* of the therapy. Once the therapist knows something the other party does
not, every intervention is affected: she cannot ask a question that would lead there, she cannot respond
naturally to an account given in good faith, and she is, in the technical sense, deceiving one client on
another's behalf. A therapy in that condition is not couple therapy; it is two individual therapies conducted
in the same room.

**Documentation.** Record the policy in the file, in the family's words if possible, and note the date it was
explained. If a secret arises, record what you did and why.

> **CASE** — During an individual session, a wife discloses that she has been having an affair for two years
> and has no intention of ending it or telling her husband. No one is at physical risk. Under a no-secrets
> policy, you tell her at the outset of the individual session that this cannot stay private if the couple
> work is to continue; you then help her decide how and when to disclose, with a deadline. Under a selective
> policy, you would negotiate what can be shared. What you must not do is carry the secret silently for
> months while conducting "couple therapy".

**The teaching decision that makes the week work.** The week turns on one demonstration: the lecturer places
three chairs, sits in one, and plays the therapist who has just been told about the affair. She then conducts
twenty minutes of ordinary couple therapy — asking the husband about his week, responding to the wife's
account of a disagreement — while the class watches the impossibility accumulate. The exercise is
uncomfortable and it is unforgettable: by the end, every student has seen that the therapist is lying, not
by commission but by the shape of her questions.

**The sentence the lecture must land.** "A secret does not sit in a drawer. It changes every question you can
ask, and after a month it has changed the therapy."

### 3.2 Five concepts that organise the week

**1. The client is a construction, not a fact.** In family work "the client" is whatever the therapist and the
family have agreed it is, and that agreement determines who holds the confidentiality. A couple can be the
client, with each partner a participant; a family can be the client, with the children participants; an
individual can be the client, with the family seen as context. Each construction is legitimate; none is
neutral; and the therapist must choose one and say so.

**2. Confidentiality is owed to the system, not distributed among its members.** If the couple is the client,
the confidentiality is held by the couple jointly, which means neither partner can demand it against the
other. This is the sentence that resolves most confusion: "I cannot keep something from the client, and the
client is both of you."

**3. Privacy is a legitimate asymmetry.** Members of a family know different things about each other, and that
is ordinary. A therapist is not obliged to equalise knowledge, and a policy that requires it is not a
confidentiality policy but a transparency rule, which is a different instrument with different costs.

**4. Secrets distort, and distortion is measurable.** The practical test of whether a secret is a
*therapeutic* secret is whether it changes what the therapist does. If she has stopped asking about the
weekends, or begun to steer away from a topic, or finds herself relieved when a session ends early, the secret
is already operating and the policy has failed.

**5. Institutions are third parties with their own claims.** A school, a court, an employer, an insurer, a
child protection agency and a religious body all have information claims, and each has a different legal
standing. The therapist's obligation is to map them at the start, tell the family who they are, and never to
discover one in the middle of a case.

### 3.2a The modified no-secrets policy, in four parts

The policy is a sentence with four components, and each component prevents a specific failure.

**Part 1 — The promise of individual sessions.** "Either of you may ask to see me alone, and I will usually
say yes." Without this, the family believes there are no individual sessions, and the request, when it comes,
is experienced as a breach.

**Part 2 — The limit.** "But I will not keep a secret that affects this relationship." The limit is stated in
terms of *effect*, not of content, because content cannot be predicted and effect can be judged.

**Part 3 — What the therapist will do.** "If something like that comes up, I will tell you both that something
has been said — not what — and I will help whoever it is to decide how and when to say it." The distinction
between telling *that* something was said and telling *what* was said is the mechanism that makes the policy
usable.

**Part 4 — The deadline.** "And I will ask for it to be said here, within a stated time, rather than leaving
it open." A secret with no deadline is a secret; a secret with a deadline is a task. The deadline is
negotiated but not indefinite, and the therapist writes it down.

**The four failure modes the parts prevent.** Without part 1, the request for an individual session feels like
a rupture. Without part 2, the therapist carries secrets by accident. Without part 3, the disclosure of *that*
something was said becomes a guessing game that is worse than the secret. Without part 4, the process has no
end and the family settles into a stable, comfortable dishonesty.

### 3.3 The method: deciding where a disclosure goes

Six questions, asked in order, whenever a family member tells the therapist something in confidence. The
method takes about five minutes of thinking and it converts an alarm into a decision.

**Question 1 — Who is the client, as agreed?** The answer determines everything else. If the couple is the
client, the disclosure belongs to the couple and cannot be kept from one member indefinitely. If the
individual is the client and the family is context, the disclosure is hers to control.

**Question 2 — Is anyone at risk?** A yes takes the case out of this method and into Week 8 or 9. Risk is
always the first question, because the confidentiality policy does not survive it and the family was told so
at the start.

**Question 3 — Is this privacy or secrecy?** Test it with one question: *does knowing this change what I do in
the joint sessions?* If yes, it is secrecy, whatever its subject. If no, it is privacy and it stays.

**Question 4 — Who else's information is in it?** Most disclosures are not about one person. An affair
involves a third party; a diagnosis involves a family's history; a plan to leave involves children. The
therapist is not only holding the confider's information, and the other people in it have claims.

**Question 5 — What happens if it moves, and when?** Who will be told, by whom, in what words, with whom
present, and on what date. The therapist's job is to make this a plan rather than an event.

**Question 6 — What is my part, and what is not?** The therapist does not disclose the secret; the client
does. The therapist's role is to insist that it moves, to help plan how, to be present, and to manage the
session afterwards. A therapist who takes on the disclosure has taken the responsibility for it, and the
client has been relieved of the only act that could repair the relationship.

**The rule that governs the six.** The therapist never lies. She may decline to answer, and she must say that
she is declining and why: "I am not going to answer that, and I want to tell you that I am not, rather than
let you think I have." The distinction between a refusal and a lie is the whole of the professional position.

### 3.3a The individual session inside family work

An individual session within a couple or family treatment is a clinical instrument with a purpose, a
structure, and an end. Used badly, it is the mechanism by which therapy is destroyed.

**Why offer one.**
- To assess risk in private, where a partner's presence would make disclosure impossible.
- To hear one person's account of something they cannot say in front of the other — usually fear, shame, or
  the wish to leave.
- To prepare a person to say something in the joint session, which is the most common and most useful reason.
- To assess for coercive control, which cannot be assessed with the controller present.

**The rule that governs the offer.** Never hold an individual session with one member of a couple or family
without having stated the policy, in front of both, before the first one. The session is not the problem; the
undisclosed session is.

**The structure, in five minutes and forty.** *First two minutes:* restate the policy — "you know I will not
keep a secret that affects the relationship; is there anything you want to say in that knowledge?" *Middle
thirty:* listen, assess, and do not ask leading questions about the other partner's conduct. *Last eight:*
agree what, if anything, moves, and how. The session does not end with a half-disclosure and a
reassurance.

**The sentence that ends it well.** "So: what I understand is that you want to leave, and you have not told him,
and you are not ready to. I am not going to tell him. I am going to ask you to tell him, here, within two
weeks, and I will help you plan what you say. If you have not told him in two weeks, I will tell him only that
something has been said and that he should ask you — and I will tell you first that I am doing it."

**What makes an individual session unsafe.** Three things. It is offered without the policy being stated. It
becomes the *only* place where anything true is said, which is the classic pattern of a couple therapy that
has quietly become two individual therapies. Or it is used to gather information about the other partner,
which converts the therapist into an investigator.

### 3.4 Confidentiality and the institutions around the family

A family does not arrive alone. The information map below is drawn on the whiteboard in the first session of
any case with an institutional referrer, and it frequently changes the treatment.

**The school.** Holds attendance, behaviour, academic and safeguarding records. Has a legitimate interest in
the child's functioning, no right to the therapy's content, and a tendency to expect a report. The therapist's
position: the school may be given what the family agrees to, and where the school is the referrer it must be
told at the outset what it will and will not receive.

**The court.** Has power to compel records and testimony, and is the only party on the list that can override
the family's wishes entirely. (Week 11.)

**The employer, the insurer, the employee assistance programme.** Frequently the payer, and frequently the
party with the weakest claim and the strongest expectation. The consent conversation must establish what the
payer will be told: usually attendance and nothing else.

**The child protection agency.** Has statutory powers that vary by jurisdiction and frequently override
confidentiality. The family must be told, at the start, in plain language, what the therapist is obliged to
report and to whom.

**The religious body.** Rarely has a legal claim and frequently has a social one. Where a pastor or imam
referred the family, or is supporting them, the question of what the therapist will tell the religious
authority must be settled early, because families in these households often assume the therapist will brief
the clergy and may be distressed to discover otherwise — or, worse, may assume confidentiality and discover
the briefing.

**The extended family.** The most consequential party in many of these cases and the least considered. The
grandmother, the uncle who pays, the aunt who is a nurse: each may be told something by a family member and
each may act on it. The therapist cannot control this and should not pretend to; the workable sentence is
"what is said in this room may be repeated outside it by any of us, and I cannot prevent that. What I can
control is what I say."

**The rule for the map.** Draw it in the first session, name every party, and ask the family who has a claim
in their view. The map is then recorded, and every subsequent request for information is answered by
reference to it rather than improvised.

### 3.4a Four scenarios at the boundary

The categories are easy in the abstract and hard at four in the afternoon. The four scenarios below are the
ones that most often divide a supervision group.

**Scenario one — the past affair.** A husband discloses, in an individual session, an affair that ended six
years ago, before the couple's second child was born. He is not going to tell his wife and says it is "over and
irrelevant". *Analysis:* secrecy, not privacy, because it changes what the therapist does — she can no longer
explore the wife's account of the last six years without knowing that the husband holds a different history.
The end of the affair does not end the secret's effect; it only removes one of the reasons for urgency. The
policy applies, the deadline is set, and the therapist is honest that the disclosure may be worse for the
marriage than the affair was.

**Scenario two — the diagnosis.** A wife discloses a new diagnosis of a chronic illness, and asks that her
husband not be told yet. *Analysis:* this is closer to privacy, and the test settles it — the opposite: knowing
this *does* change what the therapist does, but the change is in her attention rather than her honesty, and the
question of who knows the diagnosis belongs, first, to the patient. The exception is where the illness bears
directly on the treatment's subject matter, as it would if the couple presented with the husband's complaint
that his wife has "lost interest". Then the content of the illness can remain private and its *effect* must be
speakable: "there is something medical going on that she is dealing with, and it is relevant to what you are
describing; she will tell you when she is ready." That is not a lie and it is not a disclosure.

**Scenario three — the wished-for pregnancy.** A woman in a couple treatment discloses that she has stopped
her contraception without telling her husband. *Analysis:* the clearest kind of secrecy, because the treatment
is about the couple's reproductive decisions and the therapist would be participating in a deception about
their subject. It is also a case where the therapist's own views about reproductive autonomy will be
activated, and the professional requirement is to state the limit without moralising about the choice.

**Scenario four — the adult child's secret.** A mother in a family treatment discloses that her 24-year-old son
has been using drugs, and asks the therapist not to raise it with the son, who is also attending. *Analysis:*
the son is an adult and a client; his mother's disclosure is information about him that he has not given.
The therapist cannot promise the mother silence, because she is now conducting a family treatment in which she
knows something the son does not know she knows — and she cannot disclose it to him either, because the mother
told her. The workable resolution is the mother's own act: "I need you to tell him that you know, and I will
help you do it, because I cannot work with the two of you while he thinks this is not being spoken about."

**The pattern in all four.** The answer is rarely "tell" or "do not tell". It is *who tells, in what words,
by when, with the therapist doing what.*

### 3.4b The supervisor and the secret

A therapist holding a secret needs supervision and frequently avoids it, because the secret feels like a
failure and because describing it means saying it aloud. This is the mechanism by which secrets damage
therapists: the case that most needs discussing is the one least discussed.

**The rule.** Any case in which the therapist is holding information from a client — even legitimately, even
temporarily — is presented in supervision within the month.

**What supervision is for, in these cases.** Not permission, which supervision cannot give. Three things:
(1) a check on whether the therapist's reasoning is clinical or personal, since the wish to protect a client
from the consequences of disclosure is often the therapist's own; (2) an outside view on the risk analysis,
which deteriorates under pressure; (3) the documentation, because a supervisor who has read the reasoning
becomes the strongest evidence that the decision was considered.

**What the supervisor should not do.** Instruct the therapist to disclose. The decision and the responsibility
belong to the treating clinician, and a supervisor who directs it removes the judgement from the person who
will have to defend it.

**The audit question for the therapist.** At the end of each month: is there anything I know that one of my
clients does not know I know? If the answer is yes and there is no written plan with a date, the case needs
supervision this week, not next.

### 3.5 Case: two years, no intention

**Referral.** Mrs Nkiru Adeleke, 36, a bank branch operations manager, telephones about her marriage. "We have
not spoken properly in three years. I want to know whether it can be saved." Her husband, Segun, 39, a
pharmacist, attends the first session reluctantly: "she decided we needed this, so here we are."

**The household.** Nkiru and Segun, married eleven years. Two children, 8 and 5. Segun's mother lives ten
minutes away and is "very involved". Nkiru travels for work two or three nights a month.

**The first session.** The presenting complaint is "we are like flatmates". Segun's example: "she is on her
phone until midnight". Nkiru's example: "he asks me about the children and never about my day." There is no
violence, no substance misuse, no financial crisis. The therapist states the modified no-secrets policy, in the
four parts, in the first session, and both agree. The note records it.

**Session two — the individual request.** Nkiru telephones: "I need to see you alone." The therapist offers the
next appointment, and sees her for forty minutes. In the first eight minutes, Nkiru says: "I have been seeing
someone for two years. He is a colleague. I am not going to stop and I am not going to tell Segun. I want to
know whether I can save my marriage at the same time."

**The application of the six questions.**

**1. Who is the client?** The couple. The policy was stated to both and agreed by both. Nkiru was told, in
Segun's presence, that a secret affecting the relationship would not be kept.

**2. Is anyone at risk?** No physical risk. There is a risk of an STI to Segun, which is a health risk and not
a safety risk, and it is relevant — a therapist who treats this as a purely relational matter has missed
something. There is a risk to the children's stability, which is a future harm rather than a current danger.

**3. Privacy or secrecy?** Secrecy, unambiguously. The test — does knowing this change what I do in the joint
sessions? — is answered in one word: yes. Every question about the weekends, about her travel, about her
withdrawal becomes unaskable.

**4. Whose information is it?** Nkiru's, Segun's, the children's, and the third party's — who has a wife of his
own, which Nkiru mentions in passing and which the therapist does not pursue. The information is not one
person's property.

**5. What happens if it moves, and when?** The plan has to be made now, in this session, or the policy is
decoration.

**6. What is my part?** To insist that it moves, to help plan how, to be present, and not to disclose it.

**The intervention, in the session, in sequence.**

**First, the reminder, not as a threat but as a fact.** "When we started, I told you both that I would not keep
a secret that affects the marriage. I meant it then and I mean it now. So I am going to say what I can and
cannot do, and then we will decide what happens."

**Second, the question that establishes the delay.** "When did you decide that you were not going to tell him?"
The answer is "about a year ago". The therapist names it: "so for a year you have known, and I have known for
ten minutes. That is the difference I have to work with."

**Third, the refusal to collude, stated as a limit on the therapist rather than on the client.** "I am not able
to run a couple therapy in which I know something of this size and your husband does not. Not because of a
rule — because I would be lying to him every week, and he would be paying me to do it."

**Fourth, the three options, laid out plainly.** (a) She tells Segun, with the therapist's help, in a session,
by a stated date. (b) She ends the couple therapy here, and the therapist explains to Segun that the couple
work cannot continue — without giving a reason — which is honest and leaves Segun with a mystery. (c) The
therapist tells Segun that something has been disclosed which he needs to discuss with his wife, and Nkiru
tells him the content herself. Option (b) is included deliberately: a client who is not ready must have an exit
that is not a lie.

**Fifth, the deadline and the recording.** Nkiru chooses (a), with a date three weeks out and one session in
between to plan the words. The therapist writes it in the note: "client disclosed an ongoing relationship of
two years; reminded of the policy agreed at session one; client agreed to disclose to her husband by [date],
with therapist support; if not disclosed by that date, therapist will inform husband that a disclosure has been
made and that he should ask his wife."

**Sixth, the health question, which is separate and cannot wait three weeks.** "There is one thing I am going
to ask you to do this week, and it is not about your marriage. Get a sexual health check, and tell me you have
done it. If you will not tell him, you still cannot leave him exposed." Nkiru agrees, and does it, and reports
at the next session.

**The disclosure session, in outline.** Nkiru tells Segun in the fourth session, in the therapist's room, in
eleven sentences that she had rehearsed with the therapist in the third. Segun's response is not what anyone
predicted: he is not surprised. He says, "I knew something. I thought it was my fault." The session that
follows is not about the affair; it is about a three-year silence in which both of them were protecting the
other from a truth they each half-knew.

**What the case teaches.**

1. **The policy must be stated before it is needed.** Nkiru's decision to disclose was made in a session in
   which the alternative was clearly worse — ending the therapy — and that alternative existed only because
   the policy had been agreed in session one.
2. **A deadline converts a secret into a task.** Without a date, the therapist would have carried it for
   months, and the therapy would have become the deception the policy exists to prevent.
3. **The health question is not part of the confidentiality decision and must not be lost in it.** A therapist
   absorbed in the ethics of disclosure can forget that a third party is being exposed to an infection.
4. **The predicted catastrophe is frequently not the actual one.** The disclosure did not end the marriage; it
   ended a silence. That outcome is not guaranteed and it is common, and a therapist who assumes the worst will
   delay the disclosure in order to protect a relationship that is already being damaged by the delay.

### 3.5a When the client refuses the deadline

A real and frequent outcome, and the case in which the therapist's policy is actually tested.

**What refusal looks like.** "I am not going to tell him, and I am not going to stop seeing you. You said you
would help me, and now you are threatening me." The accusation of betrayal is genuine and it is also the
pressure that most therapists find hardest to withstand.

**What the therapist says.** Four sentences, in this order. "I am not threatening you; I am telling you what I
can and cannot do, which is the same thing I told you on the first day." "I will not tell him what you have
told me — that has not changed." "What I will do, on the date we agreed, is tell him that something has been
disclosed that he needs to raise with you." "And I will keep working with you, either way, because you are not
being abandoned for telling me the truth."

**Why the fourth sentence matters more than the other three.** The client's fear is that honesty will cost her
the therapist. If it does, the lesson she takes is that honesty is dangerous — which is precisely the lesson
that produced the affair's secrecy in the first place.

**What if the client ends the therapy instead?** Then the therapist writes to confirm the ending, offers to
meet the husband to explain that the couple work has stopped without explaining why, and records the reasoning
in full. The letter matters: it is the evidence that the decision was the client's, and it is the only document
that will exist if the husband later complains.

**The rule underneath all of this.** The therapist's obligation is not to the secret and not to the marriage.
It is to the honesty of the treatment she is conducting. A treatment she cannot conduct honestly is one she
must not continue, and saying so is the professional act.

**What the transcript shows.** Three things that a policy document cannot teach. First, the therapist never
moralises about the affair, which keeps the client in the room. Second, she refuses twice with the same
sentence, and the repetition matters — a limit stated once is heard as an opening position. Third, she ends
the session on the client's own deadline, which means the client leaves with an obligation she set rather than
one imposed.

### 3.7 Laboratory: the secrets protocol

**Aim.** To design, in writing, a confidentiality and secrets protocol for a named service, and to defend its
four parts under pressure from a client who is testing it.

**Procedure.** Groups of three: therapist, client, observer. Each group is given a service description (a
school counselling service; a private couple practice; a church-based clinic; a hospital family therapy unit)
and must produce a one-page protocol in thirty minutes. The client role is then briefed to test the protocol
in three ways — by asking for an individual session before the policy has been stated, by disclosing a secret
and refusing the deadline, and by asking the therapist to lie to a third party. Twenty minutes of role-play.
The observer marks the therapist's adherence to the protocol *the group itself wrote*, which is the point of
the exercise: most protocols are unwritable as written.

**Marking rubric (20 marks).**

| Criterion | 0–1 | 2–3 | 4–5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The four parts** | Two or fewer present | Three present | All four, specific, with the action the therapist will take at each point |
| **Privacy against secrecy** | Both treated the same | Distinguished, but the test is not stated | Distinguished with the operational test, and examples from practice |
| **The institutions** | Not addressed | Named | Mapped, with what each party may be told and who decides |
| **Under pressure** | Abandoned the protocol when tested | Held it, but argued | Held it, stated the limit as a limit on the therapist, and offered the client control of the manner |
| **Documentation** | None | A note exists | The note records what was said, the agreement, the date, and the contingency |

**The distinction that carries most of the marks.** The top band requires the student to hold the protocol
*without moralising*. Groups routinely win the argument and lose the client: a therapist who says "you are
being dishonest" has stated a true thing and has ended the possibility of the disclosure.

### 3.8 Six errors

**Error 1 — Stating the policy after the secret.** The policy is worth nothing once the secret exists, because
the client reasonably hears a change of rules. It is stated in session one, in front of everyone, and recorded.

**Error 2 — Confusing privacy with secrecy and demanding total transparency.** A policy that requires the
disclosure of everything produces a family who disclose nothing. The two categories must be distinguished in
the explanation, with examples from ordinary life.

**Error 3 — Carrying the secret "for now".** The phrase that has ended more couple therapies than any single
act. There is no "for now"; there is a date, or there is a lie.

**Error 4 — Telling the client's secret to protect the client from the consequences.** The therapist who
telephones the husband "for her own good" has broken a confidence and taken away the only act that could have
repaired the relationship.

**Error 5 — Refusing the individual session altogether.** A therapist who will not see one partner alone
cannot assess risk, cannot prepare a disclosure, and cannot hear the thing that is most true. The individual
session is not the danger; the unstated one is.

**Error 6 — Forgetting the third parties inside the secret.** An affair involves another household; a
diagnosis involves a family history; a plan to leave involves children. A confidentiality analysis that
counts only the people in the room has missed most of the people in the case.

### 3.9 Culture, faith and the multiperson system

**Confidentiality is a Western construct in some of these households.** The idea that an individual holds
information that a spouse has no right to is not universally shared, and in many of the families this book is
written for the operative assumption is that a married couple has one information economy. A therapist who
announces an individual confidentiality without explanation will be heard as introducing a foreign and
suspicious practice — "why would you need to speak to my wife alone?"

**The translation that works.** Two sentences. First, the practical one: "I see each of you alone once, because
there are things people cannot say in front of a husband or a wife, and I need to know them to help." Second,
the limit: "anything that affects the marriage, I will not keep." The family that understands *why* the
individual session exists agrees to it far more often than one that has been told only that it is a right.

**The elder and the family's information.** Where a grandmother or an uncle holds authority in the household,
they may expect to be told what happens in sessions, and their expectation may be shared by the family. The
workable position is not refusal but specification: "I will tell you what I am doing and whether it is
working. I will not tell you what is said in the room." Most elders accept this, because it gives them the
thing they actually want, which is to know that something is being done.

**Faith communities and the circulation of information.** A pastor who refers a family will frequently expect
a report, and the family may expect the pastor to receive one. Both expectations must be addressed in the
first session, and the answer recorded. Where a religious authority is part of the family's support, the
therapist may offer a summary *with the family's written agreement*, which is a legitimate arrangement and is
almost never offered.

**Shame and the disclosure of family business.** In a shame-oriented community, a disclosure inside the family
may become a disclosure in the community, and the family knows this better than the therapist does. That is a
reason to plan the disclosure carefully and is not a reason to avoid it — and it is a reason to ask, before
any disclosure is planned, who outside the household will come to know.

> **DEBATE** — "In couple therapy, the therapist should never hold information from either partner." Argue
> for: the client is the couple, deception is incompatible with therapy, and a therapist who holds a secret is
> no longer working with a couple. Argue against: privacy is a condition of individual disclosure, a strict
> no-secrets rule drives the most important material underground, and a woman who cannot speak freely about her
> own fear will not disclose it at all. Then state the policy you would actually operate, and the case that
> would make you change it.

### 3.9a A record of the decision, written out

The note is the therapist's only defence and the family's only protection. It is written the same day, and it
does four things: records the policy, records the disclosure, records the reasoning, and records the plan. An
example, adapted from the Adeleke case.

> **Session 4 (individual, 40 min).** Client N.A. disclosed an ongoing relationship of approximately two years
> with a colleague. Client states she does not intend to end it and does not intend to tell her husband.
>
> **Policy.** Modified no-secrets policy stated to both partners in session 1 and agreed by both; copy in file.
> Policy restated to client at the start of this session. Client acknowledged it.
>
> **Reasoning.** Client system is the couple. Disclosure affects the couple treatment and would require the
> therapist to avoid material relevant to the presenting complaint; not compatible with continued couple work.
> No current risk of violence identified. Risk of STI transmission to the husband noted and addressed
> separately (below). No statutory duty engaged. Third parties: the other party's spouse is not a client and
> has no claim on the file.
>
> **Plan.** Client elected to disclose to her husband in session 5, with therapist support. Rehearsal agreed
> for session 5 opening. If disclosure has not occurred by [date], therapist will inform the husband that a
> disclosure has been made and that he should raise it with his wife, having first told the client that she
> will do so. Client informed of this contingency and stated she understood it.
>
> **Health.** Therapist advised a sexual health screen this week, independent of the disclosure plan. Client
> agreed. To be confirmed at session 5.
>
> **Supervision.** Case to be presented at next supervision, [date].
>
> **Signed.** [Therapist], [date], [time].

**Why each part exists.** The *policy* paragraph establishes that the client was told before she spoke, which
is the difference between a trap and an agreement. The *reasoning* paragraph shows that the decision was
clinical rather than moral, which is what a regulator reads for. The *plan* paragraph, with the date, is what
makes the note a record of action rather than a record of concern. The *health* paragraph prevents the most
common omission in these cases. And the *supervision* line is the strongest single sentence in the note,
because it shows the therapist did not decide alone.

**The version not to write.** "Client disclosed an affair; discussed confidentiality." That note has no
reasoning, no date, no plan, and no policy — and if the husband complains in a year, it establishes only that
the therapist knew.

### 3.10 Evidence note

**What the research supports.** Survey and vignette studies consistently find that couple and family therapists
report operating modified no-secrets policies and consider them more workable than either rigid alternative,
while acknowledging that the policy is difficult to enact under pressure. The clinical literature on
"therapeutic secrets" is largely theoretical, but it converges on the observation that a secret held by the
therapist changes the therapist's behaviour — avoidance of topics, reduced spontaneity, and a measurable drop in
the couple's engagement — which is consistent with the triadic model of the secret as a third participant.

**What is weaker.** There is almost no empirical work on outcomes. No trial has compared no-secrets, full
secrecy and negotiated policies, and the question may be unanswerable by trial design because the policy is
chosen partly by the population: a practice serving couples in high-conflict or coercive relationships will
reasonably operate differently from one serving voluntary, low-risk couples.

**What is not supported.** That a no-secrets policy is the standard of care in any enforceable sense — the codes
do not require it. That holding a secret is always harmful — some privacy is protective, particularly in cases
involving past abuse or a partner's history of violence. And that the policy should be applied identically
across cultures, which the cross-cultural literature on family information economies does not support.

**The matrix cell.** *Development is probabilistic and non-linear; the same input produces different outcomes
depending on timing and context.* The same secret, disclosed in session two with a therapist who has stated the
policy and has a supervisor, and disclosed in session twenty with a therapist who has never mentioned it,
produces two different treatments and two different families.

### 3.11 Key terms

**Confidentiality** — the professional duty not to disclose information given in the treatment relationship,
subject to specified exceptions.

**Privacy** — information a person legitimately chooses not to share; compatible with treatment.

**Therapeutic secret** — information held from one member of a couple or family that materially affects the
treatment.

**Modified no-secrets policy** — a stated policy that individual disclosures will not be kept where they affect
the couple or family work, with a process for their disclosure.

**Client system** — the person or persons agreed to be the client; the holder of the confidentiality.

**Third-party claim** — an institution's or individual's claim on information, legal or social.

**Information map** — a drawn record of who holds what information about a family, and who decides.

**Deadline** — the dated commitment that converts a secret into a task.

**Collusion** — an arrangement in which the therapist's silence serves the client's avoidance.

**Duty to warn / protect** — the legal or ethical obligation to act on a serious threat to an identifiable
third party; jurisdiction-dependent.

### 3.12 Self-test

1. Why does individual confidentiality not transfer to couple therapy?
2. State the four parts of the modified no-secrets policy, and the failure each prevents.
3. Give the operational test that distinguishes privacy from secrecy.
4. What is the difference between telling a partner that something has been said and telling them what?
5. In the Adeleke case, why was the health question separated from the confidentiality decision?
6. What would you do if the client refused the deadline?
7. Name five institutions with claims on a family's information, and what each may legitimately be told.
8. Why is "carrying the secret for now" the most damaging option?

**Answers.** *(1)* Because the client is the couple jointly, so neither partner can demand confidentiality
against the other, and a therapist holding information from one member is deceiving a client on another's
behalf. *(2)* The promise of individual sessions (prevents the request being experienced as a rupture); the
limit stated in terms of effect (prevents accidental secrecy); the commitment to tell both that something has
been said (prevents the guessing game); the deadline (prevents a stable dishonesty). *(3)* Does knowing this
change what I do in the joint sessions? If yes, it is secrecy. *(4)* Telling *that* preserves the discloser's
control of the content and the timing, and it converts the therapist's position from deception to pressure;
telling *what* removes the client's only remaining act of repair. *(5)* Because the exposure of a third party
to infection is a health matter with its own urgency, and a therapist absorbed in the ethics of disclosure can
lose it. *(6)* Hold the limit, do not disclose the content, state the date, inform the partner on the date that
a disclosure has been made, and keep working with the client so that honesty does not cost her the therapist.
*(7)* The school (attendance and functioning, not therapy content); the court (whatever is compelled); the
insurer or employer (usually attendance only); child protection (what the statute requires); the religious body
(a summary with written agreement, or nothing); and the extended family, who have no claim and considerable
social power. *(8)* Because there is no "for now": the secret is either moving toward disclosure or it is
being carried, and a carried secret changes every question the therapist can ask.

## Week 4 — Secrets I: the individual disclosure

**Classify before you respond.** Three questions: *Is anyone at risk? Does this information change the
treatment I am providing? Is the client able to disclose it themselves, with support?*

| Type of secret | Typical response |
|---|---|
| Harmless (a surprise party) | Keep it; it is not clinically material |
| Clinically material, no risk (an affair; a debt; a diagnosis) | Negotiate disclosure with a deadline; do not collude |
| Risk to a third party (HIV status undisclosed; threats) | Assess; follow legal and public-health obligations; document |
| Risk to a child or vulnerable adult | Report; inform; protect |
| Risk to self | Risk assessment; safety planning; break confidentiality as required |

**The clinical skill: refusing to be recruited.** "I understand you are not ready to tell him. I cannot
keep this and also work on your marriage with you, because I would be working with half the truth. Let
us plan how you will tell him, and when."

**The systemic reality.** Secrets are not only information; they are structure. A secret held by one
partner and the therapist creates a coalition — and the excluded partner often senses it, which damages
the therapy's credibility.

## Week 5 — Secrets II: infidelity, paternity, HIV status

Three cases that appear in every couple therapist's career, each with a different legal overlay.

**Infidelity.** Not a reportable act. The clinical tasks: stop the bleeding, contain the disclosure,
assess for sexually transmitted infection risk, address the meaning of the affair (not only its fact), and
decide with the couple whether disclosure to a third party (the affair partner's spouse) is required.
That last question is a values question with safety implications; consult.

**Paternity.** A man may discover that a child he is raising is not biologically his. The therapist must
not conceal information that affects a child's welfare, must not give legal advice, and must help the
family decide who needs to know and in what sequence. Refer for legal advice on paternity, maintenance and
custody; the emotional work is yours.

**HIV and other communicable conditions.** Confidentiality is strong, but public-health law may create
duties where a partner is at identifiable risk and the client refuses disclosure. Know your jurisdiction's
rule, consult, and document. Never disclose without advice.

> **DEBATE** — "A therapist who keeps an affair secret for six months is colluding in the harm the affair
> causes." Argue for and against, then write your own policy in 200 words.

## Week 6 — Boundaries and multiple relationships

**Definition.** A multiple relationship exists when you have another significant relationship with a
client or someone closely associated with them. Not all are unethical; all require examination.

**The test.** (1) Could the second relationship impair your objectivity? (2) Could it exploit the power
difference? (3) Would the client experience it as exploitative later? (4) Is it avoidable?

**Common in small communities.**

| Situation | Risk | Defensible approach |
|---|---|---|
| Seeing a neighbour's family | Overhearing information; social contact | Discuss openly; agree how to behave at community events |
| Client is a fellow church member | Role confusion; spiritual authority | Clarify roles; consider referral if you hold a leadership role |
| A relative of your child's classmate | Dual loyalty | Disclose in supervision; transfer if the risk is unmanageable |
| Client asks you to be a character witness | Role change; advocacy | Decline; explain; refer to a professional who can assess |
| Gift-giving after a bereavement | Cultural obligation vs boundary | Accept small cultural gifts; document; discuss meaning |
| Trading therapy for services | Exploitation risk | Usually decline; if unavoidable, document value and rationale |

**Touch, self-disclosure, and home visits.** Each is sometimes therapeutic and always requires a rationale
you can articulate in supervision. The test is never "did it feel right?" but "whose need did it serve?"

> **PRACTICAL** — Complete a boundary audit of your present life: community roles, family ties, business
> interests, social media. Identify the three situations most likely to produce a dual relationship in the
> next two years, and pre-decide your response.

## Week 7 — Competence and referral

**Competence has four dimensions:** knowledge, skills, experience, and appropriateness (personal and
cultural fit). You can be knowledgeable and inappropriate: a therapist with unprocessed trauma working
intensively with trauma, or a young male therapist seeing a couple in a culture where his authority is
contested.

**The duty to refer** arises when: the problem is outside your training; you lack supervision for it; your
personal reactions would compromise care; or the client needs a different modality or medical input.

**How to refer well.** Name the reason without shaming ("this needs someone with specific training in X");
make a warm handover (a call, not just a name); offer a bridge session if needed; follow up once; and
document. A referral is not abandonment.

> **SELF-AUDIT** — List the ten presenting problems you are most likely to see. For each, rate your
> competence 1–5 and name the supervisor or service you would refer to. The two lowest ratings are your
> professional development plan for this year.

## Week 8 — Risk I: suicide and self-harm

**The standard.** Ask directly, ask specifically, ask about means, and document. There is no evidence that
asking about suicide increases risk, and substantial evidence that clinicians fail to ask.

**A defensible risk assessment includes:**

1. **Ideation** — frequency, intensity, duration, controllability.
2. **Plan** — specific method, place, time.
3. **Intent** — "Do you intend to act on this?"
4. **Means** — access to firearms, medication, pesticides, heights.
5. **Previous attempts** — the strongest single predictor.
6. **Protective factors** — reasons for living, responsibility to children, faith, support.
7. **Substance use and impulsivity.**
8. **Family history.**
9. **Current stressors and recent losses.**
10. **Disclosure and support network** — who can be told, who can watch.

**Documentation.** Record the questions you asked and the answers, your formulation of risk (low/moderate/
high), the plan, who was informed, and the review date. If you decide *not* to break confidentiality,
record why.

**In family work.** A parent's suicidality, an adolescent's self-harm and a child's disclosure must be
handled without turning the family session into an interrogation. Structure: see the person individually
first, agree what will be shared, then facilitate the sharing.

> **PRACTICAL (OBSERVED)** — Fifteen-minute suicide risk assessment role-play with a trained actor. You must
> ask all ten elements, formulate risk, and agree a safety plan. Pass/fail.

## Week 9 — Risk II: intimate partner violence and child protection

**Distinguish two patterns.**

| Situational couple violence | Coercive control |
|---|---|
| Mutual, low-level, escalates under stress | One partner systematically dominates |
| Fear is situational | Fear is chronic; surveillance, isolation, threats |
| Couple therapy may be appropriate after assessment | Couple therapy is contraindicated; risk increases |
| Safety planning individual | Specialist advocacy and protection |

**Screening.** Screen each partner **separately**, at least once, using direct questions: "Has your
partner ever hit, kicked, or pushed you? Has he/she ever made you afraid? Do you feel safe going home
today?" Never screen in front of the partner.

**Child protection.** Know your jurisdiction's reporting duty. In Nigeria, the Child Rights Act (2003,
adopted in many but not all states) and state child protection laws create duties and structures; the
practical route is usually the state ministry responsible for social welfare, the police family unit, or a
designated safeguarding lead. Document the concern, the advice received, and the action taken.

> **CAUTION** — Conjoint sessions can escalate violence after the session. If there is any indication of
> coercive control, separate sessions and a safety plan precede any conjoint work. Write it down.

## Week 10 — The legal framework a clinician must know

This section is **orientation, not legal advice**. Statutes and state practice vary; verify with a lawyer.

![Figure 10.1 — Marriage systems and dissolution questions in Nigeria](../assets/figures/fig39-family-law-map.svg)

**Marriage in Nigeria exists in four overlapping forms:** statutory (Marriage Act), customary, Islamic,
and cohabitation without legal marriage. A family may be married under more than one system.

**Dissolution.** Statutory divorce requires a court petition (Matrimonial Causes Act, with the
two-year rule and recognised grounds); customary and Islamic dissolutions follow their own processes and
courts. The therapist's role is not to advise on grounds but to help the family clarify what they are
deciding and to refer.

**Children.** Custody and access decisions are governed by the welfare principle — the child's best
interests. Maintenance obligations exist under both statutory and customary law. The therapist may be
asked for a report; a treating therapist should generally not conduct a forensic custody evaluation of
their own client (role conflict).

**Other relevant areas:** the Child Rights Act; the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act 2015
(applicable in the FCT and in states that have adopted it); mental health legislation in the states that
have adopted the Mental Health Act 2021 framework; data protection obligations regarding client records.

## Week 11 — Records, subpoenas and court

**Write notes for three readers:** your future self, a colleague taking over, and a court. Use factual,
behavioural language; avoid speculation and avoid character judgements. Record: date, attendees, risk
screen, interventions, plan, and who was told what.

**If a subpoena or court order arrives:** do not ignore it; do not respond immediately without advice;
verify its validity; contact your supervisor, indemnity provider or lawyer; inform the client (unless
prohibited); release only what is required, with a privilege log if advised.

**Testifying.** Your role is to report what you observed, not to advocate for a preferred outcome. Answer
the question asked; do not volunteer; do not guess; say "I don't know" when you do not. Distinguish
*fact*, *clinical opinion* and *hypothesis*.

> **PRACTICAL** — Note-writing lab. Write a progress note for a session in which a risk was disclosed and
> a report to a school was agreed. Exchange; grade each other against the "three readers" standard.

## Week 12 — Money, gifts and the business of therapy

**Fees are clinical material.** They carry meaning about worth, dependency, entitlement and shame. Have a
written policy; state it at the start; apply it consistently; discuss non-payment rather than avoiding it.

**Refusing to reduce a fee sometimes ends therapy; never reducing a fee sometimes ends therapy.** Both are
true. Decide your sliding scale in advance and document the reason for each exception.

**Gifts.** Small cultural gifts may be accepted with documentation; expensive gifts should be discussed;
gifts with strings should be declined. Ask, "What would it mean to you if I accepted / declined?"

## Week 13 — Ethics of research, assessment and diagnosis

- **Informed consent** for research must be voluntary, capacity-appropriate and revocable; families,
  children and people in crisis require special care.
- **Deception** requires justification, debriefing, and ethics approval.
- **Diagnosis** carries consequences: access to care, stigma, insurance, custody. Diagnose what you can
  defend and describe what you cannot.
- **Assessment instruments** must be validated for the population and language; using an unvalidated
  instrument and acting on it is an ethical problem, not just a methodological one.

## Week 14 — Integration: the decision model and your stance

![Figure 14.1 — A seven-step decision-making model for ethical dilemmas](../assets/figures/fig33-ethics-decision-model.svg)

**The seven steps.** Identify the dilemma → check codes and law → consult → generate at least three
options including the uncomfortable one → weigh consequences for each affected party → decide, act and
document → evaluate afterwards.

**The documentation habit.** A defensible decision is not the same as a correct decision; it is one you can
explain. Write the reasoning at the time, not after a complaint.

**Your stance.** Write 500 words: what you will never do; what you will always consult about; and where your
personal values are most likely to distort your clinical judgement.

## Glossary

**Autonomy** — the client's right to self-determination.
**Beneficence / non-maleficence** — the duty to benefit and the duty not to harm.
**Coercive control** — a pattern of domination, isolation and threat, distinct from situational violence.
**Confidentiality** — the duty not to disclose information learned in a professional relationship.
**Dual relationship** — a second significant relationship with a client or someone close to them.
**Duty to warn / protect** — a legal obligation, in some jurisdictions, to act on a serious threat.
**Informed consent** — voluntary agreement based on adequate information and capacity.
**Mandated reporting** — a legal duty to report suspected abuse or neglect.
**Privilege** — the client's legal right to prevent disclosure in court proceedings.
**Subpoena** — a legal order requiring documents or testimony.
**Welfare principle** — the rule that a child's best interests govern decisions about them.
