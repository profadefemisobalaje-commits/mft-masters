# MFT 710 — Couples Therapy

> **MSc Marriage and Family Therapy** · Semester Two · 1.71 Units · 16 teaching weeks · Prerequisite: MFT 702, 706

**Course leader's summary.** Couple work is the largest single referral stream in most family therapy
practices and the most demanding therapeutically: two people, both of whom want you to agree with them. This
course integrates attachment-based (EFT), behavioural (TBCT/IBCT) and systemic approaches, and takes
infidelity, violence, separation and cultural context seriously.

---

## How to use this book

Couple work is where the therapist's own relationship history is most easily activated. The reflective tasks
in this book are not optional extras; they are the difference between a clinician who can hold two
perspectives at once and one who quietly takes sides. Do them.

---

## 1. Course description

The course covers: assessment of the couple (including individual sessions and violence screening), the major
evidence-based models (EFT and behavioural couple therapy), the Gottman research programme, communication
training, infidelity and betrayal trauma, sexual problems, separation and divorce work, and cultural and
religious context.

Three frameworks are integrated: **attachment** (why the bond matters), **behaviour** (what maintains the
pattern), and **system** (how the pattern organises itself).

## Week 1 — What couples want, and what therapy offers

Couples arrive with a *presenting complaint* (money, sex, in-laws, phone) and an *underlying bond question*
("are you there for me?"). The complaint is the doorway; the bond is the room.

**The evidence, briefly.** Couple therapy is effective for relationship distress with moderate-to-large
effects. Behavioural couple therapy, cognitive-behavioural couple therapy, and emotionally focused therapy
have the strongest evidence base. Emotionally focused therapy has the most consistent evidence for
durability, including in high-distress couples.

**What predicts a good outcome.** Early change, motivation of both partners, shared definition of the
problem, low coercive control, and — consistently — the quality of the *shared* therapeutic alliance.

**What predicts failure.** Coercive control, undisclosed affairs that surface mid-treatment, untreated
addiction, severe individual psychopathology, and one partner who has already decided to leave but has not
said so.

> **REFLECT** — What did your own family teach you about conflict, repair, and what a marriage is *for*?
> Write 400 words. Keep it; you will return to it in Week 13.

## Week 2 — Assessment I

**Structure of a couple assessment (2–3 sessions).**

1. **Joint session:** both partners' accounts, the history of the relationship, the current pattern, and the
   goals.
2. **Individual sessions:** one with each partner — risk screen, secrets policy, individual history,
   ambivalence, and "is there anything you would not say in front of your partner?"
3. **Feedback session:** formulation, plan, and the agreement.

**What to assess.**

| Domain | Questions |
|---|---|
| Commitment | "Is there any part of you that has already decided to leave?" |
| History | How did you meet? What drew you? What was good in the early years? |
| Current pattern | "Walk me through last Tuesday's argument, minute by minute." |
| Attempted solutions | What have you tried? What happened? |
| Family of origin | Genogram of both families; how each family handled conflict, affection, money |
| Context | Work, money, migration, in-laws, children, faith, health |
| Strengths | What still works? What do you still enjoy? |
| Risk | Violence, coercive control, suicide, substance use, child safety |

**Ask about the good.** Couples in distress systematically under-report positives; a careful strengths
inventory is both assessment and intervention.

## Week 3 — Individual sessions, IPV screening and secrets

**Always see each partner alone at least once.** This is where safety is disclosed.

**Screening questions (ask directly, in private).**

- "Have you ever felt afraid of your partner?"
- "Has your partner ever hit, pushed, slapped, or thrown something at you?"
- "Has your partner ever forced or pressured you sexually?"
- "Does your partner control your money, your phone, or who you see?"
- "If you went home tonight and told him you had said all this, what would happen?"

**If coercive control is present:** stop conjoint work; safety-plan individually; refer to specialist
services; document. Conjoint work can increase danger and can silence the victim in the room.

**Secrets policy.** State it in the first joint session: *"If one of you tells me something individually
that affects our work together, I will not keep it as a secret. I will help you decide how and when to tell
your partner, and if you cannot, I will ask us all to talk about it."*

**Ambivalence.** One partner may already be planning to leave. Ask: "Where are you on a scale from 'I am
fully committed to working on this' to 'I have already decided'?" The answer changes the treatment.

## Week 4 — The negative cycle

![Figure 4.1 — Circular causality: the pursue–withdraw loop](../assets/figures/fig02-circular-causality.svg)

**The three common cycles.**

1. **Pursue–withdraw.** One escalates to get a response; the other shuts down to keep the peace. The
   pursuing increases, the withdrawal deepens.
2. **Attack–defend.** Both escalate; complaint is met with counter-complaint; the volume rises and the
   content becomes irrelevant.
3. **Mutual withdrawal.** Both avoid; the relationship becomes parallel and polite; loneliness without
   conflict — often the hardest to treat because there is no presenting crisis.

**Mapping the cycle in session.** Ask each partner for the *last three arguments* and write the sequence on
paper as a loop. Then read it back and ask: "Is this recognisable?" The moment of recognition — often
accompanied by a laugh or a sigh — is the therapeutic turn.

**The reframe.** "The problem is not that you are angry, or that you are cold. The problem is the loop
between you, and the loop has a life of its own."

> **PRACTICAL** — With a role-played couple, map the cycle on a whiteboard in 15 minutes and get both
> partners to agree with the drawing. The agreement, not the drawing, is the skill.

## Week 5 — Attachment applied to couples

![Figure 5.1 — Adult attachment: two dimensions and four patterns](../assets/figures/fig17-attachment-patterns.svg)

**Core idea.** Adult romantic love is an attachment process. Partners monitor accessibility and
responsiveness; when the bond feels threatened, protest behaviours appear (criticism, pursuit, withdrawal,
numbing), and those protest behaviours are usually the *opposite* of the need they express.

**Two dimensions.** Anxiety (about abandonment) and avoidance (of intimacy). Their combination produces four
patterns, each with a characteristic strategy and core belief (see figure).

**Clinical use.**

- Anxious partner: the protest is a request for reassurance. Help them ask for it directly rather than
  through criticism.
- Avoidant partner: withdrawal is a protection against overwhelm and shame. Help them stay present for
  longer and return rather than disappear.
- Both: the cycle is an *attachment* problem, not a character problem.

**The attachment question to hold in your head.** *"What is this person asking for, and what are they doing
instead?"*

## Week 6 — EFT I: tracking and de-escalation

![Figure 6.1 — The EFT cycle: secondary emotion and primary attachment need](../assets/figures/fig12-eft-cycle.svg)

**Stage 1 (de-escalation, sessions 1–8).** The goal is that both partners can describe the cycle and see it
as the enemy.

**Four moves.**

1. **Track the cycle** in specific incidents. Slow the film; ask for bodily experience.
2. **Access primary emotion.** "Under the anger — is there something like fear?" Name it tentatively; let
   them correct you.
3. **Reframe the problem as the cycle.** "Neither of you is the problem. This dance is."
4. **Name the attachment cry.** "So when you say 'we never talk', what you mean is 'I miss you and I am
   afraid you have gone'."

**A worked enactment (Week 6 lab).**

> **Therapist (to Aisha):** When you get loud, what happens inside your body?
> **Aisha:** My chest. Like I cannot breathe until he answers.
> **Therapist:** And if you did not get loud — what would he see?
> **Aisha:** (pause) That I am frightened.
> **Therapist:** Can you tell him that? Look at him. "I get loud because…"
> **Aisha:** I get loud because I think you have already left.
> **Therapist (to Sam):** Sam, stay with that. What is happening for you hearing it?

**Hazard.** Primary emotion must be *reached*, not *demanded*. If you push a client to feel something in
front of you before safety, you will get a performance or a shutdown.

## Week 7 — Observed couple assessment

Forty minutes: contract, both accounts, cycle map, risk screen (in the individual sessions if scheduling
permits), strengths, and feedback. Assessed on safety, structure, balance (did each partner get equal
airtime?), and formulation quality.

---

## Week 8 — EFT II: restructuring

**Stage 2 (sessions 9–20).** Withdrawal re-engagement; pursuer softening; enactment of new responses.

**Softening event — the classic sequence.**

1. The pursuer accesses the primary attachment fear and expresses it non-accusatorially.
2. The withdrawer hears it, stays present, and responds with care.
3. The pursuer risks reaching again from a position of vulnerability rather than protest.
4. Both experience a new pattern in the room.

**Therapist tasks.** Keep the withdrawer engaged ("stay with me, this is the hard part"); block the
pursuer's escalation without silencing the need; and slow everything down.

**Stage 3 (consolidation).** Integrate the new pattern, problem-solve practical issues, and rehearse
recovery from relapse: "When the cycle comes back — and it will — what will you do in the first hour?"

## Week 9 — Behavioural couple therapy

**The behavioural view.** Distress is maintained by (a) a low rate of positive exchange, (b) poor
communication under conflict, and (c) attributions that make neutral behaviour hostile.

**Core interventions.**

1. **Behavioural exchange.** Each partner lists 15–20 small caring behaviours; select three each; do them
   daily without being asked. Not transactional bookkeeping — an increase in the density of positive
   behaviour.
2. **Communication training.** Speaker–listener with a floor card, then problemsolving.
3. **Attribution retraining.** "When he is late, what do you assume? What else could be true?"
4. **Acceptance work (IBCT).** Some differences will not change; the work is to soften the *emotional
   reactivity* to them through empathic joining and tolerance building.

![Figure 9.1 — The speaker–listener technique](../assets/figures/fig35-speaker-listener.svg)

**Sequencing.** Positives first (exchange), then communication, then problem-solving. Couples cannot solve
problems they cannot discuss.

## Week 10 — The Gottman research programme

![Figure 10.1 — The Sound Relationship House](../assets/figures/fig13-sound-relationship-house.svg)

**Findings worth using.**

- **The four horsemen** — criticism, contempt, defensiveness, stonewalling — predict dissolution
  (Figure 14). Contempt is the strongest predictor.
- **The 5:1 ratio** of positive to negative interaction during conflict.
- **Bids for connection** — small moments of "turning towards" build the emotional bank account.
- **Love maps** — detailed knowledge of the partner's inner world.
- **Repair attempts** — the capacity to de-escalate mid-conflict is more predictive than the absence of
  conflict.
- **Physiological flooding** — above roughly 100 bpm, people cannot listen; a break is a skill, not an
  escape.

![Figure 10.2 — The four horsemen and their antidotes](../assets/figures/fig14-four-horsemen.svg)

**Adaptation caution.** The norms come largely from US samples; treat the *structure* as a clinical map, not
as a universal law. A couple who argue loudly and repair well is not in trouble because of the volume.

## Week 11 — Infidelity and betrayal trauma

**Two clinical events, often confused.**

1. **The disclosure** — an acute crisis requiring containment, safety and structure.
2. **The betrayal trauma** — an injury to the betrayed partner's capacity to trust and to make sense of
   reality, with symptoms resembling PTSD (intrusion, hypervigilance, dissociation).

**Phase 1: stabilise (weeks 1–4).**

- Stop the affair (or the contact) — this is usually a precondition, not a negotiation.
- Establish safety: no violence, no coercion, agreed boundaries on phone and whereabouts.
- Contain the disclosure: the betrayed partner has a right to the facts, not to every detail; agree what
  will be asked and answered.
- Address STI testing and any financial/legal exposure.
- Individual sessions to assess each partner's capacity to continue.

**Phase 2: meaning (weeks 4–12).**

- What did the affair *do* for the unfaithful partner? (Not "why did you do it?" but "what was it in service
  of?")
- What was missing that could have been spoken? (Careful: explanation is not justification.)
- What does the betrayed partner need in order to consider rebuilding — transparency, remorse, time,
  consistency?

**Phase 3: rebuilding or separating.** Not every couple should stay together. Your task is to help them
decide with full information, not to preserve the marriage at all costs.

> **CAUTION** — Do not accept a couple for affair work if the unfaithful partner will not end the affair and
> will not disclose anything; you will be running a therapy that cannot progress and colluding in a lie.

## Week 12 — Sex and intimacy

**Start with permission, then information, then specific suggestions** (Annon's PLISSIT model).

**Assessment.** Desire, arousal, orgasm, pain; frequency and satisfaction for each partner; the last time it
was good; what changed; medical factors (medication, illness, menopause, postpartum); the couple's beliefs;
and the meaning of sex in the relationship.

**Common presentations and the couple-level formulation.**

| Presentation | Individual factor | Relational factor |
|---|---|---|
| Low desire in one partner | Fatigue, medication, depression, pain | Pursuit and pressure; unresolved resentment |
| Erectile difficulty | Vascular, hormonal, anxiety | Performance focus; fear of judgement |
| Painful intercourse | Medical cause must be excluded | Fear, poor communication, coercion |
| Mismatched desire | Cycles differ normally | Negotiation failure; rejection sensitivity |

**Intervention basics.** Psychoeducation (desire discrepancy is the most common sexual complaint, not a sign
of incompatibility); sensate focus (graded, non-goal-oriented touch); scheduling; reducing performance
pressure; addressing the relational context; and medical referral when indicated.

**Cultural and religious framing.** Sexual expectations are among the most culturally loaded topics you will
discuss. Ask what each partner was taught, what they believe is permitted, and whether they can say no to
each other. Consent within marriage is consent.

## Week 13 — Violence, control and contraindications

**The distinction that matters** (from MFT 704):

| Situational couple violence | Coercive control |
|---|---|
| Mutual, escalating under stress | One-directional domination |
| No fear outside conflict | Chronic fear, surveillance, isolation |
| Couple therapy possible after assessment | Couple therapy contraindicated |
| Safety plan for both | Specialist advocacy for the victim |

**If a couple presents with violence.** See each partner individually first. Ask about fear, injury, sexual
coercion, threats, and escalation. Do not use conjoint sessions to "work through" violence; do not ask the
victim to say in front of the perpetrator what he did, unless she chooses and it is safe.

**REFLECT** — Return to your Week 1 reflection. Where in this material are you most likely to lose balance
— with the victim, with the perpetrator, with the unfaithful partner, with the one who wants to leave?

## Week 14 — Separation, divorce and stepfamilies

**Discernment counselling.** Some couples are not deciding *whether* to repair the relationship but
*whether* to end it. A structured, time-limited process (about 4–6 sessions) helps a couple reach a clear
decision rather than drifting. Both outcomes — a renewed commitment or a respectful ending — are successes.

**When the decision is to separate.**

- Help the couple tell the children together, with a simple, blame-free message.
- Refer for legal advice on custody, maintenance, property and any customary or religious processes.
- Address co-parenting: the relationship continues as a parenting partnership.
- Manage the grief: divorce is a loss, even when it is a relief.

**Stepfamilies.** The central error is treating the stepfamily as a nuclear family that arrived late. It is a
different structure with different tasks: the couple bond must be built before stepparent authority is
imposed; children need permission to love both households; and the "insider/outsider" split (biological
parent and child versus stepparent) must be named.

---

## Week 15 — Culture, faith and family of origin

**Questions to ask every couple.**

- What did each of your families teach you about marriage, money, sex, and in-laws?
- Who has authority in your household, and who decided that?
- What does your faith require of each of you?
- Which of your families is closer, and what does that closeness cost the other?
- If your parents disagreed with a decision you made, what would happen?

**Common cultural configurations.** Bride price and its obligations; polygyny; co-residence with parents;
gender-role expectations; religious courts and counselling; migration and split households; inter-ethnic and
inter-faith marriages.

**The rule.** Do not treat the cultural pattern as the problem, and do not treat it as beyond discussion.
Ask the couple what *they* want within it.

---

## Week 16 — Consolidation and ending

Write the couple a one-page **relapse prevention plan**: their cycle in their own words, three early warning
signs, two repair moves that work for them, and the agreement about when to return. Review the gains
explicitly, name the risk of recurrence without frightening them, and end on time.

## Glossary

**Attachment injury** — a betrayal or abandonment that damages trust and requires explicit repair.
**Betrayal trauma** — trauma-like symptoms following the discovery of a partner's deception.
**Bids for connection** — small attempts to gain a partner's attention or affection.
**Coercive control** — a pattern of domination and isolation, distinct from situational conflict.
**Negative cycle** — the recurrent pattern of interaction that organises couple distress.
**Pursue–withdraw** — a cycle in which one partner escalates while the other distances.
**Softening** — in EFT, the pursuer's shift from protest to vulnerable expression.
**Sentiment override** — the tendency for the relationship's overall tone to colour the interpretation of
specific events.
**Sensate focus** — graded, non-goal-oriented touch exercises used in sex therapy.
