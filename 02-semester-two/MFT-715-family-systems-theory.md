# MFT 715 — Family Systems Theory

> **MSc Marriage and Family Therapy** · Semester Two · 1.71 Units · 15 teaching weeks · Prerequisite: MFT 702

**Course leader's summary.** This is the theoretical anchor of the programme. Where MFT 702 surveys schools,
this course interrogates the *theory* beneath them: cybernetics, general systems thinking, structure,
communication, constructionism and their critics. You will finish able to explain precisely what you mean
when you say a family is "a system".

---

## How to use this book

The word "system" is used loosely in clinical settings. This course tightens it. Each week takes one
theoretical tradition, states its claims precisely, applies it to a case, and then asks what it cannot
explain. You are expected to leave with arguments, not slogans.

---

## 1. Course description

Family systems theory is not one theory but a family of theories sharing a commitment: **the unit of
analysis is the relationship, and the whole is more than the sum of its parts**. The course traces that
commitment from von Bertalanffy's general systems theory and the Macy conferences on cybernetics, through the
Palo Alto communication school, Minuchin's structuralism, Bowen's multigenerational theory, and the Milan
group, to social constructionism, critical and feminist theory, and contemporary complexity and network
perspectives.

## Week 1 — The systemic turn

**What changed.** Before the systemic turn, the family was understood as the *environment* of an individual
patient. After it, the family became the *unit of treatment*, and individual symptoms became functions of
relationship patterns.

**What it answered.** Three problems the individual model struggled with: Why does a symptom appear in a
particular child? Why does the family resist change (homeostasis)? Why does the same individual behave
differently in different relationships?

**What it risked.** Dissolving the person into the system; treating "the family" as a homogeneous actor;
ignoring power, gender, class and history; and producing descriptions that could not be falsified.

> **PRACTICAL** — Take one case. Write it twice: once as an individual formulation (as a CBT or
> psychodynamic clinician would) and once as a systemic one. Then write a paragraph on what each version
> makes visible and invisible.

## Week 2 — General systems theory

**Von Bertalanffy's propositions**, translated for clinicians:

| Proposition | Clinical meaning |
|---|---|
| A system is a set of elements in interaction | The family is defined by its interactions, not its members |
| The whole is more than the sum of its parts | The family has properties no member has alone |
| Systems are open or closed to their environment | A family that refuses outside contact becomes closed and rigid |
| Systems have boundaries | Who is inside, who is outside, and how permeable |
| Systems are hierarchical | Subsystems nested within larger systems |
| Equifinality | Different starting points can reach the same end state |
| Equipotentiality | Similar starting points can reach different end states |
| Negative entropy | Systems import energy to counter disorder (information, support) |

**Equifinality and equipotentiality are the most useful and most neglected.** Equifinality explains why two
very different families produce the same symptom; equipotentiality explains why two similar families produce
very different outcomes. Together they demolish simple causal stories.

**Example.** Two children are referred for school refusal: one in a family with high conflict and one in a
family with high achievement pressure. Equifinality explains the identical presentation; equipotentiality
explains why one family recovers in four sessions and the other takes two years.

## Week 3 — Cybernetics I

**Feedback.** A system regulates itself by monitoring its own output and adjusting its input.

![Figure 3.1 — Negative and positive feedback in a machine and in a family](../assets/figures/fig10-feedback-loops.svg)

- **Negative feedback** corrects deviation: the thermostat, the bedtime routine, the family that de-escalates
  when voices rise.
- **Positive feedback** amplifies deviation: the microphone screech, the escalation, the panic spiral.

**Homeostasis.** The system's tendency to maintain its organisation. **Morphostasis** maintains; **morphogenesis**
changes. A family can be *stably unhappy*: the symptom is one of the mechanisms that holds the system
together.

**The clinical insight.** Attempts to change a system are themselves inputs. If the family's equilibrium
depends on the symptom, your intervention will produce a counter-move. Predicting the counter-move — and
sometimes prescribing it — is the strategic tradition's contribution.

**Calibration example.** A family's daughter has "tantrums" that end a 45-minute argument between her
parents. The tantrum is negative feedback for the marital system (it restores calm) and positive feedback for
the child (it works). Two feedback systems, one event.

> **PRACTICAL** — Map both feedback loops in a disguised case: one for each "layer" (the couple system and the
> child's behaviour). Show how the same event serves both.

## Week 4 — Cybernetics II: first and second order

![Figure 4.1 — First- and second-order cybernetics: where the therapist stands](../assets/figures/fig01-two-cybernetics.svg)

**First-order cybernetics.** The observer is outside the system; interventions are inputs; the aim is
control of the system's output. This produced the heroic era: directive, strategic, clever.

**Second-order cybernetics.** The observer is part of the observed system; distinctions are drawn by an
observer; knowledge is provisional. This produced curiosity, the reflecting team, and the therapist's
accountability for their own effect.

**The clinical consequences.**

| First order | Second order |
|---|---|
| "What intervention will fix this family?" | "What am I noticing, and what am I blind to?" |
| The family is the object of change | The family and the therapist co-construct change |
| Neutrality is possible | Position is unavoidable; transparency is required |
| Expertise is the therapist's | Expertise is distributed |

**Reflexivity is not self-indulgence.** It is the discipline of accounting for your own contribution to the
clinical picture: which family member you find easier to like, which story you believed first, which data you
stopped collecting once you had a hypothesis.

## Week 5 — Communication theory

![Figure 5.1 — Watzlawick's five axioms of communication](../assets/figures/fig11-communication-axioms.svg)

**The five axioms** (Watzlawick, Beavin & Jackson).

1. One cannot not communicate.
2. Every message has a content and a relationship level.
3. Communication is punctuated into sequences by participants.
4. Messages are digital (words) and analogical (analogue).
5. Relationships are symmetrical or complementary.

**Double bind hypothesis** (Bateson): a person receives contradictory messages at different levels, cannot
comment on the contradiction, and cannot leave the relationship. The hypothesis was weakened as an explanation
of schizophrenia, but its clinical descendant — recognising contradictory, un-commentable communications in
families — remains useful.

**Critique.** Communication theory could become mechanistic (families as machines exchanging messages) and
could pathologise families of people with psychosis. It also tended to describe "systems" without the power
differences that determine who gets to define what is communicated.

> **PRACTICAL** — Transcript analysis: take ten lines of a couple's argument and identify punctuation,
> relationship-level messages, and symmetrical/complementary moves.

## Week 6 — Structural theory

![Figure 6.1 — Structural mapping: enmeshed, disengaged and cross-generational coalition](../assets/figures/fig05-structural-map.svg)

**Core propositions** (Minuchin).

1. The family's structure is the invisible set of rules that govern transactions.
2. The structure organises the family into subsystems (spousal, parental, sibling) separated by boundaries.
3. Symptoms are best understood as the family's response to its structure, and sometimes as the only way the
   structure can remain stable.
4. Change in structure produces change in experience.

**The evaluation criteria for a structure.** Boundaries must be *clear* (not diffuse, not rigid); hierarchy
must be *appropriate* to stage and culture; and subsystems must have enough autonomy to function and enough
connection to belong.

**Power and structure.** Structural theory was criticised for assuming a normative hierarchy (executive
parents, subordinate children) and for not asking *how* that hierarchy is upheld. Contemporary structural
practice inserts the questions: whose authority is legitimate in this family, and who bears the cost of it?

## Week 7 — Multigenerational theory

![Figure 7.1 — Bowen's eight interlocking concepts](../assets/figures/fig06-bowen-concepts.svg)

**Core propositions** (Bowen).

1. The family is an emotional unit; anxiety is its most important variable.
2. Differentiation of self is the capacity to remain connected while thinking independently.
3. Anxiety is managed in one of four ways: conflict, distance, projection to a child, or dysfunction in one
   member.
4. Triangles distribute anxiety and stabilise dyads.
5. Patterns are transmitted across generations; symptomatic children are often the most triangulated.

**The theory's strength** is time: it offers an account of *why now*, by locating the present pattern in
several generations of adaptation to war, migration, loss and religion.

**The theory's weakness** is its insufficient account of power, material constraint and culture. A woman
cannot always "differentiate" out of an arranged marriage; a family cannot always "stay out of triangles"
when migration has severed the network.

## Week 8 — Milan and meaning

![Figure 8.1 — Circular causality in the consulting room](../assets/figures/fig02-circular-causality.svg)

**Core propositions** (Palazzoli, Boscolo, Cecchin, Prata).

1. The family is a system of meanings (premises) that organise behaviour.
2. The therapist forms hypotheses and tests them with circular questions.
3. Neutrality is a stance of curiosity towards all members, not indifference.
4. Positive connotation frames all behaviour as serving the system's coherence.
5. Interventions disturb the system by changing premises, not by demanding behaviour change.

**The evolution.** Classical Milan used teams, rituals and directives; later Milan (and the reflecting team)
abandoned directive intervention and emphasised multiple perspectives and the therapist's own openness.

**Critique.** Neutrality became indefensible in cases of violence and abuse; circular questioning can become
an elegant evasion; and the intellectual register excluded many families.

## Week 9 — Constructivism and social constructionism

**Constructivism** (Maturana, von Glasersfeld): each person constructs a model of reality from their own
experience; we cannot access an objective reality directly. Clinically: take each family member's account
seriously, and stop trying to establish "the truth".

**Social constructionism** (Gergen, Shotter): meanings are produced *between* people, in language and in
culture, and change with context. Clinically: the problem, the roles and the identities are made in talk, and
so they can be made differently.

**The distinction matters.** Constructivism risks relativism and individualism (everything is "just your
view"); social constructionism locates meaning in relationships and power, which makes it compatible with the
critical and cultural traditions.

**Implication for practice.** The therapist's formulation is one description among several. Offering it
humbly — "here is a story I am beginning to see; how does it look to you?" — is both a constructionist
technique and a mark of respect.

## Week 10 — Narrative and postmodern theory

**Narrative** (White & Epston). Stories organise experience; dominant stories make alternatives invisible;
the task is to externalise the problem and thicken a preferred story.

**Postmodern influences.** Foucault on discourse and power-knowledge; Derrida on deconstruction. The clinical
translation: question the "official" account, ask who benefits from it, and look for what it silences.

**The reflexivity of narrative therapy** is unusual: the therapist is "decentred but influential", and the
client is the author. This is an ethical stance as much as a technique.

**Critique.** The linguistic emphasis can under-serve families with limited literacy or in acute crisis; the
evidence base, while growing, is smaller than the cognitive-behavioural tradition's.

---

## Week 11 — Critical, feminist and anti-racist systems theory

**The feminist critique** (Hare-Mustin 1978; Goldner; Walters) made four claims that changed the field:

1. "Circular" descriptions conceal power asymmetries.
2. "Neutrality" in the face of violence colludes with the perpetrator.
3. The gendered division of labour, not "the family system", is often the object of the wife's complaint.
4. Models that prize individuation encode a male norm.

**Anti-racist and postcolonial theory** extend the critique: whose family structures are treated as healthy?
Which knowledge counts as evidence? How do services reproduce the state's relationship to marginalised
families?

**Clinical translation.**

- Ask about power explicitly, in every assessment.
- Never use systemic language to make a victim responsible for the violence she receives.
- Place race, migration, class and religion inside the system, not outside it.

## Week 12 — Attachment and neuroscience in systems theory

**Two imports that have reshaped the field.**

1. **Attachment theory** gives systemic thinking a developmental mechanism: the couple and parent–child
   systems are organised by attachment strategies, and those strategies are formed in relationships.
2. **Neuroscience** gives systemic thinking a physiology: co-regulation, the window of tolerance, mirroring and
   stress physiology explain *how* relationships change bodies.

**The integration.** "Systems" are not abstract patterns; they are embodied patterns between nervous systems.
A couple's pursue–withdraw cycle is simultaneously a communication pattern, an attachment strategy, and a
sequence of autonomic states.

**Critique of the imports.** Neuroscience is often oversold in clinical texts; "mirror neurons" and "the right
brain" are frequently invoked without evidence. Use physiology where it is well established (stress,
regulation, sleep, trauma) and keep the metaphors labelled as metaphors.

## Week 13 — Critiques and limitations

| Critique | Substance | Response |
|---|---|---|
| The system has no agent | "The family did it" hides who actually did what | Locate action; ask who decided |
| Power is under-theorised | Systemic descriptions can neutralise abuse | Explicit power analysis |
| Culture is externalised | "The system" was a Western two-parent family | Cultural and critical turns |
| Evidence is thin | Many systems concepts are unfalsifiable | Operationalise and test |
| The therapist disappears | Neutral observer stance | Second-order reflexivity |
| Complexity obscures action | Endless circular description delays intervention | Use the simplest map that guides action |

**The mature position.** Systems theory is a *disciplinary sensibility*, not a complete theory. It tells you
where to look (between people, across generations, in context) and what to resist (single villains and simple
causes). It does not, by itself, tell you what to do.

---

## Week 14 — Complexity, networks and the contemporary field

**Complexity theory.** Families are non-linear, self-organising systems: small differences can produce large
divergence; cause and effect are not proportional; and feedback loops are the rule. This fits clinical
experience better than linear models, but it resists prediction.

**Network thinking.** Contemporary research treats families as networks of relationships, with density,
centrality and clustering measurable. Who is central? Who is isolated? Who is the only link between two
clusters? This is a formalised genogram, and it is improving our understanding of family structure and
resilience.

**Ecological models.** Bronfenbrenner's nested systems (Figure 7 in MFT 701) place the family inside
institutions, culture and time — a corrective to the field's tendency to treat the family as self-contained.

## Glossary

**Autopoiesis** — the self-producing organisation of living systems.
**Boundary** — the rules governing participation and exchange between systems.
**Circularity** — reciprocal causation within a system.
**Cybernetics** — the study of control and communication in systems.
**Differentiation of self** — see Bowen.
**Equifinality** — different paths leading to the same end state.
**Equipotentiality** — similar starting points leading to different end states.
**Homeostasis / morphostasis** — the maintenance of system organisation.
**Morphogenesis** — change in system organisation.
**Punctuation** — the marking of a sequence's beginning; the source of blame.
**Reflexivity** — the therapist's accountability for their own participation.
**Second-order cybernetics** — cybernetics of observing systems.
**Structure** — the repetitive patterns of transaction that organise a family.
