# MFT 714 — Trauma-Informed Care

> **MSc Marriage and Family Therapy** · Semester Two · 1.71 Units · 15 teaching weeks · Prerequisite: MFT 712

**Course leader's summary.** Trauma changes the body, the memory, the attachment system and the family.
This course teaches the physiology, the phased treatment models, and the organisational stance — and it
teaches you to avoid the two commonest errors: doing too much too soon, and doing nothing at all.

---

## How to use this book

This is the most personally demanding book in the programme. Announce it in advance to your peers; know your
own history; use supervision; and do not read the trauma chapters late at night. The material is presented in
the order treatment unfolds: safety, then processing, then integration.

---

## 1. Course description

**Trauma-informed** care means organising practice around the prevalence and impact of trauma; **trauma-
specific** care means delivering interventions designed to treat it. You need both.

The course covers: definitions and types of trauma; the Adverse Childhood Experiences evidence; the
neurobiology of threat and regulation; dissociation; attachment and developmental trauma; the phased model;
specific modalities (TF-CBT, EMDR, narrative exposure, somatic approaches); family and couple work with
trauma; intergenerational transmission; re-traumatisation in services; and practitioner self-protection.

## Week 1 — What trauma is

**Definition (DSM-5/ICD-11, paraphrased).** Exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual
violence, whether experienced directly, witnessed, learned about in relation to a close person, or through
repeated occupational exposure.

**Types.**

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Single-incident | One event, adult onset | Road traffic collision, armed robbery |
| Complex | Repeated, prolonged, interpersonal, inescapable | Domestic abuse, childhood abuse |
| Developmental | Occurs during maturation; shapes personality and attachment | Chronic neglect, chaotic caregiving |
| Collective | Shared by a community | War, displacement, massacre, epidemic |
| Vicarious / secondary | Exposure through another's trauma | Therapist, first responder, family carer |

**What makes an event traumatic is not only the event.** It is the event *plus* the meaning, the
interpersonal context (was it caused by someone who was supposed to protect you?), the aftermath (was the
person believed? supported?), and prior vulnerability.

**Complex PTSD** (ICD-11) comprises three additional clusters beyond classic PTSD: affect dysregulation,
negative self-concept, and disturbances in relationships. This matters clinically: classic PTSD responds well
to exposure-based treatment; complex PTSD usually requires a longer stabilisation phase.

> **PRACTICAL** — Classify five disguised cases by type, and note which additional features each has.

## Week 2 — The ACE evidence

**The original study.** Adverse Childhood Experiences (abuse, neglect, household dysfunction) were found to
have a graded relationship with later health outcomes, including depression, addiction, suicide attempts,
heart disease and early death.

**What it established.** Early adversity is common, cumulative, and biologically embedded. It shifted the
field from asking "what is wrong with you?" to "what happened to you?"

**What it does not establish.** That ACE scores are destiny; that any individual's future is determined; or
that a high score is a diagnosis. Roughly a third of people with high ACE scores have no significant
impairment — protective relationships matter enormously.

**The clinical use.** ACE enquiry is a conversation, not a score: "Was there anything difficult in your
childhood that we should know about? You do not have to give details."

> **CAUTION** — Asking about adversity can activate distress. Ask when you have time to respond, offer a
> choice, and have a grounding plan ready.

## Week 3 — Neurobiology I: the window of tolerance

![Figure 3.1 — The window of tolerance](../assets/figures/fig18-window-of-tolerance.svg)

**The three states.**

- **Within the window:** the person can think, feel, choose, connect and learn.
- **Hyperarousal:** fight/flight — panic, rage, hypervigilance, intrusive images, restlessness.
- **Hypoarousal:** freeze/collapse — numbness, dissociation, shutdown, shame, exhaustion.

**Trauma narrows the window.** Small triggers produce large reactions; the person's capacity to stay
relational shrinks. This is not "over-reacting"; it is a nervous system doing what it learned.

**Regulation skills to teach (and use in session).**

1. **Paced breathing** — exhale longer than inhale (4 in, 6 out) for 90 seconds.
2. **Orienting** — look around the room and name five things you can see.
3. **Grounding** — feet on the floor, weight in the chair, describe the texture of an object.
4. **Movement** — stand, walk, stretch; discharge the mobilised energy.
5. **Temperature and touch** — cold water, a heavy blanket, pressure.
6. **Naming** — "I am having a memory, not the event."

![Figure 3.2 — The polyvagal ladder](../assets/figures/fig19-polyvagal-ladder.svg)

**In family sessions.** If one member leaves the window, the session has ended for them. Pause, regulate,
and return. Teaching a couple to recognise each other's early signals is itself an intervention.

## Week 4 — Neurobiology II: memory, dissociation, the body

**Traumatic memory is different.** It is often implicit, sensory and fragmented: smells, images, body
sensations, without a coherent narrative or a sense of "pastness". This is why "just talk about it" can flood
a person and why containment matters.

![Figure 4.1 — The HPA axis and chronic stress](../assets/figures/fig20-hpa-axis.svg)

**Dissociation** — a disruption of consciousness, memory, identity or perception. Mild forms are common
(going blank, feeling unreal); severe forms include depersonalisation, derealisation and dissociative
amnesia. Assess gently: "Do you ever feel like you are watching yourself from outside? Do you lose time?"

**The clinical rule.** Do not interpret a dissociative response as resistance. If a client's eyes glaze, stop
the exercise, orient them to the room, and slow down. Then shorten the next segment.

## Week 5 — Attachment and developmental trauma

**Developmental trauma** (sometimes "complex relational trauma") occurs when the person who should provide
safety is the source of danger. The child adapts with strategies that later look like personality: vigilance,
people-pleasing, aggression, emotional shutdown, compulsive caregiving.

**The internal working model.** "I am unlovable; others are dangerous or unreliable; I must manage alone (or
never let go)." These beliefs are not irrational; they were accurate in the original environment.

**Clinical implications.**

1. Expect the therapeutic relationship to be tested — idealisation, devaluation, testing, sudden withdrawal.
2. Predictability and reliability matter more than technique.
3. Repair after rupture is the treatment, not a distraction from it.
4. Do not interpret the transference early; be the relationship first.

**Family application.** Parents with their own unresolved trauma often misread their children's ordinary
behaviour as threat. Psychoeducation about trauma and parenting can reduce harsh discipline dramatically.

## Week 6 — Trauma-informed assessment

**The four R's** (SAMHSA).

![Figure 6.1 — The four R's and six principles of trauma-informed practice](../assets/figures/fig40-trauma-informed.svg)

1. **Realise** the widespread impact of trauma.
2. **Recognise** the signs and symptoms.
3. **Respond** by integrating knowledge into practice and policy.
4. **Resist re-traumatisation.**

**Assessment principles.**

- Explain why you are asking, and offer choices ("you can skip any question").
- Ask about *the event* only at the level of detail needed for formulation; detail belongs in processing.
- Ask about **strengths, survival strategies and meaning**, not only symptoms.
- Screen for dissociation before any trauma-focused work.
- Screen for current safety (violence, self-harm, substance use).

**Instruments.** PCL-5 (PTSD symptoms), IES-R, CTQ (childhood trauma; use with care), DES-II
(dissociation), PHQ-9, AUDIT. Always follow up a positive screen with a conversation.

## Week 7 — Observed trauma-informed assessment

Forty minutes. Assessed on: safety and choice throughout, pacing, grounding as needed, formulation without
premature interpretation, and accurate risk screening.

---

## Week 8 — The phased model

**Phase 1: Safety and stabilisation.** Establish safety, build the alliance, teach regulation, address
practical dangers (housing, violence, substance use), and develop what Herman called "the capacity to
contain".

**Phase 2: Processing.** Work through the traumatic material using a validated method (TF-CBT, EMDR,
narrative exposure, prolonged exposure) — only when the person can tolerate it and has stabilisation skills.

**Phase 3: Integration and reconnection.** Rebuild identity, relationships, work, and meaning. This phase is
often the longest and the most neglected.

**Why sequence matters.** Processing without stabilisation produces flooding, drop-out, and sometimes
retraumatisation. Stabilisation without processing can produce a stable but constrained life. The judgment
is clinical, not clerical: some people need two sessions of stabilisation; some need twenty.

**Family application.** The family's safety and stability is Phase 1 at the system level. You cannot do
trauma processing in a household where the perpetrator is still present.

## Week 9 — Trauma-focused CBT with children

**TF-CBT** (Cohen, Mannarino & Deblinger) is the best-evidenced treatment for childhood trauma. Its PRACTICE
components:

| Component | Content |
|---|---|
| Psychoeducation and parenting skills | Trauma responses; consistent, non-punitive discipline |
| Relaxation | Breathing, muscle relaxation, grounding |
| Affect expression and modulation | Feelings identification, coping |
| Cognitive processing | Correcting trauma-related beliefs ("it was my fault") |
| Trauma narrative | Gradual, structured account in the child's medium (book, drawing) |
| In vivo mastery | Facing avoided, safe situations |
| Conjoint sessions | Child shares the narrative with the caregiver |
| Enhancing safety | Safety skills and planning for the future |

**The caregiver's role is essential.** The non-offending caregiver's belief, protection and support is the
strongest predictor of recovery. Work with them in parallel.

## Week 10 — Other trauma-specific modalities

| Modality | Mechanism | Evidence | Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| EMDR | Bilateral stimulation during memory recall | Strong for PTSD | Requires training; not a magic technique |
| Narrative exposure therapy | Chronological testimony of the whole life | Strong for multiple/collective trauma | Can be destabilising without stabilisation |
| Prolonged exposure | Repeated imaginal and in vivo exposure | Strong | Drop-out; requires strong alliance |
| Somatic approaches | Body-based regulation and discharge | Emerging evidence | Beware of unfounded claims |
| Yoga / mindful movement | Regulation, interoception | Good adjunctive evidence | Not a substitute for trauma processing |

**The professional duty.** Use what you are trained and supervised to use. A half-learned trauma protocol is
more dangerous than a well-delivered stabilisation phase.

## Week 11 — Couples and trauma

**Three presentations.**

1. **One partner has a trauma history.** The other becomes a de facto carer; intimacy becomes a trigger; the
   couple's sexual relationship collapses. Work with both: psychoeducation for the partner, boundaries for the
   survivor, and a paced intimacy plan.
2. **The trauma happened inside the relationship** (betrayal, violence, coercion). This is betrayal trauma.
   Safety and accountability come first; joint work only where the offending partner acknowledges the harm
   and the victim chooses it.
3. **Both partners are trauma survivors.** Two dysregulated nervous systems in one room. The pace must be
   slower, the structure tighter, and the regulation explicit.

**The couple-level formulation.** "When she freezes, he experiences rejection and pursues; when he pursues,
she freezes harder. The loop is a collision of two survival strategies, not a failure of love."

## Week 12 — Intergenerational transmission

**Mechanisms.** Parenting behaviour (frightening, inconsistent, harsh), attachment patterns, family narrative,
epigenetic and stress-physiology effects, and structural factors (poverty, displacement, community violence).

**The genogram of trauma.** Draw three generations and mark losses, violence, separation, migration,
untimely deaths and secrets. Then ask: what was never spoken about? What did each generation have to survive?

![Figure 12.1 — A three-generation genogram as a map of transmission](../assets/figures/fig04-genogram-case.svg)

**The hopeful finding.** Transmission is probabilistic, not deterministic. A consistent, protective
relationship at any point breaks the chain. Your therapy may be that relationship.

## Week 13 — Re-traumatisation in services

**How services re-traumatise, usually without meaning to.**

| Practice | Why it harms | Alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Restraint without explanation | Replicates powerlessness | Explain, offer choices, debrief |
| Closed doors, no warning | Replicates entrapment | Predictability, warnings, exits |
| Being asked to repeat the story to every professional | Replicates helplessness | Record once, share with consent |
| Long waits in public areas | Replicates exposure | Private, quiet space |
| Disclosing in front of a partner | Replicates danger | Always screen individually |
| "Non-compliance" language | Replicates blame | "What got in the way?" |

**The organisational standard.** Trauma-informed care is a policy and management issue: staff training,
physical environment, restraint policy, complaint routes, and supervision. A trauma-informed clinician in a
trauma-organised service will burn out.

---

## Week 14 — Vicarious trauma

**Distinguish three concepts.** *Burnout* (exhaustion from workload), *compassion fatigue* (reduced empathy),
and *vicarious trauma* (changes to the therapist's beliefs and inner world from exposure to others' trauma).

**Signs.** Intrusion of clients' material, nightmares, cynicism, loss of hope, changed worldview ("the world
is unsafe"), avoidance of trauma cases, over-involvement.

**Protection.** Supervision that addresses the work's emotional impact; caseload diversity; clear boundaries;
your own therapy; sleep, exercise and relationships; and acknowledging meaning — many clinicians find that
sustained trauma work deepens their sense of purpose when it is properly supported.

---

## Week 15 — What trauma-informed care is not

It is not the avoidance of difficult material. It is not an excuse for never processing trauma. It is not a
synonym for "be gentle". It is a disciplined sequencing of safety, processing and integration, delivered in a
relationship and an organisation that do not repeat the injury.

---

## Glossary

**Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs)** — potentially traumatic events in childhood, measured cumulatively.
**Betrayal trauma** — trauma caused by someone on whom the person depends.
**Complex PTSD** — PTSD plus affect dysregulation, negative self-concept and relational disturbance.
**Dissociation** — disruption of consciousness, memory, identity or perception.
**Grounding** — techniques that return attention to the present and the body.
**Re-traumatisation** — repetition of the dynamics of the original trauma in the therapy or service.
**Stabilisation** — the first phase of trauma treatment, focused on safety and regulation.
**Window of tolerance** — the arousal range within which a person can think and connect.
