# MFT 727 — Play Therapy Techniques

> **MSc Marriage and Family Therapy** · Elective · 1.71 Units · 14 teaching weeks · Prerequisite: MFT 711

**Course leader's summary.** Play is the child's language and the therapist's instrument. This course trains you
to set up a room, hold a session, use directed and non-directed methods, involve parents as partners, and
recognise when play is not enough.

---

## How to use this book

Play therapy is learned in a room, not in a book — but a badly equipped room produces bad therapy. Work through
the weeks in order: room, stance, skills, methods, family application, limits. Practise every technique on a
peer before you use it with a child.

---

## 1. Course description

The course covers the history and theory of play therapy; the child-centred (non-directive) model and its core
skills; directive and cognitive-behavioural play; sandtray; art and puppetry; filial therapy (teaching parents to
be therapeutic agents); family play therapy; assessment through play; trauma and play; and the evidence base.

## Week 1 — What play is for

**Theory.** Play is children's natural medium for expression, mastery, and meaning-making. Axline's eight
principles (1947) remain the most quoted statement of the non-directive stance: the therapist develops a warm
relationship; accepts the child as they are; establishes permissiveness; recognises and reflects feelings;
respects the child's capacity to solve problems; does not direct; does not hurry; and sets only those limits
that are necessary.

**Why it works (proposed mechanisms).** Play allows symbolic expression of experience that cannot be verbalised;
it provides a sense of control and mastery; it allows rehearsal of difficult situations; and the relationship
with the therapist provides a corrective relational experience.

**Evidence.** Play therapy has moderate support for a range of child problems, with stronger effects when
parents are involved (filial therapy) and when the approach is matched to the problem (e.g. CBPT for anxiety).
It is not a panacea, and it is not a substitute for trauma-specific treatment where indicated.

> **PRACTICAL** — Observe a child in free play for 15 minutes. Record behaviours only: what they chose, what they
> returned to, what they avoided, how they ended.

---

## Week 2 — The therapeutic powers of play

| Power | What it looks like | How the therapist supports it |
|---|---|---|
| Communication | The child enacts the problem | Track and reflect |
| Emotional regulation | Repetitive calming play | Allow repetition; contain |
| Mastery | Replaying a frightening event with a different ending | Do not interpret; note the shift |
| Symbolic distance | The "monster" rather than the father | Stay in the metaphor |
| Relationship | The child tests and then trusts | Consistency, warmth, limits |
| Imagination | New possibilities | Follow, do not lead |

**Session shape for a child.** Opening ritual (same each time), free play, a focused activity, a closing ritual.
Predictability is the intervention.

## Week 3 — The room and the toys

![Figure 3.1 — A play therapy room: layout, furniture and sightlines](../assets/figures/fig24-playroom-plan.svg)

**Principles.** Safety, predictability, accessibility, and privacy. The child should be able to reach what they
need; the therapist should be able to see everything.

**Toy categories (Landreth's classification).**

1. **Real-life toys** — dolls, family figures, cars, a cash register, a phone, cooking utensils.
2. **Acting-out/aggressive toys** — toy soldiers, a punch bag, wild animals, a toy gun, dinosaurs.
3. **Creative/expressive toys** — crayons, paint, clay, sand, blocks, musical instruments.

**What to include.** Sand tray, water, dolls and family figures, animal families, puppets, art materials, blocks,
a dollhouse, a medical kit, a small house, blankets, and a soft toy.

**What to avoid.** Toys that require the therapist's help to operate; toys with one "right" answer; electronic
toys that dominate; and anything that could be used to hurt without a limit.

**Room rules posted at child height.** "We stay in the room. We do not hurt ourselves. We do not hurt each
other. We do not hurt the room. We put things away when we finish."

## Week 4 — Limit setting

**Why limits matter.** Limits create safety, teach self-control, and protect the relationship. Permissiveness
without limits is not acceptance; it is abandonment.

**The ACT model** (Landreth).

- **A — Acknowledge the feeling:** "You are really angry with me."
- **C — Communicate the limit:** "But I am not for hitting."
- **T — Target an acceptable alternative:** "You can hit the cushion, or you can tell me with words."

**Sequence.** State the limit calmly, once; repeat with the alternative; if it continues, offer a choice ("you
can choose to put the toy down or I will put it away"); then follow through without anger.

**What not to do.** Negotiate; explain at length; threaten consequences you will not enforce; or shame the child.

> **REHEARSAL** — Role-play a child who throws a toy at you, three times. The third attempt should be delivered
> with the same calm as the first.

## Week 5 — Non-directive skills I

**Tracking** — narrating behaviour without evaluation: "You are putting the blue block on top. It wobbles."

**Reflecting content** — "You want the mother doll to go away."

**Reflecting feeling** — "You look pleased with that." / "That made you angry."

**Rules of thumb.**

- Describe, do not question.
- Name feelings tentatively ("you seem…"), allowing correction.
- Follow, do not lead.
- Stay in the metaphor; do not translate ("so the monster is your father" is an interpretation, not a reflection).

> **DRILL** — Ten minutes with a peer playing a 6-year-old. You may only track and reflect. No questions. The
> observer counts interpretations (target: zero).

---

## Week 6 — Non-directive skills II

**Returning responsibility** — handing the problem back: "That is something you can decide." / "You can try it."

**Esteem-building** — attributing effort and choice, not talent: "You kept trying until it stood up."

**Facilitating self-direction** — "You know what you want to do next."

**Enlarging the meaning** — offering a slightly wider frame: "You are the one who decides what happens in this
story."

**The therapist's own regulation.** Children notice your body. Breathe slowly, sit at their level, keep your face
open, and speak more slowly than you think you should.

---

## Week 7 — Observed non-directive session

Thirty minutes with a trained child actor (or a filmed role-play marked against criteria). Assessed on: room and
structure; tracking and reflection; returning responsibility; one limit set well; warmth; and the closing
ritual.

---

## Week 8 — Directive techniques

**Directive play is not instruction.** It is the deliberate use of a medium to address a specific issue, always
following the child's lead within the structure.

| Technique | Use | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Drawing** (family, house, fears, "worst day") | Assessment and expression | "Draw your family doing something." Then ask them to tell you about it |
| **Puppets** | Safe displacement | Let the child choose a puppet; interview the puppet, not the child |
| **Role-play** | Rehearsal and mastery | "Show me what happens at the school gate" |
| **Bibliotherapy** | Normalising and skills | Read a story about a similar problem; discuss the character |
| **Feelings cards / thermometer** | Emotional vocabulary | Sort into "big/little", "now/then" |
| **CBPT** | Anxiety, trauma | Psychoeducation, coping skills, graded exposure in play |

**Sequence for a directive activity.** Explain briefly → demonstrate → let the child lead → observe → reflect →
close.

## Week 9 — Sandtray

**What it is.** A tray of dry and wet sand with miniature figures, used to create a world.

**Two traditions.** Non-directive (the child builds, the therapist witnesses and reflects) and directive
(therapist suggests a theme, e.g. "make a world where you feel safe").

**Practicalities.** Trays with a water-resistant base; dry sand and water; a wide range of miniatures (people,
animals, buildings, vehicles, natural objects, fantasy, symbols of spirituality); a camera to record the scene
(with consent); and a clear policy about dismantling.

**Interpretation discipline.** Do not impose symbolic meanings. Ask: "Tell me about this world." "What happens
next?" "Who is this?" The child is the authority on their world.

**With older children and adults**, sandtray can be used as a non-verbal way of representing a family system —
who is placed near whom, what is buried, what is outside the tray.

## Week 10 — Assessment through play

**What you can learn.** Developmental level (language, symbolic play, attention, motor control); emotional
expression; themes (abandonment, violence, rescue, repetition); relational capacity (can they play with you?);
and the impact of specific events.

**What you cannot learn reliably.** Specific causation; the identity of an abuser; or a diagnosis from a single
drawing. Any assessment claim must be cautious and triangulated with other data.

**Signs warranting attention.** Play that is repetitive, narrowed and joyless; sexualised play beyond
developmental norms; play involving harm to self or others; a sudden change in play after an event; and a child
who cannot play at all.

**Documentation.** Describe behaviour and the child's own words; avoid interpretation in the record.

---

## Week 11 — Trauma and play; dissociation

**Principles (from MFT 714).** Safety and stabilisation first; the caregiver's regulation is the child's best
medicine; do not force the narrative; and expect repetition.

**In the playroom.** Watch for signs of dissociation (stillness, glazed eyes, going blank, sudden compliance).
Slow down, orient to the room, offer a grounding object, and return to safe play.

**Post-traumatic play** is repetitive and lacks resolution. The therapeutic task is not to stop it but to help it
move — by reflecting feeling, introducing a small element of mastery, and involving the caregiver.

**When play is not enough.** Severe trauma symptoms, self-harm, dissociation, and complex abuse histories need
trauma-specific treatment (e.g. TF-CBT) alongside or after stabilisation.

---

## Week 12 — Filial therapy

**What it is.** Training parents to conduct child-centred play sessions at home, with supervision. Developed by
Guerney; extensively researched; strong evidence for improving parent–child relationships and reducing child
problems.

**Why it works.** It increases parental empathy and reflective function, changes the parent's view of the child,
and provides a structured positive interaction that most distressed families lack.

**The structure (typically 10 sessions).**

1. Teach the principles and skills (tracking, reflecting, limit setting).
2. Demonstrate a play session with the child while the parent observes.
3. The parent conducts a session while the therapist coaches.
4. The parent conducts sessions at home (30 minutes weekly) and reports back.
5. Review and generalise.

**Practical adaptations.** Limited space at home; other children; a parent who works nights; a parent with their
own trauma history (support first).

> **PARENT SESSION** — Coach a parent through a 15-minute play session using tracking, reflection and one limit.

---

## Week 13 — Family play therapy

**Why involve the family.** The child's problem exists in a system; play can make the system visible.

**Techniques.**

- **Family drawing and sculpture** — who sits where, who is close, who is left out.
- **Co-constructed play** — the family builds one world together; observe negotiation, hierarchy and exclusion.
- **Role reversal** — the child directs the parents in a scene; useful for revealing the child's experience.
- **Structured family games** — cooperative tasks that require negotiation.

**Caution.** Family play can expose conflict. Have a structure for containing it, and be ready to end the
activity and debrief.

---

## Week 14 — Limits, evidence and integration

**The limits of play therapy.** Not a substitute for medical assessment, safeguarding action, trauma-specific
treatment or family work where the family system is the problem. Play therapy with a child in a family that
continues to harm them is not treatment; it is containment.

**The evidence summary.** Moderate support across problems; stronger when parents are involved; strongest when
the approach is matched to the problem and delivered with fidelity.

**Integration.** Play therapy sits comfortably within a systemic frame when it is used as the child's channel of
communication within a family assessment, and when parents are brought into the work.

---

## Glossary

**ACT model** — Acknowledge, Communicate the limit, Target an alternative.
**Child-centred play therapy** — non-directive approach following the child's lead (Axline, Landreth).
**CBPT** — cognitive-behavioural play therapy.
**Filial therapy** — training parents to conduct child-centred play sessions.
**Post-traumatic play** — repetitive, unresolving play following trauma.
**Sandtray** — a tray of sand with miniatures used for symbolic expression.
**Therapeutic powers of play** — the mechanisms through which play produces change.
