# MFT 722 — Professional Development and Self-Care

> **MSc Marriage and Family Therapy** · Elective · 1.71 Units · 14 teaching weeks · Prerequisite: MFT 706

**Course leader's summary.** This is not a wellbeing add-on at the end of training. It is the course that
decides whether you are still practising in fifteen years, and whether the practising is any good. It treats
the therapist's own life as the primary instrument, and professional development as a discipline rather than an
ambition.

---

## How to use this book

Every week has a **professional** task and a **personal** one. Do both. The course is assessed largely on a
portfolio, and a portfolio written retrospectively in the final week will be visible and weak. Keep a weekly
entry; it takes eight minutes.

---

## 1. Course description

The course covers: the person of the therapist; burnout, compassion fatigue and vicarious trauma; boundaries and
availability; personal therapy; reflective practice and supervision; continuing professional development;
career pathways and private practice fundamentals; money, contracts and pricing; professional identity and
belonging; and sustainable practice in resource-limited settings.

## Week 1 — The person of the therapist

**The premise.** You cannot separate the clinician from the person. Your history shapes what you notice, what you
avoid, whom you like, and where you lose your balance.

**The "therapy autobiography".** Write, privately, about: your family of origin; the rules you absorbed; the
losses; the roles you played; what you learned about conflict, intimacy, money and authority; and why you chose
this work. Do not submit it. Do keep it, and revisit it in three years.

**Common motivations and their risks.**

| Motivation | Strength | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| "I want to help people" | Compassion | Rescuing; over-functioning |
| "I want to understand my family" | Insight | Using clients to solve your own history |
| "People tell me I'm easy to talk to" | Alliance skill | Difficulty setting boundaries |
| "I survived something" | Credibility | Over-identifying; avoidance of certain material |
| "I want status/expertise" | Drive | Distance; difficulty with not-knowing |

**The professional requirement.** Know your history well enough that you can tell the difference between your
client's material and your own reaction. Personal therapy and supervision exist for this, not because something
is wrong with you.

## Week 2 — Burnout

**Definition** (Maslach): a syndrome of **emotional exhaustion**, **depersonalisation/cynicism**, and **reduced
sense of accomplishment**, arising from chronic occupational stress.

**The six drivers** (Maslach & Leiter) — all of them organisational:

1. Workload
2. Control (or lack of it)
3. Reward (financial, social, intrinsic)
4. Community
5. Fairness
6. Values (mismatch between what you believe and what the system asks)

**The clinically important point.** Burnout is not a personal failing and cannot be solved by yoga. If the
caseload is impossible, the supervision absent and the values compromised, self-care is a distraction from the
real problem.

**Self-assessment.** Use the Maslach Burnout Inventory or the freely available Copenhagen Burnout Inventory.
Score yourself honestly; write one paragraph on which of the six drivers is most active in your current
situation.

## Week 3 — Compassion fatigue and vicarious trauma

**Three distinct concepts.**

| Concept | Definition | Signature |
|---|---|---|
| Burnout | Exhaustion from workload | "I am too tired to care" |
| Compassion fatigue | Reduced empathy and satisfaction from caring | "I used to feel for people; now I don't" |
| Vicarious trauma | Changes to beliefs and inner world from exposure to others' trauma | "The world is not safe; people are dangerous" |

**Signs to monitor in yourself.** Intrusion of client material; nightmares; avoidance of certain cases;
cynicism; irritability at home; numbness; loss of hope; over-involvement; boundary erosion; sleep and appetite
change; increased alcohol use.

**Protective factors with evidence.** Supervision that addresses the emotional impact; caseload diversity;
control over scheduling; peer support; meaning-making; sleep and exercise; personal therapy; and organisational
support.

## Week 4 — Boundaries

**The boundaries that matter most in early practice.**

| Boundary | The trap | A workable standard |
|---|---|---|
| Time | Sessions over-run; you arrive early, leave late | Fixed session length; 10 minutes between clients |
| Contact | Clients text at night; you reply | Written policy; emergency route; no personal number |
| Disclosure | You share to reassure | Disclose for the client's benefit, rarely, and briefly |
| Gifts | Refusing feels rude | Accept small cultural gifts; discuss significant ones |
| Social media | Client requests; you see their posts | Clear policy; never search for clients |
| Dual roles | Church, community, family ties | Disclose, discuss, refer if unmanageable |
| Emotional labour | You become the family's friend | Warmth plus frame; endings are explicit |

**The test.** Not "was it kind?" but "whose need did it serve, and would I be comfortable explaining it in
supervision?"

## Week 5 — Personal therapy

**Requirements vary.** Many training programmes require a minimum number of personal therapy hours. Treat the
requirement as a floor, not a target.

**What it is for.** Not to become "fixed", but to know your patterns well enough to notice them in the room;
to have a place to process work that is not your supervisor's job; and to experience being a client.

**What to look for in a therapist.** A different orientation from yours (to see another way of working); a
recommendation from someone you trust; a clear contract; and someone you can be honest with.

**The cost problem.** Therapy is expensive, and trainees are often poorly paid. Options: reduced-fee schemes,
group therapy, university counselling services, peer consultation groups, and budgeting it as a professional
expense rather than a luxury.

## Week 6 — Reflective practice

**Reflection is a method, not a mood.** Use a structure.

**The Gibbs cycle**, applied to a session: description → feelings → evaluation → analysis → conclusion → action
plan.

**Six questions to answer weekly.**

1. What happened that surprised me?
2. What did I feel, and where in my body?
3. What did I avoid?
4. What did I do that worked?
5. What would I do differently?
6. What do I need to bring to supervision?

**Writing.** Keep one page per week. Do not write case details in a personal journal that is not secure; use
initials or pseudonyms, and never store client-identifying material outside the clinical record.

---

## Week 7 — Supervision as a career-long practice

**The norms.** One hour of supervision for every five hours of clinical work is the common standard; more for
complex cases and for trainees.

**How to use supervision well.**

- Arrive with an agenda and one clear question.
- Bring the case you least want to bring.
- Say what you felt, not only what you did.
- Ask for observation, not only opinion ("what would you have said?").
- Record what you will do differently, and report back next time.

**Peer consultation groups.** A complement, not a substitute: 3–5 clinicians, monthly, structured, with a
confidentiality agreement and a rotating facilitator.

---

## Week 8 — Continuing professional development

**Frameworks.** Most professional bodies require annual CPD across domains: clinical practice, supervision,
ethics, research, teaching, management. Map your year against the domains rather than collecting certificates.

**A CPD plan that works.**

1. Identify three gaps from your competency self-assessment.
2. For each, specify an activity, a date, and an evidence of completion.
3. Include one activity that stretches you (a new modality, a research skill, a leadership role).
4. Review every six months; adjust.

**Beware CPD theatre.** Ten webinars on topics you already know count for nothing. One supervised course in an
area of genuine weakness counts for a great deal.

## Week 9 — Career pathways

| Pathway | What it needs | What it gives | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clinical specialist | Depth in a modality; supervision; certification | Expertise, referrals | Narrowing |
| Public sector clinician | Systems navigation; resilience | Security, breadth | Bureaucracy, heavy caseloads |
| Private practice | Business skills, capital, network | Autonomy, income potential | Isolation, income volatility |
| Academic/teaching | Research, publication, teaching | Influence, stability | Bureaucracy, distance from clinic |
| Supervision/consultation | Experience, accreditation | Leverage, variety | Responsibility for others' practice |
| NGO/humanitarian | Flexibility, languages, resilience | Meaning, breadth | Insecurity, exposure to trauma |
| Policy/advocacy | Writing, networks, patience | Scale of impact | Slowness, compromise |

**The honest question.** Which of these fits the life you actually want — including your family, your health,
and your finances — not only the career you imagined at 22?

## Week 10 — Money, fees and contracts

**Fees are clinical decisions.** Set them deliberately; publish them; apply them consistently; review annually.

**A fee policy covers:** the fee; session length; payment method and timing; late cancellation; non-attendance;
sliding scale criteria (decided in advance); annual review; and what happens to outstanding balances.

**Contracts.** Even a one-page agreement prevents most disputes: parties, purpose, confidentiality and limits,
fees, cancellation, contact policy, complaints route, and signatures.

**The uncomfortable truths.**

- Charging is not exploitation; under-charging until you resent clients is.
- A sliding scale with no criteria becomes arbitrary and unfair.
- Chasing unpaid fees is a clinical conversation, not an administrative one.
- You will need to budget for tax, insurance, supervision, CPD and sick leave.

---

## Week 11 — Private practice fundamentals

**The blueprint.** Location and room (privacy, sound, waiting area, accessibility); legal structure and
registration; professional indemnity insurance; record-keeping and data protection; booking and payment
systems; referral network; risk and emergency protocols; supervision arrangement; and a marketing approach that
is ethical and honest.

**Starting safely.** Keep a part-time employed post while you build; take a business course or find a mentor;
decide your niche only after 50 cases, not before; and never practise without supervision, however experienced
you are.

**Isolation is the occupational hazard.** Build a peer group before you need one.

---

## Week 12 — Professional identity and belonging

**Belonging matters.** Clinicians who are connected to a professional community report less burnout, better
decision-making, and greater persistence.

**How to build it.** Join a professional association; attend one conference a year; contribute to a peer group;
teach one workshop; write one short piece; mentor one junior colleague.

**Identity in a contested field.** Family therapy sits between psychology, psychiatry, social work and
counselling. Be clear about who you are and what you offer, without disparaging others: "I am a systemic
therapist; my expertise is in relationships. I work alongside your psychiatrist, not instead of them."

---

## Week 13 — Practice readiness viva

Twenty minutes. Questions drawn from: boundary scenarios, burnout scenarios, ethical dilemmas, fee
conversations, supervision difficulties, and career planning. Assessed on judgement, honesty, and the ability to
name your own limits.

---

## Week 14 — CPD and career presentation

Ten minutes on where you are, where you are going, what you need, and what you will stop doing. Include one
commitment to your own sustainability that is specific, dated, and verifiable.

## Glossary

**Burnout** — exhaustion, cynicism and reduced efficacy from chronic occupational stress.
**Compassion fatigue** — reduced empathy and satisfaction resulting from prolonged caring.
**Compassion satisfaction** — the positive counterpart: meaning and pleasure derived from helping.
**CPD** — continuing professional development.
**Reflective practice** — structured examination of one's own work and reactions.
**Vicarious trauma** — transformation of the therapist's inner world through exposure to others' trauma.
