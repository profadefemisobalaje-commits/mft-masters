# MFT 723 — Special Populations in Therapy

> **MSc Marriage and Family Therapy** · Elective · 1.71 Units · 14 teaching weeks · Prerequisite: MFT 708, 714

**Course leader's summary.** "Special populations" is a compromise term. It names groups whom services tend to
miss, misread or harm — and it trains the clinician to adapt the frame without lowering the standard of care.

---

## How to use this book

Each week takes one population and works through four questions: what is the specific stressor, what does the
evidence say helps, what must be adapted, and what should I not do? The aim is competence in adaptation, not
expertise in every group.

---

## 1. Course description

The course covers families affected by forced displacement; LGBTQ+ clients and their families; people with
disabilities and chronic illness; older adults; families involved with the criminal justice system; veterans and
uniformed services; single-parent and grandparent-headed households; childless and infertile couples; families
affected by HIV; and street-connected children.

Each carries a specific combination of stigma, legal precarity, service exclusion and clinical complexity that
requires a specific adaptation of the systemic frame.

## Week 1 — Forced displacement

**The specific stressors.** Loss (home, status, language, network, identity); prolonged uncertainty; trauma
before, during and after flight; family separation; role reversal; and the "limbo" of waiting for legal status.

**The clinical tasks.** Safety and basic needs first; normalising distress without pathologising; restoring
roles; supporting the family's own rituals and faith; and addressing the grief of the *life left behind* — often
unmourned because the family is still in survival mode.

**Adaptation.**

- Do not require a full trauma narrative in early sessions.
- Use practical problem-solving alongside emotional work; both are therapeutic.
- Involve community and faith organisations.
- Ask about the family members who are missing — literally and emotionally.

---

## Week 2 — Asylum-seeking families

**The "second trauma"** is the legal process: interviews, credibility assessments, detention, dispersal,
uncertainty, and the requirement to tell the worst story repeatedly to strangers.

**Clinical realities.**

- Status anxiety dominates every decision: whether to report violence, whether to seek medical care, whether
  to disclose sexuality.
- Children may be the family's cultural and linguistic bridge.
- Court and immigration reports may be requested; the report and the therapy must be kept distinct.

**What helps.** Concrete assistance with documentation and access; clear, honest explanation of what you will
write and to whom; liaison with legal representatives (with consent); and a therapeutic space where the person
is not required to justify their deserving.

---

## Week 3 — LGBTQ+ clients and their families

**Minority stress.** Prejudice, expectation of rejection, internalised stigma and concealment produce chronic
stress and elevated rates of anxiety, depression, self-harm and suicide.

**Family work.** Families often present in crisis after disclosure, with religious and cultural conflict. The
work is to: create safety for the young person; hear the parents' fear without endorsing rejection; slow the
timetable (rejection, like acceptance, is a process); and set limits on harm — verbal abuse, expulsion, forced
"corrective" interventions.

**What not to do.**

- Do not promise to change the young person, or to change the family.
- Do not require the client to come out as a condition of care.
- Do not collude with secrecy that is unsafe, or with disclosure that is premature.
- Do not refer to "the lifestyle"; use the client's language.

**The clinical stance.** You are not there to adjudicate theology or politics. You are there to reduce harm,
improve relationships where possible, and support the person's dignity.

---

## Week 4 — Disability

**The two models.** Medical (the body is the problem) and social (society's barriers are the problem).
Clinically, both matter.

**What families need.**

- Accurate information about the condition and its trajectory.
- Help with the burden of care and the sibling experience.
- Support for the parental relationship, which is often absorbed by the caregiving.
- Advocacy with schools, employers and health services.
- Attention to the disabled person's own voice and autonomy, which families often override in the name of
  protection.

**Adaptation.** Accessible rooms; longer sessions; communication support; written materials in accessible
formats; and a willingness to conduct sessions in the home when necessary.

---

## Week 5 — Chronic illness and palliative care

**The illness as a third member of the family.** It takes a role, demands attention, reorganises routines and
relationships.

**Clinical tasks.** Rebalance the couple relationship when one partner becomes a carer; address the children's
understanding and fears; manage the family's beliefs about illness and fate; support decision-making about
treatment; and prepare for death without abandoning hope.

**Palliative and end-of-life work.** Advance care planning; legacies; unfinished business; the family's
conflicts about treatment; and bereavement support afterwards.

**The clinician's own mortality.** This work touches your own fears. Supervision is essential.

---

## Week 6 — Older adults

**Specific stressors.** Retirement, bereavement, physical decline, loss of role, relocation, dependence, and
the assumption by others that life is over.

**Clinical presentations.** Depression misdiagnosed as ageing; anxiety; cognitive change (which must be
differentiated from delirium, depression, hearing loss and dementia); family conflict about care; elder abuse;
and grief.

**Adaptation.** Slower pace; larger print; hearing support; sessions at home; involving the family in practical
decisions; and taking the older person's goals as primary, not the family's convenience.

**Elder abuse.** Ask directly and separately: "Has anyone hurt you, taken your money, or stopped you seeing
people?" Know your safeguarding route.

---

## Week 7 — Criminal justice involvement

**The absence.** Imprisonment removes a person from the household while keeping them psychologically present.
Children are often told a story; partners carry shame and practical burden.

**Clinical tasks.** Support the remaining household; address the children's understanding and stigma; prepare
for release and re-entry; manage the couple's renegotiation; and address the family's history (which may
include the behaviour that led to imprisonment).

**Re-entry.** The returning member often expects to resume an old role, while the family has reorganised without
them. Naming this mismatch early prevents a second rupture.

**Adaptation.** Sessions in prison settings; rules about contact; careful confidentiality (the prison is not a
confidential setting); and coordination with probation or social services.

---

## Week 8 — Uniformed services

**The specific culture.** Stoicism, mission focus, hierarchy, and a strong norm against help-seeking.

**Clinical presentations.** Deployment and reintegration; moral injury; PTSD; substance use; domestic violence;
and the family's secondary traumatisation.

**Adaptation.** Frame treatment in terms the culture respects (performance, mission readiness, the family's
stability); involve the unit where appropriate; and address the couple's shift in roles during absence and
return.

---

## Week 9 — Single-parent and grandparent-headed households

**The specific stressors.** Role overload; financial strain; the absent parent's shadow; stigma; and children's
questions.

**Strengths.** Many single-parent households function very well; treat the structure as a structure, not a
deficit.

**Grandparent-headed households.** Grief (for the parent, often due to death or incapacity); the generation gap;
legal status (custody, maintenance); health limitations; and the children's loyalty conflicts.

**Adaptation.** Practical support first; a clear parenting plan; support for the child's contact with the absent
parent where safe; and attention to the carer's own health.

---

## Week 10 — Infertility and childlessness

**The cultural weight.** In many settings, childlessness carries severe social stigma, blamed disproportionately
on women, with consequences for marriage stability and family standing.

**Clinical themes.** Grief (often disenfranchised); shame; blame and the couple's conflict; pressure from
extended family; religious meaning; treatment decision-making and its cost; and the couple's sexual relationship,
which becomes medicalised.

**The clinical stance.** Do not assume that parenthood is the goal for every couple. Ask what a good life looks
like for them. Name the social pressure as external, not personal failure.

---

## Week 11 — Families affected by HIV

**The clinical realities.** Stigma and secrecy; disclosure decisions (to partner, children, family, church);
medication adherence; transmission anxiety; bereavement and orphanhood; and the intersection with poverty and
gender inequality.

**Disclosure work.** Help the person decide who, when and how; rehearse; prepare for different responses; and
ensure the children's questions are answered at their developmental level.

**Adaptation.** Confidentiality is paramount in small communities; coordinate with HIV services carefully;
address the family's beliefs about illness and blame; and support the "worried well" partner as well.

---

## Week 12 — Street-connected children and youth

**The specific realities.** Survival needs first (food, safety, shelter); mistrust of adults and institutions;
peer networks that function as family; substance use; exposure to violence and exploitation; and legal
vulnerability.

**What helps.** Outreach and continuity; a trusted adult who does not disappear; harm reduction; family
reunification work only where it is safe; and collaboration with community organisations, not only clinics.

**What harms.** Coercive removal; moralising; short-term projects that end abruptly; and treating the peer group
as pathology when it is often the only protection available.

---

## Week 13 — Intersectionality and service design

**The design questions.**

1. Who does not come to our service, and why?
2. What are the access barriers — cost, transport, language, hours, stigma, documentation, disability?
3. Who is the first point of contact, and are they trained?
4. What does the physical environment communicate?
5. What happens when a client needs an interpreter, a wheelchair, a child-friendly space, or a private entrance?
6. How do we measure whether these groups are actually being served?

**A pathway that works** typically includes: community outreach; a named link worker; flexible appointments;
interpreters; transport support; plain-language materials; and a feedback loop with the communities served.

> **PROPOSAL (25%)** — A service pathway for one under-served population, with access analysis, staffing, and
> outcome measures.

---

## Week 14 — Integration

**The generalizable skills.** Ask rather than assume; adapt the frame, not the standard; work with community
organisations; attend to legal precarity; protect confidentiality in small communities; and examine your own
discomfort.

**The generalizable caution.** "Special population" can become a way of othering. Every family is specific; the
skill is in the specificity, not in the label.

---

## Glossary

**Asylum seeker** — a person seeking protection who has not yet been granted refugee status.
**Disenfranchised grief** — grief that is not socially recognised or supported.
**Minority stress** — chronic stress from stigma, prejudice and concealment.
**Moral injury** — psychological distress from acting against one's values in service.
**Social model of disability** — disability located in societal barriers rather than the body.
