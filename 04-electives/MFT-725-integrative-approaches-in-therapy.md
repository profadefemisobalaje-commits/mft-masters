# MFT 725 — Integrative Approaches in Therapy

> **MSc Marriage and Family Therapy** · Elective · 1.71 Units · 14 teaching weeks · Prerequisite: MFT 702, 715

**Course leader's summary.** Most experienced clinicians are integrative, whether or not they say so. The
question is whether the integration is disciplined or accidental. This course builds a principled framework for
combining models without producing incoherence, and teaches you to justify every combination.

---

## How to use this book

Integration is a design problem, not a mood. Each week adds a dimension to a framework you will use for the
rest of your career. By Week 14 you will have a written integrative framework, tested on three cases, and
defended in a viva.

---

## 1. Course description

The course covers: the varieties of integration (assimilative, theoretical, sequential, pluralistic); common
factors and their implications; the major dimensions of family therapy (systemic, attachment, behavioural,
experiential, cognitive, narrative, biological); decision frameworks for matching model to client and phase;
feedback-informed treatment; staged and stepped care; and the risks of eclecticism.

## Week 1 — Why integrate

**Three pressures.**

1. **Evidence.** No single model is superior for everything. Different models have stronger evidence for
   different problems (FBT for adolescent anorexia, EFT for couple distress, PMT for conduct problems, family
   psychoeducation for psychosis).
2. **Clients.** Real families present with multiple, interacting problems: trauma plus addiction plus couple
   distress plus a child's school refusal.
3. **Clinicians.** Most experienced therapists drift towards integration as their caseload diversifies.

**The danger.** Integration without discipline becomes eclecticism: a technique for every mood, no coherent
theory of change, and a client who cannot tell what is expected of them.

## Week 2 — Forms of integration

| Form | Definition | Example | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assimilative | A home model, enriched by techniques from elsewhere | EFT therapist borrowing a behavioural task | Drift from the core theory |
| Theoretical | A new model synthesising several | Integrative behavioural couples therapy | Complexity, training burden |
| Sequential | Different models at different phases | Structural stabilisation, then EFT | Discontinuity for the family |
| Parallel | Different models for different subsystems | Couple work + individual trauma work | Splitting between clinicians |
| Pluralistic | Collaborating with the client on method | "Which approach fits you?" | Requires an informed client |

**Choosing a form.** Assimilative integration is the most realistic for a newly qualified clinician: it keeps
coherence while allowing flexibility. Sequential and parallel integration are appropriate for complexity.
Pluralistic practice is an ethical stance as much as a technique.

---

## Week 3 — Common factors

**The claim.** Much of psychotherapy's effect comes from factors shared across models: the alliance, agreement
on goals, therapist empathy, client expectancy, and extratherapeutic factors.

**The clinical use — without relativism.**

1. **Monitor the alliance** in every session, with every family member.
2. **Repair ruptures** quickly; the repair is often more powerful than the original intervention.
3. **Agree goals explicitly**; disagreement about goals predicts drop-out.
4. **Engender hope** honestly (not false reassurance).
5. **Then use techniques** — which matter most for specific problems and for structuring the work.

**The critique.** Percentages attributed to common factors are illustrative, not measured; "all models are
equal" is over-stated; and technique does matter for specific disorders. The defensible position: the alliance
is necessary but not sufficient; technique without alliance is inert; alliance without technique is often
insufficient.

## Week 4 — The systemic dimension

**What it contributes.** Attention to pattern, structure, triangles, context and power. It tells you *where to
look* and *what maintains* the problem.

**What it lacks alone.** Specific techniques for symptom change; guidance for individual emotion and trauma;
and a sufficient account of biology.

**Integration in practice.** Systemic formulation + any intervention. You can use a behavioural task, an EFT
enactment, or a narrative externalising conversation, provided the systemic formulation justifies it.

---

## Week 5 — The attachment dimension

**What it contributes.** A developmental account of why bonds matter; a map of strategies under threat; a
mechanism for change (corrective emotional experience, enacted in the room).

**Integration with systemic work.** Systemic thinking tells you the loop; attachment tells you why the loop
matters so much and what each person is asking for.

**Integration with behavioural work.** Behavioural tasks build positive exchange; attachment work deepens the
meaning of the exchange. Alone, either can be shallow.

---

## Week 6 — The behavioural and cognitive dimensions

**What they contribute.** Specific, testable, teachable skills; measurable change; homework; structure; and a
strong evidence base for many problems.

**Integration cautions.** Behavioural work can feel mechanical when the emotional bond is damaged; cognitive
restructuring can be premature in acute trauma; and skills practice without meaning can become performance.

**A practical sequence.** Stabilise and structure (behavioural) → access emotion and meaning (experiential,
attachment) → consolidate with skills and plans (behavioural, cognitive).

---

## Week 7 — The experiential and narrative dimensions

**What they contribute.** Access to primary emotion; meaning-making; identity; and the language of the family's
own story.

**Integration.** Experiential work deepens the emotional field; narrative work organises it into a story the
family can hold. Together they answer "what do we feel?" and "what does it mean?".

**Caution.** Emotional work without containment can flood; narrative work without emotion can become
intellectual. The integration is in the pacing.

---

## Week 8 — The biological dimension

**What it contributes.** Sleep, medication, substances, hormonal change, illness, and the physiology of stress
and trauma.

**Integration.** A formulation that ignores biology will fail in predictable ways: the depressed client who does
not respond to therapy because of untreated hypothyroidism; the couple whose sexual problem is a medication side
effect; the adolescent whose sleep deprivation mimics ADHD.

**Your role.** Screen, refer, coordinate, and never advise on medication.

---

## Week 9 — Matching, sequencing and staging

**Decision framework — five questions.**

1. **What is the most dangerous thing here?** (Risk governs sequence.)
2. **What is the most changeable thing here?** (Start where change is possible; hope is built on early wins.)
3. **What must be in place before other work can happen?** (Safety, stabilisation, attendance, sobriety.)
4. **Which model has the best evidence for the primary problem?** (Use it, or refer.)
5. **What does this family want and believe in?** (Engagement is a precondition, not a luxury.)

**Staging example.** A couple with active violence, alcohol misuse, and a child's school refusal:
Phase 1 — safety and sobriety (individual, specialist referral); Phase 2 — couple de-escalation and parenting
plan; Phase 3 — child's return to school and family consolidation.

---

## Week 10 — Feedback-informed treatment

**The instruments.** The Outcome Rating Scale (ORS) and Session Rating Scale (SRS) are brief, free, and
practical: four visual analogue items each, completed every session.

**How to use them.**

1. Introduce them as a way of making sure the therapy is working.
2. Look at the scores before you speak.
3. Ask about a drop: "Your score fell by three points. What happened?"
4. Ask about the alliance: "Is there anything about how we work that is not fitting?"
5. Act on the answer in the session, not next month.

**The evidence.** Routine outcome monitoring with feedback improves outcomes and reduces deterioration,
particularly for clients who are not progressing. It is one of the most robust findings in the field.

> **PORTFOLIO (15%)** — Ten sessions with ORS/SRS data, your interpretation, and the changes you made.

---

## Week 11 — Comorbidity and complexity

**Complexity is the norm.** Typical community caseloads include trauma plus substance use plus couple distress
plus child behaviour plus financial strain.

**Principles.**

1. Formulate the whole system, not a list of diagnoses.
2. Identify the keystone: the one change that would make others possible.
3. Sequence: safety → stabilisation → skills → processing → consolidation.
4. Share the load: refer where specialist input is needed; coordinate rather than compete.
5. Review: complexity requires more frequent review, not longer sessions.

---

## Week 12 — Culture, values and the ethics of integration

**Culture shapes which models fit.** An approach that prizes emotional expressiveness may feel exposing; one
that prizes autonomy may feel cold; one that requires homework may be impractical.

**Values shape technique choice.** Your beliefs about gender, family duty, forgiveness and individual autonomy
will show up in what you emphasise. Audit them.

**Ethical integration.** Do not use a technique you are not trained in; do not combine models to appear
sophisticated; and do not use integration as a reason to avoid referring.

---

## Week 13 — Coherence

**The coherence test.**

| Question | If the answer is no… |
|---|---|
| Can I state the formulation in three sentences? | You do not have one yet |
| Does every intervention follow from the formulation? | You are improvising |
| Can I name the model I am using at this moment? | You are drifting |
| Can I predict what will happen next, and be wrong productively? | Your formulation is too vague |
| Would a supervisor understand my reasoning without me explaining it? | Document it |

**The coherence defence.** Present a case to peers and answer: what are you doing, why, and how will you know
whether it worked?

> **COHERENCE DEFENCE** — Ten minutes with a case, followed by hostile questioning from peers.

---

## Week 14 — Your integrative framework

Write 2,000 words:

1. Your home model and its theory of change.
2. The dimensions you integrate (systemic, attachment, behavioural, experiential, narrative, biological).
3. Your decision rules for matching and sequencing.
4. Your use of feedback and outcome data.
5. Your limits: what you do not do, and to whom you refer.
6. How you will know you are drifting.

Defend it in a viva.

## Glossary

**Assimilative integration** — a home model enriched by techniques from elsewhere.
**Common factors** — mechanisms shared across therapies (alliance, empathy, expectancy, goals).
**Feedback-informed treatment** — using session-by-session outcome and alliance data to adjust therapy.
**Keystone change** — a change that makes other changes possible.
**Pluralistic practice** — collaborating with the client to choose methods.
**Sequential integration** — using different models at different phases of treatment.
**Stepped care** — matching intensity of intervention to need.
