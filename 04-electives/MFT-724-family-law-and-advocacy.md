# MFT 724 — Family Law and Advocacy

> **MSc Marriage and Family Therapy** · Elective · 1.71 Units · 14 teaching weeks · Prerequisite: MFT 704

**Course leader's summary.** Family therapists work at the edge of the legal system: custody disputes,
protection proceedings, divorce, maintenance, guardianship. This course gives you the legal literacy to be
useful, the boundaries to stay in your lane, and the advocacy skills to change the systems your clients pass
through.

---

## How to use this book

**This is orientation, not legal advice.** Statutes, case law and state practice vary and change. Every week
names the questions you should ask a lawyer, not the answers you should give a client. The final weeks turn from
individual cases to systemic advocacy.

---

## 1. Course description

The course covers: the Nigerian marriage and family law framework (statutory, customary, Islamic and
cohabitation); divorce and dissolution; custody, access and maintenance; the welfare principle; child rights and
protection law; domestic violence legislation; mediation and alternative dispute resolution; report writing and
expert testimony; the therapist's role in court; and advocacy for policy and service change.

## Week 1 — Why legal literacy matters

**Three reasons.**

1. **Your clients are inside legal processes.** Divorce, custody, maintenance, protection orders, immigration,
   inheritance. If you do not know the terrain, you cannot help them navigate it.
2. **You will be asked for opinions you should not give.** "Will I get custody?" is a legal question with a
   clinical component. Confusing them harms clients.
3. **You may be a witness or a report-writer.** The quality of your documentation determines whether your
   evidence is useful or damaging.

**The boundary map.**

| Activity | Who does it | Your role |
|---|---|---|
| Legal advice | Lawyer | Refer; explain the difference |
| Mediation | Trained mediator | Conduct only if trained and if safe; otherwise refer |
| Therapeutic work | You | Your core role |
| Expert assessment (custody) | Independent expert | Generally not your own client |
| Court testimony | Witness | Fact witness; opinion only within expertise |
| Advocacy | You, as a professional | Legitimate, within your competence and evidence |

## Week 2 — Four marriage systems

| System | Legal basis | Form | Dissolution route |
|---|---|---|---|
| Statutory | Marriage Act | Monogamous; registered | High Court petition under the Matrimonial Causes Act |
| Customary | Customary law; state customary courts | Bride price, kin consent; potentially polygynous | Customary court; customary arbitration |
| Islamic | Islamic law; Sharia courts in relevant states | Up to four wives | Sharia court; talaq, khul', or judicial dissolution |
| Cohabitation | No marriage | — | No dissolution; limited statutory protection |

**Clinical implications of the overlap.** A couple may be married under two systems; the customary marriage may
not be registered; the religious ceremony may have legal consequences; and children's status may depend on
which system applies. Do not assume.

**Ask:** "How was your marriage formalised? Was it registered? Is there a certificate? Which court would you go
to if you needed to?" Then refer to a lawyer for the answer's consequences.

## Week 3 — Divorce and dissolution

**Statutory divorce (orientation).** Governed by the Matrimonial Causes Act: a petition to the High Court; a
two-year minimum duration (with exceptions); a recognised ground (adultery, unreasonable behaviour, desertion,
separation, or living apart); and provision for custody, maintenance, property and costs.

**Customary and Islamic dissolution.** Different processes, different courts, different evidentiary rules,
different outcomes for women in practice. Know where to refer.

**The therapist's role.**

- Help the couple/clients clarify what they are deciding (discernment).
- Explain that therapy is not a legal process and does not produce evidence for court.
- Support the emotional work alongside the legal process.
- Refer for legal advice early, not at the point of crisis.
- Warn clients that therapy records may be requested in proceedings.

## Week 4 — Custody, access and the welfare principle

**The welfare principle.** Decisions about children are governed by the child's best interests, not the parents'
preferences or the mother's automatic entitlement.

**What courts consider (broadly).** The child's wishes (weighted by age and understanding); continuity of care;
the capacity of each parent; the child's relationship with each parent; siblings; education and health; and any
risk of harm.

**The therapist's contribution.** You can describe the child's relationships, needs and views (carefully, and
with appropriate methods); you can support the parents to reduce conflict; and you can help them construct a
parenting plan.

**What you should not do.** Offer a custody recommendation for your own client. That is an expert assessment
requiring independence, a defined methodology and often a court appointment. Role conflict invalidates the
opinion and harms the family.

**The parenting plan.** Residence, contact, holidays, schooling, health decisions, communication, transitions,
handovers, new partners, and a dispute-resolution clause. Concrete plans reduce conflict.

## Week 5 — Maintenance and financial provision

**Child maintenance.** Both parents have an obligation to maintain their children, under statutory and customary
law. Amounts are determined by need, capacity and standard of living; enforcement is a practical problem.

**Spousal maintenance.** Available in some statutory proceedings; less so in customary and religious frameworks
and highly variable in practice.

**The therapist's role.** Money is clinical material: it carries meaning about fairness, power and abandonment.
Help the couple separate the *amount* from the *meaning*; encourage full financial disclosure; and refer for
legal advice on entitlements.

**Practical support.** Referral to legal aid, mediation services, and women's rights organisations where
appropriate.

---

## Week 6 — Child rights and protection

**The Child Rights Act (2003)** — adopted in many but not all Nigerian states — sets out the rights of the child,
including protection from abuse and neglect, the right to be heard, and provisions on child marriage, child
labour, and alternative care. State child protection laws and structures operationalise it.

**Your duties.**

1. Recognise signs of abuse and neglect.
2. Respond without leading or investigating.
3. Record verbatim.
4. Report through the designated route (state ministry responsible for social welfare; police family unit;
   designated safeguarding lead).
5. Document the advice received and the action taken.
6. Support the child through the process.

**What not to do.** Investigate yourself; promise secrecy; confront the alleged abuser; or delay the report
while you "gather more information".

---

## Week 7 — Domestic violence law

**The Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act 2015** applies in the FCT and in states that have adopted it;
other states have their own laws or rely on criminal provisions. Protection orders, duties of the police and
support for victims vary substantially by jurisdiction.

**The clinician's knowledge.** Where the nearest police family unit is; what a protection order requires; which
shelters and legal aid organisations exist; and what happens procedurally to a woman who reports.

**The clinician's limits.** You are not the police, the lawyer, or the judge. Your role is safety planning,
documentation, referral and support — and not making the client's decisions for her.

## Week 8 — Mediation and alternative dispute resolution

**What mediation is.** A structured, voluntary process in which a neutral third party helps the parties reach
their own agreement. It is not therapy and not arbitration.

**When it is suitable.** Both parties voluntary and safe; no coercive control; reasonably balanced power; an
issue that can be negotiated; and willingness to disclose information.

**When it is not.** Violence or coercion; a severe power imbalance (including literacy, language or economic
dependency); untreated substance use or mental illness that prevents participation; and safeguarding concerns.

**Structure of a mediation session.** Opening and ground rules → each party's account → identification of issues
→ exploration of interests → generation of options → negotiation → agreement documented → review.

**The therapist's temptation.** To slip into therapy mid-mediation (exploring feelings) or into advice. Explain
the frame at the start and hold it.

---

## Week 9 — Reports and court

**The report structure.**

1. Your role, qualifications and the basis of your opinion.
2. Who you saw, when, and with whose consent.
3. Methodology (interviews, observation, measures).
4. Findings, described behaviourally.
5. Opinion, clearly separated and limited to your expertise.
6. Limitations, explicitly stated.
7. Recommendations, if requested and within competence.

**Language.** "It is my opinion that…" not "the court should". No adjectives about character. No speculation about
events you did not observe.

**Testimony.** Answer the question asked; do not volunteer; distinguish fact from opinion from hypothesis; say
"I don't know" when you don't; and remain courteous under challenge.

**Records.** Assume that anything you write may be read in court. Write accordingly.

## Week 10 — Expert versus treating roles

![Figure 10.1 — An ethics decision model for role conflicts](../assets/figures/fig33-ethics-decision-model.svg)

| Treating clinician | Expert assessor |
|---|---|
| Paid by/for the client; therapeutic duty | Paid by the court; duty to the court |
| Confidential | Limited confidentiality; report disclosed |
| Advocates for the client's wellbeing | Neutral; reports what the evidence shows |
| Continuous relationship | Time-limited, defined assessment |
| Cannot be objective about custody | Can be objective if independent |

**The conflict is structural, not a matter of willpower.** Accepting both roles compromises the therapy and the
evidence. If asked, explain and refer.

**Where you can help.** Provide factual records with consent and a court order as required; support the client
through the process; and help parents reduce conflict.

---

## Week 11 — Working with lawyers and courts

**What lawyers need from you.** Clear, dated, factual information; a timely response; an opinion you can defend;
and an understanding of your limits.

**What you need from them.** Clarity about the purpose of the report; the legal questions to address; the
deadline and format; and confirmation of consent arrangements.

**Interprofessional etiquette.** Do not tell a lawyer their job; do not let a lawyer tell you your clinical
findings. Negotiate the boundary explicitly and in writing.

---

## Week 12 — Mediation skills assessment

Thirty minutes, role-played. Assessed on: framing and ground rules; neutrality; screening for safety; interest-based
exploration; option generation; documentation; and referral decisions.

---

## Week 13 — Advocacy

**From case to policy.** Individual work reveals systemic failures: no shelter, no legal aid, no interpreter, no
protection order enforcement, no follow-up. Advocacy is the professional response.

**Levels.**

1. **Case advocacy** — with one client: writing a letter, making a call, accompanying to a service (with care).
2. **Service advocacy** — improving your own organisation's pathway.
3. **Policy advocacy** — submissions, research briefs, media, coalitions.
4. **Community advocacy** — working with community organisations and faith groups.

**The evidence base for advocacy.** Documented case patterns, service data, and research. Anecdote persuades
sympathetic listeners; data persuades sceptical ones.

**Ethical guardrails.** Consent before using a client's story; never identify a client; separate your
professional opinion from your personal politics; and do not compromise clinical relationships for a campaign.

---

## Week 14 — Advocacy proposal and integration

Present a 1,500-word proposal: the problem, the evidence, the target, the ask, the allies, the timeline, and how
you will know whether it worked. Then, in the final discussion, return to the boundary map from Week 1 and
revise it with what you have learned.

## Glossary

**ADR** — alternative dispute resolution.
**Coercive control** — a pattern of domination and isolation that makes mediation unsafe.
**Guardian ad litem** — a person appointed to represent a child's interests in proceedings.
**Maintenance** — financial support owed to a spouse or child.
**Matrimonial Causes Act** — the Nigerian statute governing statutory divorce.
**Protection order** — a court order restraining a person from specified conduct.
**Welfare principle** — the rule that the child's best interests govern decisions about them.

### Short answer

1. Distinguish therapy, mediation, expert assessment and legal advice.
2. What are the grounds for refusing to mediate?
3. Outline a court-facing report structure.
4. List the steps when you suspect child abuse.
