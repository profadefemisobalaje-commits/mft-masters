MFT 717 — Psychopathology in Families

Course leader's summary. Severe mental distress is not caused by families, and it is not treated without them. This course builds the knowledge and the nerve to work with psychosis, mood disorders, severe anxiety, personality-level difficulties and eating disorders in a relational frame — alongside medication and psychiatry, not instead of them.

How to use this book

The course deliberately reverses a historical mistake. For decades, family therapy implied that families caused severe mental illness; that legacy still harms families who arrive expecting to be blamed. Each week therefore begins with what is known about aetiology, then asks what a family therapist actually contributes.

1. Course description

The course covers the major categories of severe psychopathology as they present in family life: psychotic disorders, bipolar disorder, major depression, severe anxiety and OCD, personality-level difficulties, eating disorders, and the interface with substance use. It emphasises family psychoeducation, expressed emotion, family accommodation, relapse prevention, caregiver burden, the recovery model, and respectful collaboration with psychiatric services.

Week 1 — The historical legacy

The mistake. Mid-twentieth-century theories blamed mothers for schizophrenia ("schizophrenogenic mother"), attributed psychosis to family communication ("double bind"), and described "families of origin" as pathogenic. These ideas were poorly evidenced, deeply damaging, and are still remembered by families.

The correction. Contemporary research shows that families are neither the cause nor the cure of psychotic disorders, but they are among the strongest predictors of outcome — through support, monitoring, accommodation and emotional climate.

What family therapy offers now:

  • psychoeducation (reliable information, in plain language)
  • communication and problem-solving training
  • reduction of criticism, hostility and over-involvement where they are high
  • relapse prevention and early-warning planning
  • support for carers
  • help with the ordinary relational life that the illness interrupts
— Read one historical text and one contemporary family-intervention trial. Write 400 words on what changed, and what harm the earlier framing did.

Week 2 — Psychosis

What it involves. Hallucinations, delusions, disorganised thinking, negative symptoms (flat affect, anhedonia, withdrawal), and cognitive impairment. Onset is typically late adolescence or early adulthood, which means it lands in the middle of a family's launching stage.

What families experience. Fear, grief, confusion, exhaustion, stigma, violence in a minority of cases, and a chronic state of vigilance. Siblings often report invisibility; parents often report marital strain.

What helps (evidence summary). Antipsychotic medication is the foundation. Family intervention (typically 10–12 sessions of psychoeducation plus communication and problem-solving) reduces relapse and improves functioning. Supported employment, cognitive remediation and long-term follow-up matter.

What to avoid. Blaming; interpreting delusions symbolically; colluding with the illness ("he cannot help it, so nothing is required of him"); and treating the family as the patient.

Week 3 — Expressed emotion and family intervention

Expressed emotion (EE) comprises criticism, hostility and emotional over-involvement. High EE predicts relapse in schizophrenia and other conditions, though the effect is smaller than early research suggested and is bidirectional (illness severity also raises EE).

Assessing EE without blaming. Ask about the last month: how many arguments, what triggered them, how the carer responded, how much time is spent managing symptoms, and whether the carer feels able to have a life of their own.

The compassionate reframe. High criticism is usually exhaustion; over-involvement is usually love and fear. Naming that — before teaching any skill — is what makes the intervention acceptable.

Script: "You have been managing something terrifying for four years. Of course you are exhausted and sometimes sharp. The aim of these sessions is not to make you nicer; it is to give you back some room."

Communication training then teaches: making positive requests, active listening, expressing negative feelings safely, and problem-solving in structured steps.

Week 4 — Family psychoeducation

The format. 6–12 sessions, ideally including the person with the diagnosis and their carers, with a combination of information, communication training and problem-solving.

A single-session structure.

SegmentContent
1. What the family already knowsElicit their model of the illness
2. What is knownSymptoms, course, causes (multi-factorial), treatment, prognosis
3. What helpsMedication adherence, sleep, stress reduction, early warning signs, routines
4. What the family can doCommunication, problem-solving, reducing accommodation, protecting their own life
5. What to do in a crisisNumbers, triggers, what to say, who to call
6. QuestionsTen minutes of honest answers

Language. Plain, non-technical, and hopeful without being false: "This is a serious condition that can be managed. It is not anyone's fault, and it is not a life sentence."

Week 5 — Bipolar disorder

What families see. Periods of elevated mood, reduced sleep, grandiosity, irritability, risk-taking, and then depression. The family's experience is often of a person they recognise only intermittently.

Family tasks. Recognising the relapse signature (early signs of an episode in this person), agreeing a plan in advance, protecting sleep, managing money during highs, and responding to risk.

The relapse signature plan.

  1. List the person's own early signs (e.g. sleeping 4 hours and feeling fine; starting three projects).
  2. Agree what the family will do (e.g. call the psychiatrist; take over the bank card; reduce stimulation).
  3. Agree what the family will not do (argue about whether an episode is real; wait for a crisis).
  4. Review after each episode.

Psychoeducation plus family-focused treatment has good evidence for relapse prevention in bipolar disorder.

Week 6 — Depression in relational context

Two directions. Depression is influenced by relationships (criticism, conflict, isolation, caregiving burden), and it influences them (withdrawal, irritability, reduced libido, helplessness).

Couple-based interventions have good evidence for depression, particularly where relationship distress is present. Key mechanisms: reducing criticism and hostility, increasing positive exchange, addressing attributional blame, and supporting behavioural activation with a partner's help.

What not to do. Treat the depressed person as the sole problem; ask the partner to become a therapist; ignore the couple's sexual and practical reality; or reassure prematurely.

— A husband's depression follows a job loss. The couple's cycle: he withdraws, she takes over the household, he feels useless, she feels abandoned. Intervention: behavioural activation with agreed tasks that restore his role, plus work on her anxiety about relinquishing control.

Week 7 — Severe anxiety, OCD and accommodation

Family accommodation is the family's participation in the anxiety: answering the same question forty times, checking locks, avoiding supermarkets, providing reassurance, taking over tasks.

Why it persists. It reduces distress immediately, which reinforces it for both parties.

Reducing accommodation without cruelty. Build a hierarchy with the family; reduce one item at a time; agree what the person will do instead; expect a temporary increase in distress; and rehearse the script: "I love you, and I am not going to answer that again. I will sit with you for ten minutes instead."

For OCD specifically: psychoeducation about the function of compulsions; family agreement not to participate in rituals; ERP as the treatment of choice, with the family as allies.

Week 8 — Observed psychoeducation session

Forty minutes: eliciting the family's model, delivering information in plain language, teaching one communication skill, agreeing a crisis plan, and answering questions honestly.

Week 9 — Personality-level difficulties

Caution with language. "Personality disorder" is a contested and stigmatising label. Many clinicians describe "personality-level difficulties" or "complex relational patterns" and focus on the pattern rather than the category.

What families experience. Emotional storms, self-harm, idealisation and devaluation, fear of abandonment, and often years of contact with services that have not helped.

What helps.

  1. Validation before change: "Given what you have survived, this reaction makes sense."
  2. Consistency: the same rules, the same times, the same responses.
  3. Boundary clarity: "I will call you back at six" — and doing it.
  4. Family psychoeducation about emotion regulation and invalidation.
  5. Coordination between services, because splitting between professionals is part of the pattern.

What harms. Moralising; interpreting the behaviour as manipulation; inconsistency; and treating every crisis as a new emergency rather than part of a known pattern.

Week 10 — Eating disorders

The relational reality. Eating disorders reorganise the household: meals become battlegrounds, siblings become collateral, and parents oscillate between control and helplessness.

Family-based treatment (FBT) for adolescent anorexia is first-line. The stance is agnostic about cause and externalising of the illness: the family defeats anorexia, and no one is blamed for it.

Phase 1: parents take charge of refeeding, with support, and are absolved of guilt. Phase 2: control is returned to the adolescent as developmentally appropriate. Phase 3: identity and adolescent issues.

Medical safety is primary. Eating disorders have the highest mortality of any mental disorder. Weight, electrolytes and cardiac status are medical matters; do not work alone.

For adults with longstanding eating disorders, the work is often individual and relational: shame, identity, control, and the couple's accommodation.

Week 11 — Caregiver burden and the household

Assess the carers, not only the patient. Burden is measurable and treatable: use a brief carer measure and ask directly about sleep, work, finances, social life, and their own health.

Siblings. Often invisible, often parentified, often resentful and guilty about the resentment. Give them a session, language for their experience, and permission to have their own life.

The household system. Who is doing the caring? Who has given up what? Is there a "designated carer" whose own life has been suspended? Naming this is often the most useful intervention of the month.

Carer support plan. Respite, information, peer support groups, practical help, and their own GP.

Week 12 — Medication and collaboration

Your role. Support adherence without policing it; explore ambivalence about medication as you would any other ambivalence; report side effects; never advise stopping or changing a dose; and know the prescriber's name.

Collaboration. Write a short letter; attend a case conference; use SBAR on the phone; agree who is responsible for what. Families are often caught between two professionals who have never spoken.

Week 13 — Risk, hospitalisation and the family

Admission is a family event: relief, guilt, fear, and often secrecy. Prepare the family, explain the process, agree visiting, and plan from the first day for discharge.

Discharge is the highest-risk period. Plan: medication supply, follow-up appointment, who is at home, what the early warning signs are, and what to do at night.

When the family is afraid. Take the fear seriously; it is usually based on experience. Build a written crisis plan that the whole household knows.

Week 14 — Recovery, stigma and the future

The recovery model emphasises hope, identity, meaning and self-determination, not only symptom reduction. For families, this means asking: what does a good life look like for this person, and what would support it?

Stigma operates inside the family too: shame, secrecy, "we do not tell anyone", blame of the mother. Addressing internalised stigma is a clinical task.

Planning the future. Housing, work, relationships, and what happens when the parents can no longer care. This conversation is difficult and usually deferred; the therapist's job is to make it possible.

Week 15 — Integration

Four principles.

  1. No blame. Families are partners, not pathogens.
  2. Information is an intervention. Accurate, plain-language psychoeducation changes outcomes.
  3. Emotion is contagious. Reducing criticism and over-involvement helps — and it is achieved by supporting carers, not by instructing them.
  4. Collaboration beats isolation. Medication, psychiatry, community services and family work together.

Glossary

Accommodation — family behaviours that participate in and maintain symptoms. Expressed emotion (EE) — criticism, hostility and emotional over-involvement in carers. Family-focused treatment (FFT) — a manualised programme of psychoeducation, communication and problem-solving, with good evidence in bipolar disorder. Family psychoeducation — structured information and skills for families living with a disorder. Prodrome — the early signs preceding a full episode. Recovery model — an orientation emphasising hope, identity, meaning and self-determination. Relapse signature — an individual's characteristic early warning signs of an episode.

Short answer

  1. State the evidence for family intervention in psychosis in four sentences.
  2. What is expressed emotion, and how do you assess it compassionately?
  3. Outline a single-session family psychoeducation structure.
  4. Give three ways to reduce family accommodation without cruelty.
  5. What should a discharge plan contain?

Essay (choose one, 2,000 words)

  1. "Families are partners, not pathogens." Trace the history of this claim and its clinical implications.
  2. Design a family intervention for a young person with first-episode psychosis, including the first four sessions.
  3. Critically discuss the ethics of using the diagnosis "personality disorder" with a family in the room.
