MFT 713 — Substance Abuse and Family Dynamics

MSc Marriage and Family Therapy · Semester Two · 1.71 Units · 15 teaching weeks · Prerequisite: MFT 710

Course leader's summary. Addiction is a family-organising process. This course teaches you to work with the user, the family, and the household system — using motivational, behavioural, systemic and recovery-oriented approaches — without moralising, colluding or abandoning hope.

How to use this book

Read the case at the start of each week before the theory. Addiction work punishes abstraction: families are exhausted, professionals are frustrated, and the evidence is often misrepresented by both. Hold two principles throughout: the person is not the substance, and the family is not the cause.

1. Course description

The course covers: models of addiction (disease, learning, biopsychosocial, systemic); assessment; the family cycle of use, secrecy and shame; motivational interviewing; relapse prevention; behavioural couples therapy for substance use; CRAFT (for concerned relatives); the interface with mental illness ("dual diagnosis"); recovery as a family process; medication-assisted treatment; and policy and cultural context.

Week 1 — Models of addiction

ModelCore claimClinical implicationCritique
MoralUse is a choice; user is weak or badPunishment, exclusionShame increases use; ignores biology
Disease / medicalAddiction is a chronic brain diseaseTreatment, medication, compassionCan reduce agency; "once an addict always" fatalism
Learning / behaviouralUse is reinforced behaviourSkills, contingency managementCan ignore neurobiology and context
BiopsychosocialBiology × psychology × social contextMultimodal treatmentDescriptive rather than prescriptive
Systemic / familyUse is embedded in and organises relationshipsFamily-inclusive treatmentRisk of blaming the family
Recovery / socialRecovery is a social process, not only clinicalPeer support, community, identityNeeds availability of recovery capital

The position used here. Addiction is a chronic, relapsing condition shaped by biology, learning, trauma, relationship, culture and economics. The person retains agency but not unlimited agency. Families are neither causes nor cures; they are the environment in which recovery either becomes possible or does not.

DEBATE — "Addiction is a disease" versus "addiction is a choice". Take a side, then argue the other side, then write the practical position you will hold with clients.

Week 2 — The family cycle

Six stations that repeat: use → secrecy → enabling → crisis → shame → promise → use.

Enabling is not weakness. It is a set of behaviours that reduce immediate consequences because the alternative (the person losing their job, being arrested, or dying) feels unbearable: paying a debt, calling in sick, hiding the bottles, giving money "for food".

Family roles (an imperfect but useful map): the enabler, the hero, the scapegoat, the lost child, the mascot. Use them as questions, never as labels: "Who in the family keeps the peace? Who takes the blame? Who disappears?"

The systemic point. The cycle is maintained by both the using and the accommodating. This is not a statement of blame; it is a statement of leverage. Change the accommodating behaviour and the cycle must change.

— Map a family cycle from a disguised case. At each station, write: who does what, what they feel, and what they fear. Then identify two points of leverage.

Week 3 — Assessment

Substance history.

DomainQuestions
SubstancesWhat, how much, how often, by what route, for how long
PatternTime of day, alone or social, binge or daily
FunctionWhat does it do for you? (sleep, courage, relief, numbness, belonging)
ConsequencesHealth, money, work, relationships, legal, children
Control attemptsHave you tried to stop? What happened? How long?
WithdrawalSymptoms, previous seizures, previous medically assisted withdrawal
DependenceTolerance, withdrawal, compulsion, time spent, continued despite harm
ReadinessDo you want to change? Why now? What would it take?
RiskSuicide, violence, driving, overdose, child safety

Validated tools. AUDIT (alcohol), DUDIT (drugs), ASSIST (multiple substances), CRAFFT (adolescents). Use them as a structure for conversation, not a verdict.

Assess the family separately. With the user absent: what has been tried, what has been covered up, what each member fears, and who is at risk.

Week 4 — Motivational interviewing I

The spirit (Miller & Rollnick): partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation. You are not the expert on what they should do; you are the expert on a conversation that helps them say it themselves.

The four core skills (OARS).

SkillWhat it isExample
Open questionInvites elaboration"What is a typical evening like?"
AffirmationNames a strength, specifically"You kept going to work through all this."
ReflectionReturns content or feeling"You are worn out by the effort it takes to keep this hidden."
SummaryCollects and connects"So on one hand it helps you sleep; on the other it is costing you your marriage."

What to avoid. The righting reflex (the urge to fix), confronting, warning, advising without permission, and arguing. When you argue for change, the client argues against it — and you have made change less likely.

Reflection is the active ingredient. Aim for two reflections for every question, and for complex reflections (which add meaning) over simple ones (which repeat content).

— OARS drill: 8 minutes, no advice, no questions after the first one. Observer counts reflections, and rates how many were complex.

Week 5 — Motivational interviewing II

Change talk — client statements in favour of change. Four types: desire ("I want to"), ability ("I could"), reasons ("because…"), need ("I have to"). Also mobilising talk: commitment ("I will"), activation ("I am ready"), taking steps ("I bought the medication").

Discord is not resistance; it is a signal about the relationship. It appears as arguing, interrupting, discounting, ignoring. The response is not confrontation but rolling with it: "You are not sure this is worth it. Tell me more about that."

The MI-consistent techniques.

  • Elicit–provide–elicit: "What do you already know about what alcohol does to the liver?" → brief, neutral information → "What does that mean for you?"
  • The decisional balance: "What are the good things about using? And the less good things?" (Ask in that order, always.)
  • Looking forward: "If nothing changes, where do you imagine you will be in five years?"
  • Readiness ruler: "On 0–10, how important is it to change? How confident are you that you could? What would move you from 4 to 6?"

Avoid the readiness trap: a score of 3 is an argument for a smaller intervention, not for discharge.

ASSESSED — 25-minute MI session with a trained role-player. Rated on: spirit, OARS fidelity, ratio of reflections to questions, and handling of discord.

The family's stage often differs from the user's. A wife in action married to a husband in pre-contemplation becomes the "enforcer", which damages the relationship and does not work. Stage-matched intervention means working separately where necessary.

Week 7 — Relapse prevention

The model (Marlatt & Gordon). Relapse is a process, not an event: high-risk situations → poor coping → lapse → the abstinence violation effect ("I've blown it, so I may as well continue") → relapse.

The plan.

  1. High-risk situations — people, places, times, feelings (loneliness, anger, celebration, payday).
  2. Coping strategies for each.
  3. A lapse protocol — what to do in the first 24 hours, and whom to call.
  4. Lifestyle balance — the "shoulds" versus the "wants"; boredom and resentment are relapse risks.
  5. The family's role — agreed, non-punitive responses to a lapse, decided in advance.

With the family. A relapse response agreed in advance prevents the cycle of crisis, shame and secrecy from restarting. Write it down: "If I use again, we will…"

Week 8 — Behavioural couples therapy for substance use

The evidence. Behavioural couples therapy (BCT) for alcohol and drug problems has good evidence, particularly where the partner is non-using and the relationship is not violent.

The "daily recovery plan" (O'Farrell). Each day: a shared trust discussion in which the abstinent partner states their intention and the other expresses support, then a specified rewarding activity together. It is behavioural, daily, and builds the relationship as a recovery resource.

Why couples work helps. Partners are often the strongest motivator and the most reliable monitoring system — but only if the relationship becomes a source of reward rather than a site of surveillance.

Contraindications. Current violence, coercive control, a partner with active untreated addiction, or a relationship the client is being coerced to remain in.

Week 9 — Working with relatives: CRAFT

CRAFT (Community Reinforcement and Family Training) works with the concerned significant other to change the contingencies around the user's behaviour, without confrontation. It has better evidence for treatment entry than traditional "interventions" or Al-Anon-style support alone.

Three goals. 1) Improve the relative's own wellbeing; 2) reduce the user's substance use; 3) increase the chance the user enters treatment.

Core skills taught to the relative.

  • Functional analysis: what triggers use, what consequences follow, what the relative does.
  • Positive communication: brief, specific, warm.
  • Allow natural consequences: stop removing consequences where it is safe.
  • Reward non-use: notice and reinforce sober days.
  • Domestic safety: never ask a relative to accept violence.
  • Self-care and social support: the relative's life must not be entirely about the user.
  • Invitation to treatment: a script, rehearsed.
— CRAFT role-play: a mother of a 24-year-old cannabis user. Teach positive communication and one natural consequence. Assessor rates warmth, specificity and safety.

Week 10 — Enabling, co-dependency and boundaries

"Co-dependency" is a contested term. Used carefully it describes patterns of over-functioning and self-neglect in relationships with an addicted person. Used carelessly it blames the relative — usually a woman — for the addiction.

Better language: accommodation and boundary. Ask what the relative does, what it costs them, and what they would like to do differently.

Boundary setting that works. Specific, behavioural, enforceable, and about the relative's own actions: "I will not lend money. If you come home intoxicated, I will sleep in the other room and we will talk in the morning." Not: "You must stop drinking or I will leave." (An unenforceable ultimatum teaches the family that threats are empty.)

Week 11 — Dual diagnosis

The overlap is the rule. Anxiety, depression, PTSD, ADHD and psychosis commonly co-occur with substance use. Direction of causality varies; the treatment must be integrated rather than sequential.

Principles.

  1. Treat both, at the same time, by the same team where possible.
  2. Do not require abstinence before treating mental illness, and do not require remission of mental illness before treating addiction.
  3. Be cautious with medication (benzodiazepines, stimulants) and coordinate with the prescriber.
  4. Distinguish substance-induced symptoms from independent disorders (timing and persistence).
  5. Trauma is common; assume it until assessed.
PLAN (35%)** — A family case with dual diagnosis: assessment, formulation, integrated plan, coordination with medical and recovery services, risk plan, and outcome measures.

Week 12 — Medication-assisted treatment

Opioid agonist therapy (methadone, buprenorphine) reduces mortality and improves retention; it is evidence-based treatment, not "substituting one drug for another". Acamprosate and naltrexone reduce alcohol relapse; disulfiram works only with supervision. Withdrawal management alone is not treatment.

The family therapist's role. Support adherence, address stigma within the family, challenge the belief that medication is cheating, and coordinate with the prescriber. Do not advise on doses or discontinuation.

Week 13 — Recovery as a family process

Recovery capital (Cloud & Granfield): social (relationships, support), physical (health, housing), human (skills, education, health literacy), cultural (values, belonging). Treatment that builds recovery capital predicts better outcomes than treatment that focuses only on symptoms.

The family's long haul. Early recovery is often called "the pink cloud"; the harder work is at six to eighteen months, when the family must renegotiate roles that were organised around the addiction. A partner who has run everything may not want to hand back control; a child who has been the carer may not want to be a child. Name this, and plan for it.

Relapse is data. The question is not "why did you fail?" but "what happened, and what does the plan need?"

Week 14 — Culture, faith, stigma and community

  • Substance use in context: alcohol is culturally embedded in many Nigerian settings (burial rites, festivals, business); kola, cannabis, tramadol and codeine have specific local patterns.
  • Faith: deliverance and prayer can be powerful resources and can also delay medical care. Coordinate, do not compete.
  • Stigma: "a disgrace to the family" keeps use hidden and prevents early help. Your language matters: "person with a substance use disorder", not "addict".
  • Community resources: mutual aid (AA/NA and their local equivalents), faith-based recovery groups, community health workers — map what actually exists near your clinic.

Week 15 — Integration

What works: screening and brief intervention; MI; CBT-based relapse prevention; BCT and CRAFT; medication where indicated; integrated treatment for dual diagnosis; recovery support; and continuity over time. What does not: confrontation, "tough love" as a single strategy, sequential treatment for dual diagnosis, and asking a family to do the work alone.

Glossary

Abstinence violation effect — the spiral of guilt and continued use after a lapse. CRAFT — Community Reinforcement and Family Training, for concerned significant others. Change talk — client statements favouring change. Dual diagnosis — co-occurring substance use and mental disorder. Enabling — behaviours that remove the natural consequences of use. Harm reduction — interventions that reduce harm without requiring abstinence. Motivational interviewing — a collaborative, directive method for strengthening intrinsic motivation. Recovery capital — the resources a person can draw on to sustain recovery. Relapse — return to problematic use after a period of change.
