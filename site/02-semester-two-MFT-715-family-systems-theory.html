Course leader's summary. This is the theoretical anchor of the programme. Where MFT 702 surveys schools, this course interrogates the theory beneath them: cybernetics, general systems thinking, structure, communication, constructionism and their critics. You will finish able to explain precisely what you mean when you say a family is "a system".

How to use this book

The word "system" is used loosely in clinical settings. This course tightens it. Each week takes one theoretical tradition, states its claims precisely, applies it to a case, and then asks what it cannot explain. You are expected to leave with arguments, not slogans.

1. Course description

Family systems theory is not one theory but a family of theories sharing a commitment: the unit of analysis is the relationship, and the whole is more than the sum of its parts. The course traces that commitment from von Bertalanffy's general systems theory and the Macy conferences on cybernetics, through the Palo Alto communication school, Minuchin's structuralism, Bowen's multigenerational theory, and the Milan group, to social constructionism, critical and feminist theory, and contemporary complexity and network perspectives.

Week 1 — The systemic turn

What changed. Before the systemic turn, the family was understood as the environment of an individual patient. After it, the family became the unit of treatment, and individual symptoms became functions of relationship patterns.

What it answered. Three problems the individual model struggled with: Why does a symptom appear in a particular child? Why does the family resist change (homeostasis)? Why does the same individual behave differently in different relationships?

What it risked. Dissolving the person into the system; treating "the family" as a homogeneous actor; ignoring power, gender, class and history; and producing descriptions that could not be falsified.

— Take one case. Write it twice: once as an individual formulation (as a CBT or psychodynamic clinician would) and once as a systemic one. Then write a paragraph on what each version makes visible and invisible.

Week 2 — General systems theory

Von Bertalanffy's propositions, translated for clinicians:

PropositionClinical meaning
A system is a set of elements in interactionThe family is defined by its interactions, not its members
The whole is more than the sum of its partsThe family has properties no member has alone
Systems are open or closed to their environmentA family that refuses outside contact becomes closed and rigid
Systems have boundariesWho is inside, who is outside, and how permeable
Systems are hierarchicalSubsystems nested within larger systems
EquifinalityDifferent starting points can reach the same end state
EquipotentialitySimilar starting points can reach different end states
Negative entropySystems import energy to counter disorder (information, support)

Equifinality and equipotentiality are the most useful and most neglected. Equifinality explains why two very different families produce the same symptom; equipotentiality explains why two similar families produce very different outcomes. Together they demolish simple causal stories.

Example. Two children are referred for school refusal: one in a family with high conflict and one in a family with high achievement pressure. Equifinality explains the identical presentation; equipotentiality explains why one family recovers in four sessions and the other takes two years.

Week 3 — Cybernetics I

Feedback. A system regulates itself by monitoring its own output and adjusting its input.

  • Negative feedback corrects deviation: the thermostat, the bedtime routine, the family that de-escalates when voices rise.
  • Positive feedback amplifies deviation: the microphone screech, the escalation, the panic spiral.

Homeostasis. The system's tendency to maintain its organisation. Morphostasis maintains; morphogenesis changes. A family can be stably unhappy: the symptom is one of the mechanisms that holds the system together.

The clinical insight. Attempts to change a system are themselves inputs. If the family's equilibrium depends on the symptom, your intervention will produce a counter-move. Predicting the counter-move — and sometimes prescribing it — is the strategic tradition's contribution.

Calibration example. A family's daughter has "tantrums" that end a 45-minute argument between her parents. The tantrum is negative feedback for the marital system (it restores calm) and positive feedback for the child (it works). Two feedback systems, one event.

— Map both feedback loops in a disguised case: one for each "layer" (the couple system and the child's behaviour). Show how the same event serves both.

Week 4 — Cybernetics II: first and second order

First-order cybernetics. The observer is outside the system; interventions are inputs; the aim is control of the system's output. This produced the heroic era: directive, strategic, clever.

Second-order cybernetics. The observer is part of the observed system; distinctions are drawn by an observer; knowledge is provisional. This produced curiosity, the reflecting team, and the therapist's accountability for their own effect.

The clinical consequences.

First orderSecond order
"What intervention will fix this family?""What am I noticing, and what am I blind to?"
The family is the object of changeThe family and the therapist co-construct change
Neutrality is possiblePosition is unavoidable; transparency is required
Expertise is the therapist'sExpertise is distributed

Reflexivity is not self-indulgence. It is the discipline of accounting for your own contribution to the clinical picture: which family member you find easier to like, which story you believed first, which data you stopped collecting once you had a hypothesis.

Week 5 — Communication theory

The five axioms (Watzlawick, Beavin & Jackson).

  1. One cannot not communicate.
  2. Every message has a content and a relationship level.
  3. Communication is punctuated into sequences by participants.
  4. Messages are digital (words) and analogical (analogue).
  5. Relationships are symmetrical or complementary.

Double bind hypothesis (Bateson): a person receives contradictory messages at different levels, cannot comment on the contradiction, and cannot leave the relationship. The hypothesis was weakened as an explanation of schizophrenia, but its clinical descendant — recognising contradictory, un-commentable communications in families — remains useful.

Critique. Communication theory could become mechanistic (families as machines exchanging messages) and could pathologise families of people with psychosis. It also tended to describe "systems" without the power differences that determine who gets to define what is communicated.

— Transcript analysis: take ten lines of a couple's argument and identify punctuation, relationship-level messages, and symmetrical/complementary moves.

Week 6 — Structural theory

Core propositions (Minuchin).

  1. The family's structure is the invisible set of rules that govern transactions.
  2. The structure organises the family into subsystems (spousal, parental, sibling) separated by boundaries.
  3. Symptoms are best understood as the family's response to its structure, and sometimes as the only way the structure can remain stable.
  4. Change in structure produces change in experience.

The evaluation criteria for a structure. Boundaries must be clear (not diffuse, not rigid); hierarchy must be appropriate to stage and culture; and subsystems must have enough autonomy to function and enough connection to belong.

Power and structure. Structural theory was criticised for assuming a normative hierarchy (executive parents, subordinate children) and for not asking how that hierarchy is upheld. Contemporary structural practice inserts the questions: whose authority is legitimate in this family, and who bears the cost of it?

Week 7 — Multigenerational theory

Core propositions (Bowen).

  1. The family is an emotional unit; anxiety is its most important variable.
  2. Differentiation of self is the capacity to remain connected while thinking independently.
  3. Anxiety is managed in one of four ways: conflict, distance, projection to a child, or dysfunction in one member.
  4. Triangles distribute anxiety and stabilise dyads.
  5. Patterns are transmitted across generations; symptomatic children are often the most triangulated.

The theory's strength is time: it offers an account of why now, by locating the present pattern in several generations of adaptation to war, migration, loss and religion.

The theory's weakness is its insufficient account of power, material constraint and culture. A woman cannot always "differentiate" out of an arranged marriage; a family cannot always "stay out of triangles" when migration has severed the network.

Week 8 — Milan and meaning

Core propositions (Palazzoli, Boscolo, Cecchin, Prata).

  1. The family is a system of meanings (premises) that organise behaviour.
  2. The therapist forms hypotheses and tests them with circular questions.
  3. Neutrality is a stance of curiosity towards all members, not indifference.
  4. Positive connotation frames all behaviour as serving the system's coherence.
  5. Interventions disturb the system by changing premises, not by demanding behaviour change.

The evolution. Classical Milan used teams, rituals and directives; later Milan (and the reflecting team) abandoned directive intervention and emphasised multiple perspectives and the therapist's own openness.

Critique. Neutrality became indefensible in cases of violence and abuse; circular questioning can become an elegant evasion; and the intellectual register excluded many families.

Week 9 — Constructivism and social constructionism

Constructivism (Maturana, von Glasersfeld): each person constructs a model of reality from their own experience; we cannot access an objective reality directly. Clinically: take each family member's account seriously, and stop trying to establish "the truth".

Social constructionism (Gergen, Shotter): meanings are produced between people, in language and in culture, and change with context. Clinically: the problem, the roles and the identities are made in talk, and so they can be made differently.

The distinction matters. Constructivism risks relativism and individualism (everything is "just your view"); social constructionism locates meaning in relationships and power, which makes it compatible with the critical and cultural traditions.

Implication for practice. The therapist's formulation is one description among several. Offering it humbly — "here is a story I am beginning to see; how does it look to you?" — is both a constructionist technique and a mark of respect.

Week 10 — Narrative and postmodern theory

Narrative (White & Epston). Stories organise experience; dominant stories make alternatives invisible; the task is to externalise the problem and thicken a preferred story.

Postmodern influences. Foucault on discourse and power-knowledge; Derrida on deconstruction. The clinical translation: question the "official" account, ask who benefits from it, and look for what it silences.

The reflexivity of narrative therapy is unusual: the therapist is "decentred but influential", and the client is the author. This is an ethical stance as much as a technique.

Critique. The linguistic emphasis can under-serve families with limited literacy or in acute crisis; the evidence base, while growing, is smaller than the cognitive-behavioural tradition's.

Week 11 — Critical, feminist and anti-racist systems theory

The feminist critique (Hare-Mustin 1978; Goldner; Walters) made four claims that changed the field:

  1. "Circular" descriptions conceal power asymmetries.
  2. "Neutrality" in the face of violence colludes with the perpetrator.
  3. The gendered division of labour, not "the family system", is often the object of the wife's complaint.
  4. Models that prize individuation encode a male norm.

Anti-racist and postcolonial theory extend the critique: whose family structures are treated as healthy? Which knowledge counts as evidence? How do services reproduce the state's relationship to marginalised families?

Clinical translation.

  • Ask about power explicitly, in every assessment.
  • Never use systemic language to make a victim responsible for the violence she receives.
  • Place race, migration, class and religion inside the system, not outside it.

Week 12 — Attachment and neuroscience in systems theory

Two imports that have reshaped the field.

  1. Attachment theory gives systemic thinking a developmental mechanism: the couple and parent–child systems are organised by attachment strategies, and those strategies are formed in relationships.
  2. Neuroscience gives systemic thinking a physiology: co-regulation, the window of tolerance, mirroring and stress physiology explain how relationships change bodies.

The integration. "Systems" are not abstract patterns; they are embodied patterns between nervous systems. A couple's pursue–withdraw cycle is simultaneously a communication pattern, an attachment strategy, and a sequence of autonomic states.

Critique of the imports. Neuroscience is often oversold in clinical texts; "mirror neurons" and "the right brain" are frequently invoked without evidence. Use physiology where it is well established (stress, regulation, sleep, trauma) and keep the metaphors labelled as metaphors.

The mature position. Systems theory is a disciplinary sensibility, not a complete theory. It tells you where to look (between people, across generations, in context) and what to resist (single villains and simple causes). It does not, by itself, tell you what to do.

Week 14 — Complexity, networks and the contemporary field

Complexity theory. Families are non-linear, self-organising systems: small differences can produce large divergence; cause and effect are not proportional; and feedback loops are the rule. This fits clinical experience better than linear models, but it resists prediction.

Network thinking. Contemporary research treats families as networks of relationships, with density, centrality and clustering measurable. Who is central? Who is isolated? Who is the only link between two clusters? This is a formalised genogram, and it is improving our understanding of family structure and resilience.

Ecological models. Bronfenbrenner's nested systems (Figure 7 in MFT 701) place the family inside institutions, culture and time — a corrective to the field's tendency to treat the family as self-contained.

Week 15 — Your theoretical position

Write 1,000 words and defend it in ten minutes:

  1. What is the unit of analysis you will work with, and why?
  2. What do you believe about causation in families?
  3. What is the therapist's position, and how will you manage your own power?
  4. Which systemic concept do you find least defensible, and what do you use in its place?
  5. What would make you abandon your position?

Glossary

Autopoiesis — the self-producing organisation of living systems. Boundary — the rules governing participation and exchange between systems. Circularity — reciprocal causation within a system. Cybernetics — the study of control and communication in systems. Differentiation of self — see Bowen. Equifinality — different paths leading to the same end state. Equipotentiality — similar starting points leading to different end states. Homeostasis / morphostasis — the maintenance of system organisation. Morphogenesis — change in system organisation. Punctuation — the marking of a sequence's beginning; the source of blame. Reflexivity — the therapist's accountability for their own participation. Second-order cybernetics — cybernetics of observing systems. Structure — the repetitive patterns of transaction that organise a family.

Short answer

  1. Define equifinality and equipotentiality and give one clinical example of each.
  2. Distinguish negative and positive feedback with a family example.
  3. What are the three criteria for evaluating a family's structure?
  4. State the feminist critique of "circular" formulations in four sentences.
  5. What is reflexivity, and why is it a clinical skill?

Essay (choose one, 2,000 words)

  1. "Systems theory tells you where to look but not what to do." Discuss.
  2. Critically evaluate the concept of homeostasis in families where violence is present.
  3. Compare Bowen's and Minuchin's accounts of how problems develop, and assess which is better supported.
