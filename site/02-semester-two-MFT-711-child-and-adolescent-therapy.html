MFT 711 — Child and Adolescent Therapy

Course leader's summary. Children rarely refer themselves and rarely benefit from an adult conversation. This course teaches you to work with children as agents in a family system: how to speak to them, play with them, assess developmentally, and intervene through the adults who live with them.

How to use this book

Each week has a developmental frame (what is normal at this age), a clinical frame (what is different when it goes wrong), and a practice frame (what you actually do in the room). The play and activity protocols are written so you can use them next week, not after you qualify.

1. Course description

Child and adolescent work in family therapy is not a specialism bolted on to adult work; it is a distinct skill set. Children communicate through play, behaviour and the body; adolescents communicate through testing, silence and peers; and both live inside systems that can either support or undermine change.

The course covers: developmental assessment, engaging children and adolescents, family-based interventions, common presentations (anxiety, behaviour problems, school refusal, trauma, self-harm, eating difficulties), divorce and blended families, safeguarding, and the interface with schools and paediatrics.

Week 1 — Children in context

The paradox of child therapy. A child is referred because of behaviour. The behaviour occurs in a context. If you treat the child alone, you have removed them from the context that maintains the behaviour and asked them to change it on their own. Family-based work is therefore not an ideological preference; it is a practical inference.

Children as agents. Children are not passive recipients of family dynamics. They notice, interpret, protect, sacrifice and resist. A nine-year-old who refuses school may be keeping a depressed mother company; a thirteen-year-old who fights may be absorbing a father's rage to protect a younger sibling.

The evidence. Family-based treatments have strong evidence for: conduct problems (parent management training, functional family therapy, multisystemic therapy), adolescent substance misuse, eating disorders (family-based treatment for anorexia nervosa is first-line), anxiety in children (parent-involved CBT), and psychosis relapse prevention (family intervention).

— Observe a child (with consent) for 15 minutes in free play. Record only behaviour: what they did, in what order, what they returned to. No interpretation for the first ten minutes.

Week 2 — Developmental assessment and red flags

The four-category check (from MFT 703): normal variation, delay, regression, symptom.

Red flags requiring referral, not therapy alone.

Assessment with children. Use several modes: interview, drawing, play observation, parent report, teacher report, and standardised measures (SDQ, RCADS). Children's self-report and parents' report diverge systematically — collect both, report both.

Week 3 — Engaging children

Rules of engagement.

  1. Explain the frame to the child, not just the adult. "We will meet four times. We will talk and sometimes draw. You can say 'pass' to any question."
  2. Get on their level. Sit on the floor, use their name, match their pace.
  3. Use a medium. Drawing, puppets, sand, figures, or a simple game. The medium is not a distraction; it is the language.
  4. Do not interrogate. One question, then follow. "Tell me about this house" beats "who lives here?"
  5. Be honest. Children detect adult pretence and are damaged by it. If you cannot answer, say so.

Three reliable techniques.

  • The three wishes. "If you had three wishes, what would they be?" (One is usually for the child, one for the family, one impossible.)
  • Draw your family. Then ask: "Who is this? What are they doing? Who is closest to whom?" Note who is omitted, who is drawn largest, who is at the edge.
  • The magic wand / miracle. "If you woke up and the problem was gone, what would be different at breakfast?"

Avoid interpretation. "That must be your father" is a projection. Ask instead: "Tell me about this person."

— 20-minute drawing interview with a peer playing an 8-year-old. Use the three techniques. Debrief: how many questions did you ask, and what did you learn without asking?

Week 4 — Engaging adolescents

What adolescents need from a therapist. Honesty, respect for autonomy, a genuine alliance with them (not with their parents), and confidentiality that is real except where safety requires otherwise.

The first five minutes. Speak to the adolescent first. Ask their version before the parents'. Explain confidentiality in their language: "What you tell me stays here, unless I think you or someone else is in danger. If that happens, I will tell you before I tell anyone else."

Techniques that work.

  • Third-person questions. "What do teenagers your age usually say about this?" Lowers the exposure.
  • Scaling. "On 0–10, how much does this bother you? How much does it bother your mum?"
  • Future questioning. "What do you want your life to look like in two years? What would have to change first?"
  • Silence tolerance. Do not fill every pause.
  • Negotiated goals. "What would make this worth your time?"

What fails. Lectures, moralising, siding with parents, pretending to be young, and asking "why".

Week 5 — Consent, confidentiality and divided loyalty

Consent. For minors, a parent or guardian consents; the young person assents. Both matter. Record both. Where a competent adolescent seeks help without parental knowledge, follow your jurisdiction's law and your agency policy — and do not improvise.

Confidentiality with children.

SituationApproach
Child discloses something minorKeep it; help them tell the parent if they want
Child discloses self-harmAssess risk; agree a plan; inform carers with the child's knowledge
Child discloses abuseFollow safeguarding procedure; tell the child what will happen next
Parent asks what the child saidGive themes, not transcripts; protect the alliance
Parent demands a report for courtRefer; a treating therapist should not provide forensic opinion

Divided loyalty. The most common clinical trap: the child tells you something that would hurt the parent, or the parent asks you to "fix" the child's attitude. Name the dynamic and refuse to be recruited: "I am not going to be the one who tells your mother. I will help you do it."

Week 6 — Anxiety and school refusal

Anxiety in children. Developmentally normal fears (separation, darkness, monsters, social evaluation) become disorders when they are persistent, impairing and out of proportion.

The family-based approach.

  1. Psychoeducation. Anxiety is a false alarm; avoidance reduces anxiety now and increases it later.
  2. Map the accommodation. What does the family do to prevent the child's distress? (Answering questions for them, sleeping in their bed, allowing school avoidance, checking repeatedly.) Accommodation is usually maintained by love, and naming it must be done without blame.
  3. Graded exposure. Together, build a ladder from easiest to hardest; start at the bottom; reward approach, not the absence of anxiety.
  4. Parental coaching. Parents become coaches, not rescuers: "I know this is hard. I will be here at 3 o'clock."
  5. The school plan. One named adult, a quiet space, a gradual return timetable, and no negotiation at the school gate.

School refusal — a differential. Anxiety, separation difficulty, bullying, learning difficulty, family crisis or a bid to protect a parent. The treatment differs for each. Assess by asking what happens on Sunday night, and what the child's absence makes possible at home.

— Build an exposure ladder with a role-played parent for a 10-year-old with separation anxiety. Ten rungs, specific, graded, with a reward and a review date.

Week 7 — Behaviour problems and parenting

The evidence base. Parent management training (PMT) and its variants have the strongest evidence for child conduct problems. The mechanism is consistent: increase positive attention, give clear instructions, use consistent consequences, reduce harsh discipline, and rebuild the parent–child relationship.

The core skills (teach, model, rehearse, review).

SkillWhat it looks like
Special time10 minutes daily of child-led play, no correction
PraiseSpecific, immediate, frequent ("thank you for putting your shoes away")
InstructionsGet attention, get close, one instruction, allow 5 seconds, praise compliance
Planned ignoringFor minor attention-seeking; not for dangerous behaviour
ConsequencesConsistent, proportionate, predictable, delivered calmly
RoutinePredictable structure reduces conflict

Why parenting programmes fail. Too much content per session; the parent is depressed or exhausted; the child has an untreated condition; another adult undermines the plan; or the family's material circumstances make consistency impossible. Address these before adding more technique.

Adaptation. In households where several adults care for the child (grandmother, aunt, house help), bring them all in and agree one plan. A plan implemented differently by four adults is not a plan.

Week 8 — Observed child assessment

Thirty minutes with a child (role-played by a trained peer/actor), then twenty minutes of feedback to the carers. Assessed on: engagement, developmental appropriateness, information gathering without interrogation, risk screen, and the quality of the feedback.

Week 9 — Trauma and maltreatment

Principles (developed in MFT 714). Safety first; stabilise before processing; the caregiver's own regulation is the child's best medicine; and never force a narrative.

What trauma looks like in children.

AgePresentation
0–2Feeding and sleep disruption, startled response, withdrawal
3–6Regression, repetitive play, nightmares, clinging
7–11Aggression, school problems, somatic complaints, guilt
12–18Risk-taking, self-harm, dissociation, substance use, precocious sexuality

Family work after maltreatment. The non-offending caregiver's belief and protection is the single strongest predictor of recovery. Work with them first. Where the perpetrator remains in the home, safety planning precedes everything.

Safeguarding. Know your reporting route, document verbatim disclosures without leading questions, and never promise a child secrecy about abuse.

Week 10 — Self-harm and suicidality in adolescents

Ask directly. "Sometimes when people feel this bad, they think about hurting themselves or ending their life. Has that happened for you?" Direct questions do not plant ideas.

Assess: frequency, method, function (relief, punishment, communication, control), intent, plan, means, protective factors, and the family's response.

Manage the family. Parents often respond with panic, punishment or secrecy. Help them respond with calm supervision and connection: remove means, increase monitoring without surveillance, and seek help immediately if risk rises.

Confidentiality. A young person's request for secrecy must yield to safety. Tell them what you will disclose and why, and involve them in how.

Week 11 — Divorce, separation and blended families

Telling the children. Both parents together, a simple message with no blame, permission to love both parents, and reassurance that the adults' decision is not the child's fault. No details about the affair, the money or the courts.

What harms children. Exposure to conflict (more than separation itself), being used as a messenger, being asked to choose, hearing a parent denigrated, and instability.

What protects them. A cooperative co-parenting relationship, structure, a relationship with both parents, and adults who can manage their own distress.

Blended families. Build the couple bond first; stepparents take authority gradually and through the biological parent; children need permission to love both households; and the "insider/outsider" split must be named and worked with.

Week 12 — Eating difficulties

Family-based treatment for anorexia nervosa (FBT, "Maudsley") is a first-line intervention for adolescents. It is agnostic about cause and externalises the illness: the family is recruited to defeat the eating disorder, not blamed for it.

Three phases. 1) Parents take charge of refeeding; 2) control is returned to the adolescent, age-appropriately; 3) identity and developmental issues are addressed.

Medical safety. Eating disorders carry the highest mortality of any mental disorder. Weight, vital signs and bloods are medical matters; do not work in isolation.

Avoid. Explaining the illness as a consequence of family dynamics; power struggles over food in session; and colluding with the idea that the young person can simply "decide to eat".

Week 13 — Schools and paediatric services

The school as a system. Teachers see the child in a different context and hold different data. Ask for specifics, not impressions: attendance, incidents, academic trajectory, peer relationships, and what strategies have been tried.

Writing a useful report for a school. One page: what you observed, what it means, three concrete recommendations, and who to contact. Avoid diagnosis-led recommendations the school cannot implement.

Paediatric liaison. Distinguish functional from organic symptoms without dismissing either. A referral that names what you want excluded is more likely to be answered (see MFT 707).

REPORT — Write a one-page school communication for a child with school refusal, including a graded return plan with named adults.

Week 14 — Neurodivergence in family context

Reframe. Autism and ADHD are not behavioural choices and not caused by parenting. But the environment determines how disabling they are.

Practical family work.

  • Psychoeducation and demystification for siblings and grandparents.
  • Environmental adaptation: predictability, visual schedules, sensory accommodations, transition warnings.
  • Parental coaching: reduce the demand–avoidance cycle; increase specific praise; build in recovery time.
  • Couple work: neurodivergence in a partner or child strains the parental alliance; address it explicitly.
  • School liaison: accommodations are usually more effective than consequences.

Avoid. Behavioural contracts for skills the child does not yet have; blaming parents; and treating a diagnosis as an explanation for everything.

Week 15 — Endings with children

Children end differently. Use a session to review: what we did, what changed, what you will remember, and what you will do when you need help again. Use the medium you used (drawing, play) to close the loop. Give the child something concrete (a certificate, a drawing, a letter) and make sure the carers know when and how to return.

Glossary

Accommodation (of anxiety) — family behaviours that reduce a child's distress in the short term and maintain the anxiety. Assent — a minor's agreement to treatment, alongside guardian consent. Externalising (the illness) — separating the child from the disorder, as in FBT. Family-based treatment (FBT) — a manualised family approach to adolescent anorexia nervosa. Parent management training (PMT) — behavioural skills training for parents of children with conduct problems. School refusal — difficulty attending school owing to emotional distress, distinct from truancy. Separation anxiety — developmentally excessive distress about separation from attachment figures.
