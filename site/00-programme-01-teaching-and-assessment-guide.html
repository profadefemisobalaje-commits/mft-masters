For the person delivering the course. Short, practical, and designed so a new lecturer can pick up a book and teach it next week.

1. The default session shape (2 hours + 1 hour practical)

TimeSegmentPurpose
0–10 minCheck-in roundOne sentence each: "one thing that stayed with you from last week". Builds the relational field you will later teach about.
10–25 minRetrieval quizFive closed questions on last week's material, answered individually then in pairs. Retrieval, not recognition.
25–60 minLecture block 1One big idea, one case, one diagram. Never more than one new diagram per block.
60–70 minBreakNon-negotiable; protects attention for block 2.
70–100 minLecture block 2The model applied to the same case; show a recorded or live demonstration.
100–120 minWhole-group debrief"What would you have done differently?" Collect on the board; do not resolve everything.
Practical hourSkills triadObserver / therapist / client, 12-minute rounds, structured feedback sheet, rotate.

The observer is not passive. Give the observer a single narrow task per round — count the closed questions, note every time the therapist interrupted, or track who spoke most. Narrow observation is what makes feedback usable.

2. Assessment design principles

  1. Assess the skill, not the vocabulary. A student who can define "enactment" and cannot conduct one should not pass the clinical component.
  2. Use unseen cases for exams and seen cases for assignments. Seen cases teach; unseen cases test.
  3. Mark formulations on reasoning, not on agreement with your formulation. The question is "is this hypothesis supported by the evidence in the case and testable?", not "is it the one I would have had?"
  4. Require risk documentation in every clinical assignment. A formulation with no risk screen is incomplete, however elegant.

Sample marking rubric for a case formulation (30%)

CriterionWeightWhat excellence looks like
Systemic description20%Circular, multi-person, includes context, no single-villain language
Evidence use20%Claims traced to specific case detail; gaps named
Formulation quality25%5-P grid coherent; one clear focus; explicitly testable
Model application15%Technique justified by formulation, not by preference
Ethics, culture and risk10%Risk screen, power, culture and consent addressed
Writing and referencing10%Plain professional prose; accurate APA-style referencing

3. Teaching the difficult topics

Suicide and self-harm (MFT 712). Do not lecture about risk assessment without demonstrating one. Use a scripted role-play with a colleague, then let students practise in triads with a checklist. Expect anxiety; name it and use it.

Intimate partner violence (MFT 704, 710, 712). Never role-play a live violent couple in a whole-group format without a clear stop signal and a pre-brief. Screen separately, always. Teach the difference between situational couple violence and coercive control early, because the treatment implications diverge sharply.

Culture (MFT 708). Avoid the "cultural knowledge" trap. Teach students to ask, not to assume, and to locate themselves on the same dimensions they are assessing.

Trauma (MFT 714). Students with their own trauma histories will be activated by this material. Announce content in advance, offer an opt-out route that is not stigmatising, and keep a referral pathway for students as well as clients.

4. Running the skills labs

  • Groups of three, fixed for the semester. Continuity produces trust; changing groups each week produces polite performances.
  • Twelve-minute rounds, three-minute feedback, rotate. Protect the time strictly; over-running is the most common facilitation error.
  • Feedback sandwich is banned. Use: "what I saw", "what it did to me as the client", "one thing to keep, one thing to try".
  • Record one round per student per month (with consent) so they can see themselves rather than argue with feedback.
  • The lecturer circulates and joins rounds as a co-therapist, which is more powerful modelling than any demonstration.

6. Materials, ethics and logistics

  • Recorded sessions: obtain written consent from every adult participant and a guardian for minors, explain who will view the recording, and store it encrypted with a deletion date.
  • Live supervision: brief the family in advance that a supervisor may call in; agree the signal the therapist can use to pause.
  • Role-play disclosure: state at the start of the semester that personal material shared in skills practice is confidential within the group but that students may always decline to use their own experience.
  • Assessment security: rotate unseen cases each year and keep the case bank in one place.

8. If you only have three hours with this book

Teach Week 1 (framing), the week containing the central model, and the integration chapter. Set the practicals as self-directed triad work with a submitted log. That preserves the spine of the course: what the work is, how it is done, and how it fits together.

9. Printing and distributing the materials

Every course is available as a finished A4 PDF in ../pdf/; the whole programme is in MFT-Library-Complete-28-volumes.pdf. Practical notes for running a semester from the print edition:

  • Set the reading from the page numbers, not the chapter titles. Each PDF's contents page carries real page numbers, and the bookmarks in the sidebar match them, so "read pages 6–9 before Thursday" is unambiguous.
  • Print the week, not the book. The weekly sections are self-contained: the idea, the practice, a case and a reflective task. A 14-week course prints to roughly 25 pages of core text.
  • Photocopy the practicals. Callout panels (case, practical, caution, debate, reflection) are set in tinted boxes precisely so they can be lifted onto a worksheet without re-typing.
