MFT 719 — Family Life Cycle and Transitions

MSc Marriage and Family Therapy · Semester Three · 1.71 Units · 15 teaching weeks · Prerequisite: MFT 703, 715

Course leader's summary. Most families come to therapy during a transition they did not choose, or one they chose but did not anticipate. This course teaches you to locate the symptom in time: at which stage is this family, which transition is it facing, and what must be renegotiated.

How to use this book

The life-cycle framework is a diagnostic instrument, not a moral timetable. Each week applies it to a different stage and a different kind of transition, including the ones that do not fit the classic sequence — migration, polygyny, foster care, sudden death, disability. Culture is not an exception to the model; it is the material from which the timetable is made.

1. Course description

The course covers the Carter–McGoldrick stage model; the distinction between predictable (normative) and unpredictable (non-normative) transitions; horizontal and vertical stressors; the family's need to renegotiate roles, rules and boundaries at each transition; rituals; intergenerational legacies; and the transitions particular to contemporary African and diasporic family life.

Week 1 — Why transitions produce symptoms

The thesis. Symptoms appear at points of transition because the family's existing organisation is no longer adequate to the new demands, and the renegotiation has not yet happened.

Three mechanisms.

  1. Role vacuum. A role disappears (the working mother, the eldest child at home) and no one has been assigned the new one.
  2. Boundary mismatch. A boundary that suited the previous stage is now too rigid or too diffuse (parents who cannot let an adolescent have privacy; an adolescent who has become the mother's confidante).
  3. Unfinished business. The previous transition was never completed (a father who never really left home; a family that never mourned a stillbirth), so the new one cannot be navigated.

Clinical question. "What changed in the last 6–18 months?" almost always produces the precipitant.

— Draw a timeline of your family of origin marking moves, births, deaths, marriages, migrations, and job changes. Mark where symptoms or conflicts appeared. Note the pattern.

Week 2 — The six stages

StageEmotional taskSecond-order change required
1. Leaving homeAccept emotional and financial responsibilityDifferentiate from family of origin; develop intimate peer relationships
2. JoiningCommit to a new systemForm a couple; realign relationships with extended families
3. Young childrenAccept new membersAdjust the couple system; co-parent; realign with grandparents
4. AdolescentsIncrease flexibilityShift parent–child boundaries; refocus on midlife marriage and careers
5. LaunchingAccept many entries and exitsRenegotiate the marriage as a dyad; include in-laws and grandchildren
6. Later lifeAccept shifting rolesDeal with loss, disability, and the next generation's centrality

Two warnings. The model is descriptive of a particular Western, middle-class trajectory. And the stages overlap: in many families, launching and caregiving occur simultaneously, children return home, and grandparents raise grandchildren.

Week 3 — Normative and non-normative transitions

Normative transitions are expected: marriage, first child, school entry, adolescence, launching, retirement, death of elderly parents.

Non-normative transitions are unexpected or off-time: divorce, a child's disability, a death of a young parent, migration, redundancy, imprisonment, a pandemic, infertility, a child born outside marriage.

Why the distinction matters. Normative transitions have cultural scripts — rituals, advice, role models. Non-normative transitions usually do not, which leaves families improvising under stress, often with shame.

Clinical implication. For non-normative transitions, part of the work is script-making: helping the family construct a way to do something no one they know has done.

— Take a non-normative transition (e.g. a child's diagnosis). List the scripts that exist, the scripts that do not, and what the family has had to invent.

Week 4 — Horizontal and vertical stressors

Horizontal stressors — events moving through time: life-cycle transitions, illness, accidents, migration, poverty, discrimination.

Vertical stressors — legacies handed down: family myths, expectations, secrets, taboos, ethnic and religious beliefs, scripts about gender and success.

The clinical insight. A family can usually manage horizontal stressors or vertical ones; the crisis comes when both arrive at once.

Example: A family facing redundancy (horizontal) in which the father's family has a myth that "a man who cannot provide is worthless" (vertical). The financial problem is manageable; the collision is not.

Week 5 — Leaving home and joining

Leaving home. The task is differentiation, not departure. A young adult may live at home and be differentiated, or live abroad and be fused. The clinical markers: can they disagree with a parent without catastrophe? Can they ask for help without regression?

Joining. The couple must build a "we" while remaining connected to two families of origin. The most common failure is a boundary problem: a spouse who remains more loyal to a parent than to the partner.

Assessment questions.

  • "When you disagree with your mother, what happens?"
  • "Who did you consult first about this decision?"
  • "How often do you see each family? Who decided that?"
  • "Whose opinion matters most in this marriage?"

Week 6 — Young children

The reorganisation. Time, sleep, money, division of labour, sexuality, identity, and the relationship with grandparents all change at once. Research consistently shows a dip in relationship satisfaction after the first child, and that the quality of the co-parenting alliance predicts child outcomes more strongly than conflict.

The clinical presentations.

  1. The couple disappears into parents. No couple time, no sexuality, no conversation that is not about the child.
  2. The grandmother becomes a third parent. A cross-generational coalition forms; the father becomes peripheral.
  3. The child becomes the regulator. A toddler's behaviour organises the household's emotional life.

Intervention. Protect the couple subsystem (time, privacy, an agreement about the division of labour); clarify the parental subsystem (a united front, agreed rules); and negotiate explicitly with grandparents about their role.

Week 7 — Adolescents

The family task is to increase flexibility: more autonomy with more accountability; less control and more influence; boundaries that permit privacy without permitting secrecy.

Two failure patterns.

PatternWhat it looks likeConsequence
EscalationParents tighten as the adolescent pushesConflict, running away, secrecy, risk
AbdicationParents withdraw to avoid conflictUnsupervised risk, no structure, anxiety

The middle path is negotiation with limits: clear non-negotiables (safety), negotiable domains (time, phone, clothes), and consequences agreed in advance.

Also at this stage: the parents' own midlife renegotiation, which often appears as an "adolescent problem" in the referral.

Week 8 — Launching and the empty nest

The task is to renegotiate the marriage as a dyad, and to accept new members and exits (partners, grandchildren, returns home).

The common presentations.

  • The couple who have nothing left to say. They organised everything around the children; now there is silence.
  • The parent who cannot launch. A mother who sabotages the child's independence because her role depends on being needed.
  • The child who will not leave. Economic dependency, anxiety, or a parent's covert invitation.

Intervention. Rebuild the couple relationship deliberately; address the parent's identity and loss; and set a graduated plan for independence that neither humiliates the young adult nor abandons them.

Week 9 — Later life

The tasks. Accept generational role changes; deal with loss (parents, siblings, spouse, friends); support the next generation without taking over; prepare for disability and death; and make meaning of a life.

Clinical presentations. Caregiving disputes among siblings; a widowed parent's new relationship; the "responsible child" who carries everything; inheritance conflict; a grandparent raising grandchildren.

Intervention. Make the loyalty ledger explicit (who has given what, and what is owed); negotiate caregiving tasks concretely; and address the unspoken grief that usually sits under the conflict.

The dual-process frame for grief (Figure 22 in MFT 712) helps here: restoration-oriented activity (tasks, roles, legal arrangements) is not avoidance; it is part of grieving.

Week 10 — Migration and displacement

Migration is a family transition with all the features of a life-cycle stage and none of the rituals.

PhaseFamily taskCommon difficulty
DecisionWho goes, who stays, who paysCoercion, debt, secrecy
SeparationMaintain attachment across distanceChildren left with relatives; marital drift
ArrivalEstablish a householdStatus loss, housing, language
ReunificationRe-form as a familyChildren who do not know the parent; new authority conflicts
SettlementIntegrate without dissolving identitySecond-generation conflict; "you are not like us"

The "astronaut" and "parachute" patterns. A parent working abroad while the family remains; a child sent abroad for education. Both produce high achievement and high loneliness, and often a "satellite child" who is expected to justify the family's sacrifice.

Clinical questions. Who decided? Who was left? What was lost (status, language, community, identity)? What does the family owe the ones who stayed?

Week 11 — Observed transition plan session

Forty minutes with a role-played family at a transition (launching, caregiving, migration reunification). Assessed on: accurate location of the transition; renegotiation of roles and boundaries; inclusion of all members; and a concrete, reviewable plan.

Week 12 — Divorce, remarriage and blended families

Divorce as a transition, not an ending. The spousal relationship ends; the parental relationship continues in a different form. The family's task is to reorganise across two households while protecting the children from conflict.

The stages. Decision → telling the children → practical reorganisation → legal process → emotional adjustment → new equilibrium (often 2–4 years).

Remarriage and stepfamilies. A new structure with different tasks:

  • Build the couple bond before the stepparent assumes authority.
  • Negotiate household rules across two systems.
  • Allow children to love both households without disloyalty.
  • Expect 4–7 years to integrate, not months.
  • Address the "insider/outsider" split explicitly.

Clinical error. Treating the stepfamily as a nuclear family that started late, and applying a nuclear family timetable.

Week 13 — Births, deaths and unfinished transitions

Unfinished transitions are those that were never marked, mourned or renegotiated: a stillbirth nobody mentions, a migration that was supposed to be temporary, a divorce that was never explained to the children, a death the family does not discuss.

Signs. Anniversary reactions, a name that cannot be spoken, a child named after the dead, a room kept unchanged, a recurring argument with no present cause.

Intervention. Name the unfinished transition gently; help the family construct a way to mark it; and allow the grief that the avoidance was managing.

Week 14 — Rituals

Rituals consolidate transitions. They mark the boundary between the old state and the new, assign new roles, and connect the family to its wider culture.

Types. Cultural and religious rites (naming, marriage, burial); family rituals (Sunday meals, holidays, anniversaries); and therapeutic rituals (a letter, a ceremony, a shared object, a family meeting).

Designing a therapeutic ritual.

  1. Purpose. What transition needs marking?
  2. Participants. Who must be present, and who must be absent?
  3. Symbol. What object, place, word or action carries the meaning?
  4. Structure. Beginning, middle, end.
  5. Follow-up. How will the change be maintained?

Caution. Respect the family's religious and cultural boundaries; do not invent rites that displace their own; and never use a ritual to bypass a necessary conversation.

— Design a ritual for a family that has never marked a bereavement. Present it in five minutes.

Week 15 — Integration: the family in time

The life-cycle lens gives you three questions that belong in every assessment:

  1. Where is this family in time? (Stage, and the transition in progress.)
  2. What must be renegotiated? (Roles, boundaries, loyalties, rules.)
  3. What has not been finished? (Losses, transitions, legacies.)

Ask them in the first session and you will rarely be short of a focus.

Glossary

Horizontal stressors — events and transitions moving through time. Vertical stressors — legacies, myths and expectations transmitted across generations. Normative transition — an expected, culturally scripted life event. Non-normative transition — an unexpected or off-time event. Second-order change — change in the system's organisation, not only in its behaviour. Unfinished transition — a transition that was never marked or renegotiated. Satellite child — a child sent abroad or away for education, carrying the family's hopes.

Short answer

  1. Distinguish normative from non-normative transitions with two examples each.
  2. Explain horizontal and vertical stressors with a case.
  3. What must be renegotiated when a first child is born?
  4. Name four features of migration as a family transition.
  5. Give five elements of a therapeutic ritual.

Essay (choose one, 2,000 words)

  1. "The life cycle is a Western timetable." Critically evaluate and adapt the model for Nigerian families.
  2. Analyse a family's presenting problem as a transition problem, and show what changed in your treatment plan.
  3. Discuss the role of rituals in consolidating therapeutic change.
