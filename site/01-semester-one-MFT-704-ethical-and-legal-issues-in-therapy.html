Course leader's summary. This course trains judgement, not just compliance. You will learn the codes, the law, and — more importantly — how to reason when the codes and the law point in different directions, when the family's culture conflicts with the institution's policy, and when your own values are in the room.

How to use this book

Every week presents a standard, a case, and a decision. Ethics is not memorised; it is practised. The weekly incidents in this book are drawn from the ordinary texture of clinical life — the secret, the subpoena, the gift, the neighbour, the unpaid fee — because those are the situations that actually end careers.

1. Course description

Family therapy multiplies ethical complexity. In individual work there are two parties (you and the client). In family work there may be six, with conflicting interests, unequal power, and secrets that each person believes belongs to them. Add a school, a child protection agency, a court, an insurance company, and a faith community, and the question "who is my client?" becomes the central professional question of the course.

The course combines normative ethics (codes, duties, principles), law (the parts a clinician must know and the parts a clinician must refer), and professional formation (the kind of clinician you are becoming).

Week 1 — Moral philosophy for clinicians

You do not need to become a philosopher, but you need to know which argument you are making when you say "that would be wrong".

TraditionCentral questionTherapy example
Deontology (duty)What are my duties regardless of outcome?Keeping confidentiality even when disclosure would be convenient
ConsequentialismWhat produces the best overall outcome?Breaking confidentiality to prevent a serious harm
Virtue ethicsWhat would a person of good character do?Refusing a gift that would create obligation
Ethics of careWhat does this relationship require of me?Attending to the family's dependence on you
Justice / rightsAre power and resources fairly distributed?Whose voice is missing from this room?

The four principles (Beauchamp & Childress) remain the most portable framework: autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice. In family work they collide constantly — respect for the father's autonomy may conflict with the mother's safety.

— Take three ethical dilemmas from your own life (not clinical). For each, identify which tradition you used, and which you used to justify it afterwards.

Week 2 — The codes and informed consent

What codes do and do not do. Codes set a floor, not a ceiling. They are written to be enforceable, which means they are often silent on the hardest questions. They also vary by jurisdiction and by professional body; know which one governs you.

The core obligations across the major codes:

  1. Competence and its limits
  2. Informed consent (including consent for recording and for supervision)
  3. Confidentiality and its limits
  4. Multiple relationships
  5. Sexual intimacy with clients and former clients (prohibited; time limits vary)
  6. Fees and financial arrangements
  7. Advertising and representation
  8. Research ethics
  9. Cultural competence and non-discrimination
  10. Reporting obligations and legal compliance

Informed consent in family therapy is a document and a conversation. It should cover: who the client is; who is seen and in what combination; the confidentiality policy including secrets; recording and supervision; fees and cancellation; the limits of confidentiality (risk, abuse, court order); and the complaint route. Review it aloud in the first session, in the family's language, and record that you did.

— Draft a one-page informed-consent document for couple therapy. Exchange with a peer and identify the three sentences most likely to be misunderstood by a family with limited literacy. Rewrite them.

Week 3 — Confidentiality in the multiperson system

There is no single confidentiality in family work; there is a policy you negotiate.

Three workable models.

ModelRuleAdvantagesRisks
No-secretsAnything told individually may be shared with the coupleSimple, transparentIndividual may withhold important material
Full secrecyIndividual disclosures are kept privateProtects individual therapyCreates a therapeutic secret that distorts the couple work
Selective (negotiated)The therapist and client agree in advance what will be sharedFlexible and realisticRequires skill and documentation

Most experienced couple therapists operate a modified no-secrets policy: they will not keep a secret that materially affects the couple's work, and they tell each partner this before the first individual session. This protects both the relationship and the therapist.

Documentation. Record the policy in the file, in the family's words if possible, and note the date it was explained. If a secret arises, record what you did and why.

— During an individual session, a wife discloses that she has been having an affair for two years and has no intention of ending it or telling her husband. No one is at physical risk. Under a no-secrets policy, you tell her at the outset of the individual session that this cannot stay private if the couple work is to continue; you then help her decide how and when to disclose, with a deadline. Under a selective policy, you would negotiate what can be shared. What you must not do is carry the secret silently for months while conducting "couple therapy".

Week 4 — Secrets I: the individual disclosure

Classify before you respond. Three questions: Is anyone at risk? Does this information change the treatment I am providing? Is the client able to disclose it themselves, with support?

Type of secretTypical response
Harmless (a surprise party)Keep it; it is not clinically material
Clinically material, no risk (an affair; a debt; a diagnosis)Negotiate disclosure with a deadline; do not collude
Risk to a third party (HIV status undisclosed; threats)Assess; follow legal and public-health obligations; document
Risk to a child or vulnerable adultReport; inform; protect
Risk to selfRisk assessment; safety planning; break confidentiality as required

The clinical skill: refusing to be recruited. "I understand you are not ready to tell him. I cannot keep this and also work on your marriage with you, because I would be working with half the truth. Let us plan how you will tell him, and when."

The systemic reality. Secrets are not only information; they are structure. A secret held by one partner and the therapist creates a coalition — and the excluded partner often senses it, which damages the therapy's credibility.

Week 5 — Secrets II: infidelity, paternity, HIV status

Three cases that appear in every couple therapist's career, each with a different legal overlay.

Infidelity. Not a reportable act. The clinical tasks: stop the bleeding, contain the disclosure, assess for sexually transmitted infection risk, address the meaning of the affair (not only its fact), and decide with the couple whether disclosure to a third party (the affair partner's spouse) is required. That last question is a values question with safety implications; consult.

Paternity. A man may discover that a child he is raising is not biologically his. The therapist must not conceal information that affects a child's welfare, must not give legal advice, and must help the family decide who needs to know and in what sequence. Refer for legal advice on paternity, maintenance and custody; the emotional work is yours.

HIV and other communicable conditions. Confidentiality is strong, but public-health law may create duties where a partner is at identifiable risk and the client refuses disclosure. Know your jurisdiction's rule, consult, and document. Never disclose without advice.

DEBATE — "A therapist who keeps an affair secret for six months is colluding in the harm the affair causes." Argue for and against, then write your own policy in 200 words.

Week 6 — Boundaries and multiple relationships

Definition. A multiple relationship exists when you have another significant relationship with a client or someone closely associated with them. Not all are unethical; all require examination.

The test. (1) Could the second relationship impair your objectivity? (2) Could it exploit the power difference? (3) Would the client experience it as exploitative later? (4) Is it avoidable?

Common in small communities.

SituationRiskDefensible approach
Seeing a neighbour's familyOverhearing information; social contactDiscuss openly; agree how to behave at community events
Client is a fellow church memberRole confusion; spiritual authorityClarify roles; consider referral if you hold a leadership role
A relative of your child's classmateDual loyaltyDisclose in supervision; transfer if the risk is unmanageable
Client asks you to be a character witnessRole change; advocacyDecline; explain; refer to a professional who can assess
Gift-giving after a bereavementCultural obligation vs boundaryAccept small cultural gifts; document; discuss meaning
Trading therapy for servicesExploitation riskUsually decline; if unavoidable, document value and rationale

Touch, self-disclosure, and home visits. Each is sometimes therapeutic and always requires a rationale you can articulate in supervision. The test is never "did it feel right?" but "whose need did it serve?"

— Complete a boundary audit of your present life: community roles, family ties, business interests, social media. Identify the three situations most likely to produce a dual relationship in the next two years, and pre-decide your response.

Week 7 — Competence and referral

Competence has four dimensions: knowledge, skills, experience, and appropriateness (personal and cultural fit). You can be knowledgeable and inappropriate: a therapist with unprocessed trauma working intensively with trauma, or a young male therapist seeing a couple in a culture where his authority is contested.

The duty to refer arises when: the problem is outside your training; you lack supervision for it; your personal reactions would compromise care; or the client needs a different modality or medical input.

How to refer well. Name the reason without shaming ("this needs someone with specific training in X"); make a warm handover (a call, not just a name); offer a bridge session if needed; follow up once; and document. A referral is not abandonment.

SELF-AUDIT — List the ten presenting problems you are most likely to see. For each, rate your competence 1–5 and name the supervisor or service you would refer to. The two lowest ratings are your professional development plan for this year.

Week 8 — Risk I: suicide and self-harm

The standard. Ask directly, ask specifically, ask about means, and document. There is no evidence that asking about suicide increases risk, and substantial evidence that clinicians fail to ask.

A defensible risk assessment includes:

  1. Ideation — frequency, intensity, duration, controllability.
  2. Plan — specific method, place, time.
  3. Intent — "Do you intend to act on this?"
  4. Means — access to firearms, medication, pesticides, heights.
  5. Previous attempts — the strongest single predictor.
  6. Protective factors — reasons for living, responsibility to children, faith, support.
  7. Substance use and impulsivity.
  8. Family history.
  9. Current stressors and recent losses.
  10. Disclosure and support network — who can be told, who can watch.

Documentation. Record the questions you asked and the answers, your formulation of risk (low/moderate/ high), the plan, who was informed, and the review date. If you decide not to break confidentiality, record why.

In family work. A parent's suicidality, an adolescent's self-harm and a child's disclosure must be handled without turning the family session into an interrogation. Structure: see the person individually first, agree what will be shared, then facilitate the sharing.

(OBSERVED)** — Fifteen-minute suicide risk assessment role-play with a trained actor. You must ask all ten elements, formulate risk, and agree a safety plan. Pass/fail.

Week 9 — Risk II: intimate partner violence and child protection

Distinguish two patterns.

Situational couple violenceCoercive control
Mutual, low-level, escalates under stressOne partner systematically dominates
Fear is situationalFear is chronic; surveillance, isolation, threats
Couple therapy may be appropriate after assessmentCouple therapy is contraindicated; risk increases
Safety planning individualSpecialist advocacy and protection

Screening. Screen each partner separately, at least once, using direct questions: "Has your partner ever hit, kicked, or pushed you? Has he/she ever made you afraid? Do you feel safe going home today?" Never screen in front of the partner.

Child protection. Know your jurisdiction's reporting duty. In Nigeria, the Child Rights Act (2003, adopted in many but not all states) and state child protection laws create duties and structures; the practical route is usually the state ministry responsible for social welfare, the police family unit, or a designated safeguarding lead. Document the concern, the advice received, and the action taken.

— Conjoint sessions can escalate violence after the session. If there is any indication of coercive control, separate sessions and a safety plan precede any conjoint work. Write it down.

Week 10 — The legal framework a clinician must know

This section is orientation, not legal advice. Statutes and state practice vary; verify with a lawyer.

Marriage in Nigeria exists in four overlapping forms: statutory (Marriage Act), customary, Islamic, and cohabitation without legal marriage. A family may be married under more than one system.

Dissolution. Statutory divorce requires a court petition (Matrimonial Causes Act, with the two-year rule and recognised grounds); customary and Islamic dissolutions follow their own processes and courts. The therapist's role is not to advise on grounds but to help the family clarify what they are deciding and to refer.

Children. Custody and access decisions are governed by the welfare principle — the child's best interests. Maintenance obligations exist under both statutory and customary law. The therapist may be asked for a report; a treating therapist should generally not conduct a forensic custody evaluation of their own client (role conflict).

Other relevant areas: the Child Rights Act; the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act 2015 (applicable in the FCT and in states that have adopted it); mental health legislation in the states that have adopted the Mental Health Act 2021 framework; data protection obligations regarding client records.

Week 11 — Records, subpoenas and court

Write notes for three readers: your future self, a colleague taking over, and a court. Use factual, behavioural language; avoid speculation and avoid character judgements. Record: date, attendees, risk screen, interventions, plan, and who was told what.

If a subpoena or court order arrives: do not ignore it; do not respond immediately without advice; verify its validity; contact your supervisor, indemnity provider or lawyer; inform the client (unless prohibited); release only what is required, with a privilege log if advised.

Testifying. Your role is to report what you observed, not to advocate for a preferred outcome. Answer the question asked; do not volunteer; do not guess; say "I don't know" when you do not. Distinguish fact, clinical opinion and hypothesis.

— Note-writing lab. Write a progress note for a session in which a risk was disclosed and a report to a school was agreed. Exchange; grade each other against the "three readers" standard.

Week 12 — Money, gifts and the business of therapy

Fees are clinical material. They carry meaning about worth, dependency, entitlement and shame. Have a written policy; state it at the start; apply it consistently; discuss non-payment rather than avoiding it.

Refusing to reduce a fee sometimes ends therapy; never reducing a fee sometimes ends therapy. Both are true. Decide your sliding scale in advance and document the reason for each exception.

Gifts. Small cultural gifts may be accepted with documentation; expensive gifts should be discussed; gifts with strings should be declined. Ask, "What would it mean to you if I accepted / declined?"

Week 13 — Ethics of research, assessment and diagnosis

  • Informed consent for research must be voluntary, capacity-appropriate and revocable; families, children and people in crisis require special care.
  • Deception requires justification, debriefing, and ethics approval.
  • Diagnosis carries consequences: access to care, stigma, insurance, custody. Diagnose what you can defend and describe what you cannot.
  • Assessment instruments must be validated for the population and language; using an unvalidated instrument and acting on it is an ethical problem, not just a methodological one.

Week 14 — Integration: the decision model and your stance

The seven steps. Identify the dilemma → check codes and law → consult → generate at least three options including the uncomfortable one → weigh consequences for each affected party → decide, act and document → evaluate afterwards.

The documentation habit. A defensible decision is not the same as a correct decision; it is one you can explain. Write the reasoning at the time, not after a complaint.

Your stance. Write 500 words: what you will never do; what you will always consult about; and where your personal values are most likely to distort your clinical judgement.

Glossary

Autonomy — the client's right to self-determination. Beneficence / non-maleficence — the duty to benefit and the duty not to harm. Coercive control — a pattern of domination, isolation and threat, distinct from situational violence. Confidentiality — the duty not to disclose information learned in a professional relationship. Dual relationship — a second significant relationship with a client or someone close to them. Duty to warn / protect — a legal obligation, in some jurisdictions, to act on a serious threat. Informed consent — voluntary agreement based on adequate information and capacity. Mandated reporting — a legal duty to report suspected abuse or neglect. Privilege — the client's legal right to prevent disclosure in court proceedings. Subpoena — a legal order requiring documents or testimony. Welfare principle — the rule that a child's best interests govern decisions about them.

Short answer

  1. State a confidentiality policy for couple therapy in six sentences a family could understand.
  2. List the ten elements of a defensible suicide risk assessment.
  3. Distinguish situational couple violence from coercive control, and state the treatment implication.
  4. What should you do when you receive a subpoena? Five steps.
  5. Why is "I know it is unethical but it was the right thing to do" an inadequate defence?

Essay (choose one, 2,000 words)

  1. "The most difficult ethical problems in family therapy are not conflicts between right and wrong but between two rights." Discuss with three cases.
  2. A father discloses that his 14-year-old daughter is self-harming and asks you not to tell her mother. Analyse the ethical, legal and clinical dimensions and set out your plan.
  3. Critically examine the claim that the professional codes are culturally biased in favour of Western individualism.
