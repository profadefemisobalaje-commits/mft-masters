MFT 721 — Research Methodology

MSc Marriage and Family Therapy · Semester Three · 1.71 Units · 14 teaching weeks · Prerequisite: MFT 705

Course leader's summary. This is the advanced methods course that turns a proposal into a thesis. You will work with real data, choose and justify analytic strategies, defend your design against examiners, and learn to write findings that practitioners will actually read.

How to use this book

Every week's task contributes directly to your thesis. Bring your own dataset, or use the teaching dataset supplied with the course. Do not read statistics passively; run them, break them, and interpret them badly at least once.

1. Course description

The course covers advanced design (longitudinal, dyadic, single-case, participatory), analysis (regression, multilevel modelling, dyadic models, thematic and framework analysis, mixed-methods integration), measurement and psychometric evaluation, systematic reviewing, data management and reproducibility, writing for publication, and defence of a study.

Week 1 — Protocol and pre-registration

The protocol is the study's constitution: question, design, sample, measures, analysis plan, ethics, and timeline. Write it before you collect data; revise it with dated amendments if you must change course.

Pre-registration (e.g. on OSF) commits you publicly to a plan, which reduces the risk of unconscious analytical flexibility ("p-hacking"). It is standard in trials and increasingly expected elsewhere.

Honest limits. Exploratory qualitative work and community-based participatory research are often incompatible with rigid pre-registration. Declare your approach and be transparent about decisions made during the study.

A live analysis log — one page per week, dated — is the single most useful document in a thesis. It records what you did, what you decided, and why.

— Draft a two-page protocol for your thesis. Include one paragraph on what you will do if recruitment is half of what you expect.

Week 2 — Design for relational questions

Four designs especially suited to family questions.

DesignQuestionStrengthWeakness
Longitudinal panelDoes X predict change in Y?Temporal orderingAttrition, cost
Dyadic (APIM)How do partners affect each other?Separates actor and partner effectsRequires both partners
Single-case experimental (SCED)Does this intervention work for this family?Clinical realism, causal inference within caseGeneralisation
Participatory action researchWhat would improve this community's experience?Validity, ethics, impactTime, power negotiation

The design decision log. For each choice, record: the alternative considered, the reason for rejection, and the threat it leaves unresolved. Examiners reward this; it shows you know what your design cannot do.

Week 3 — Sampling, power and feasibility

Power. Do the calculation before you collect (GPower, R's pwr, or simulation). For dyadic and multilevel designs, power depends on the number of clusters* (couples, families), not the number of individuals.

Practical realities in clinical settings.

  • Referral rates: how many families does the clinic actually see per week?
  • Eligibility: what proportion will meet your criteria?
  • Consent: what proportion will agree? (Often 30–60% in clinical samples.)
  • Attrition: assume 20–30% at follow-up.
  • Seasonality: school terms, holidays, religious calendars.

The feasibility sentence. "To recruit 60 couples in six months, I need 4 eligible referrals per week with a 60% consent rate; the clinic receives 9 per week; two colleagues have agreed to refer."

Week 4 — Data preparation

The unglamorous work that determines everything.

  1. Codebook — every variable, its label, its values, its missingness convention.
  2. Raw data frozen — never edit the original file after data entry.
  3. Cleaning log — every decision (a value of 99 recoded to missing; one duplicate removed).
  4. Missing data — describe the pattern; distinguish missing at random from missing not at random; consider multiple imputation rather than listwise deletion.
  5. Reverse scoring — check; a mis-scored subscale invalidates the results.
  6. Outliers — inspect, do not automatically delete.
— Produce a codebook and cleaning log for your dataset (or the teaching dataset).

Week 5 — Regression

What regression does. Estimates the association between predictors and an outcome, adjusting for other predictors, and expresses it in the outcome's units.

Assumptions to check. Linearity, independence of errors, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals (for inference), and no severe multicollinearity. Report the checks; do not just assert them.

Reading output. Coefficient (direction, size, units), standard error, confidence interval, p-value, R², and model fit. Report effect sizes with confidence intervals, not only p-values.

Clinical translation. "Each additional point on the criticism scale was associated with a 0.3-point lower satisfaction score (95% CI −0.5 to −0.1), holding depression constant." That sentence is worth more than a table of asterisks.

Week 6 — Multilevel models

The problem. Individuals nested in couples, couples nested in therapists, repeated measures nested in people. Standard regression assumes independence and will produce confidence intervals that are too narrow.

The multilevel solution. Model the nesting explicitly:

  • Fixed effects — the average relationship across the sample.
  • Random effects — how intercepts (and slopes) vary across clusters.
  • ICC — the proportion of variance attributable to the cluster. A high ICC means the clustering matters greatly and cannot be ignored.

Model-building sequence. Null model (ICC) → add level-1 predictors → add level-2 predictors → test random slopes → compare with likelihood ratio tests or information criteria (AIC/BIC).

Report. Always report the ICC, the number of clusters, and the estimation method.

Week 7 — Dyadic models

APIM in four numbers.

  • Actor effect (a): my predictor on my outcome.
  • Partner effect (p): my predictor on my partner's outcome.
  • Distinguishability: whether partners are distinguishable (e.g. by gender or role) and whether effects can be constrained equal.
  • K: the correlation between partners' residuals, which models the non-independence.

Interpretation example. In a study of 80 couples, criticism predicted lower satisfaction for the person giving it (actor effect) more strongly than for the person receiving it (partner effect). Clinical implication: the critic is also being harmed, which is a more useful reframe than "stop criticising your partner".

Software. R (lme4, nlme, ape), Mplus, SPSS MIXED.

Week 8 — Qualitative analysis

Two workable approaches.

ApproachLogicOutput
Reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke)Themes constructed from coding, with the analyst's role explicitRich thematic account
Framework analysis (Ritchie & Spencer)Matrix of cases × themes, often for applied policy questionsStructured matrix, comparable across cases

Rigour in practice.

  • Keep a coding audit trail: initial codes, revisions, discarded codes and why.
  • Use analytic memos after each transcript.
  • Second-coder check on at least 20% of data with a documented discussion of divergence.
  • Member reflection where appropriate (not to validate themes, but to deepen them).
  • Reflexive journal on your own reactions and assumptions.

The common error is presenting themes as "findings that emerged" from data, as though the analyst were not present. Themes are constructed; say so.

Week 9 — Psychometrics

A psychometric evaluation of a measure in your population.

  1. Internal consistency — Cronbach's α (and ω, which is more appropriate for congeneric items).
  2. Factor structure — exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis; report fit indices (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR).
  3. Construct validity — correlations with related and unrelated measures.
  4. Measurement invariance — configural, metric, scalar, across groups (gender, language, age).
  5. Responsiveness — change scores in a treated sample.

A local validation study is a perfectly good thesis, and often more useful to the field than another outcome study using an unvalidated instrument.

REPORT (20%) — A 1,500-word psychometric evaluation of one instrument, with recommendations.

Week 10 — Systematic review basics

Steps. Register the protocol (PROSPERO) → define the question (PICO) → search systematically (at least three databases; record the strategy) → screen (two reviewers; report agreement) → extract data → assess risk of bias (e.g. Cochrane RoB 2, or CASP for qualitative) → synthesise (narrative or meta-analytic) → assess certainty (GRADE).

Meta-analysis, briefly. Pool effect sizes with a random-effects model unless there is a justification for fixed effects; report heterogeneity (I², τ²); explore it with subgroup analysis or meta-regression if there are enough studies; assess publication bias with a funnel plot and Egger's test, understanding their limitations.

What examiners look for. A search strategy that could be reproduced; a bias assessment that is not perfunctory; and a conclusion proportionate to the evidence.

Week 11 — Single-case and small-N designs

Why they matter in family therapy. Clinical samples are small, families are heterogeneous, and randomised trials are expensive. Single-case designs give causal inference within a case.

Core designs.

DesignStructureInference
A–BBaseline, then interventionWeak (confounded by time)
A–B–ABaseline, intervention, withdrawalStronger if reversal occurs
Multiple baseline across behavioursStaggered introductionStrong; no withdrawal needed
Multiple baseline across participantsStaggered across familiesStrong; realistic in clinics

Analysis. Visual analysis (level, trend, variability) plus a quantitative index (e.g. Tau-U) and, ideally, systematic replication across cases.

Reporting standard. Follow the SCED reporting guidelines (e.g. SCRIBE), including baseline stability and treatment fidelity.

Week 12 — Mixed methods integration

The integration question. What does the combination tell you that neither strand could alone?

Joint display format.

Quantitative findingQualitative findingConvergence / divergenceInterpretation
Attendance predicted outcome (β = .32)Families described "being known" by the therapist as decisiveDivergence in mechanismAttendance may be a proxy for alliance
Drop-out 34%Families cited transport and work shiftsConvergenceStructural barriers, not motivation

Avoid the two common failures. Reporting two parallel studies with no integration; and using qualitative data merely to illustrate quantitative findings.

REPORT (20%) — A 2,000-word integration report with at least three joint display rows.

Week 13 — Robustness and pitfalls

Pitfalls you will meet.

PitfallWhat it looks likeFix
p-hackingMany tests, reporting the significant onesPre-specify; adjust; report all
HARKingHypothesis stated after results knownRegister the hypothesis
OverfittingModel too complex for the sampleSimpler model; cross-validation
Ignoring clusteringTreating partners as independentMultilevel or dyadic models
Selective reportingReporting one measure, ignoring anotherReport all pre-specified outcomes
Causal language"Caused" from cross-sectional dataUse associational language

Sensitivity analysis. Run your main analysis again with a different reasonable decision (e.g. excluding an outlier, using a different missing-data approach) and report whether conclusions change. If they do, say so.

Week 14 — Writing, publishing and defending

The findings chapter. Present, do not interpret (that is the discussion's job). Structure by question. Report descriptive statistics before inferential. Use tables for numbers and prose for meaning.

The discussion. What you found → how it compares with the literature → mechanisms → limitations (specific, not generic) → implications for practice and policy → future research.

Defending. Prepare for four questions: (1) Why this design? (2) What are the threats to validity and how did you address them? (3) Why these analyses? (4) What can and cannot be concluded? Rehearse short answers.

Publishing. Choose a journal that publishes practitioner research; follow the author guidelines exactly; expect a rejection and a revision; respond to reviewers point by point.

Glossary

APIM — Actor–Partner Interdependence Model. Cluster — a grouping unit (couple, family, therapist) whose members are not independent. GRADE — a system for rating the certainty of evidence. ICC — intraclass correlation; the share of variance due to clustering. Measurement invariance — equivalence of a measure's functioning across groups. Pre-registration — public commitment to a study plan before data collection. SCED — single-case experimental design. Sensitivity analysis — re-analysis under different assumptions to test robustness.
